## 1. Answer box

> **Quick answer:** To prepare for a gastroenterologist appointment, track your symptoms, meals and bowel movements (with Bristol stool type) for about 2 weeks; write down when symptoms started, how often they happen and what makes them better or worse; list every medicine and supplement; note family history of colon cancer, IBD or celiac disease; gather past test results; and bring your top 3 questions. Don't start a gluten-free diet before any celiac testing.

## 2. Intro

GI appointments are short, and waits for them can be long. Many people walk out realizing they forgot the one detail that mattered — or feeling they weren't taken seriously. A little preparation changes that. As one person in a patient forum put it, take your food diary, symptom diary and notes "and see the difference in how you are treated" [11].

This checklist covers what to track in the 2 weeks before, what to bring, what the doctor will likely ask and do, and the questions worth asking — plus a free one-page visit summary.

**In this guide:** [2-week tracking plan](#track) · [What to bring](#bring) · [What the doctor will ask](#ask) · [What to expect](#expect) · [Questions to ask](#questions) · [Free visit summary](#printable)

## 3. Key takeaways

- **Track for ~2 weeks**: symptoms with times and 0–10 severity, Bristol stool type, meals, and red flags.
- **Bring a one-page summary**, not a stack of raw notes.
- **List medicines and supplements**, including over-the-counter ones.
- **Know your family history** of colon cancer, IBD and celiac disease [1].
- **Don't change your diet** (e.g., going gluten-free) right before testing unless told to [2].

## 4. Before the appointment: the 2-week tracking plan {#track}

**H2:** What should you track before a GI appointment?

**Answer:** The same things your doctor will ask about: what your symptoms are, how often they come, when (for example after eating), how long you've had them, and how your bowel habits have changed [1][3]. Two weeks of daily logs turns those answers from guesses into facts.

**Checklist — log daily for ~14 days:**
- [ ] **Bowel movements:** time, Bristol type 1–7 ([chart](/resources/bristol-stool-chart)), urgency, straining, feeling of incomplete emptying, no-go days
- [ ] **Anything unusual in stool:** blood, black or tarry stool, mucus, pale color — and photos only if your clinic asks for them
- [ ] **Symptoms:** pain (where, what it feels like, 0–10), bloating, gas, nausea, reflux — with the time each started and how long it lasted ([how to describe abdominal pain](/blog/how-to-describe-abdominal-pain))
- [ ] **Relation to meals and bowel movements:** does pain ease or worsen after a BM? Start soon after eating? [3][4]
- [ ] **Meals and drinks** with times (a simple [food and symptom diary](/resources/food-symptom-diary) works)
- [ ] **Night-time symptoms:** anything that wakes you
- [ ] **Weight:** once a week, same scale
- [ ] **Context:** stress 0–10, sleep, period/cycle day, travel, illness, new medicines

**If your appointment is weeks away:** keep logging the whole time — see [waiting for a GI appointment? What to track](/blog/gastroenterologist-wait-time). If red-flag symptoms appear while you wait (§9), don't wait — contact your doctor or urgent care.

## 5. What to bring {#bring}

**H2:** What should you bring to a gastroenterologist appointment?

| Bring | Details |
|---|---|
| One-page visit summary | Printable below (§11) or your app's report |
| Your 2-week log | Paper diary or a PDF summary — offer it; don't expect the doctor to read every page |
| Symptom timeline | When it started; what's changed; anything you think set it off (infection, travel, new medicine, stressful event) [1] |
| Medicine & supplement list | Name, dose, how often — include over-the-counter, laxatives, antidiarrheals, antacids, iron, probiotics, herbal products [1] |
| Diet changes already tried | What, for how long, and what happened (e.g., low-FODMAP, dairy-free, more fiber) |
| Family history | Colon cancer, polyps, IBD, celiac disease, and the relative's age at diagnosis [1] |
| Past results | Blood tests, stool tests, imaging, endoscopy/colonoscopy reports, pathology — especially from other clinics |
| Referral letter, insurance card, ID | As required by the clinic |
| Your top 3 questions | Written down (§8) |
| A companion (optional) | Helpful if you're anxious or expect a lot of information |

**Do / don't before the visit**
- **Do** follow any prep instructions you're given (fasting, bowel prep, stopping certain medicines) if a procedure is booked the same day.
- **Don't** start a gluten-free diet before celiac testing — it can affect the results [2].
- **Don't** stop prescribed medicines without asking the prescriber.

## 6. What the doctor will ask {#ask}

**H2:** What questions will a gastroenterologist ask?

**Answer:** Expect questions about your symptoms, their pattern and duration, your medical and family history, your medicines, recent infections, stressful events and what you eat [1][3]. Having your log means you can answer with numbers.

| They'll likely ask | Answer from your log |
|---|---|
| What are your symptoms? Which bothers you most? | Your top symptom and its average 0–10 score |
| When did this start? Has it changed? | Start date; what changed and when |
| How often? Do they come and go? | "Pain on 6 of 14 days; worst 7/10" |
| Is pain related to bowel movements? | "Eases after a BM on most days" [4] |
| How often do you go, and what does it look like? | BMs per day/week; Bristol types — "mostly 6, sometimes 7" ([how to describe your poop](/blog/how-to-describe-poop-to-doctor)) |
| Any blood, black stool, weight loss, symptoms at night? | Yes/no with dates [1] |
| What medicines and supplements do you take? | Your list |
| Family history of colon cancer, IBD or celiac? | Your notes [1] |
| What have you tried? | Diet and medicine changes and results |

## 7. What to expect at the appointment {#expect}

**H2:** What happens at a first GI appointment?

**Answer:** Usually a detailed conversation about your symptoms and history, then a physical exam of your abdomen. Depending on what they find, the doctor may order tests — often blood and stool tests first, and sometimes procedures [1][3].

- **History:** the questions above.
- **Physical exam:** the doctor may check your abdomen for bloating, listen with a stethoscope and press for tenderness [1]; depending on symptoms, a rectal exam may be suggested [5].
- **Possible tests** (your doctor decides which, if any):
  - **Blood tests** — e.g., for anemia, infection or celiac disease [1][2]
  - **Stool tests** — for blood or infection; in people with suspected IBS and diarrhea, the ACG suggests fecal calprotectin to help rule out inflammatory bowel disease [1][6]
  - **Breath tests** — for small intestinal bacterial overgrowth or problems digesting carbohydrates such as lactose [1]
  - **Upper endoscopy or colonoscopy** — when symptoms, results or family history call for it [1]
- **IBS can be diagnosed without lots of tests.** The ACG recommends a "positive diagnostic strategy" based on symptoms rather than ruling out everything first, to start treatment sooner [6]. Normal test results don't mean symptoms are "all in your head" — they mean no dangerous cause was found [7].
- **Screening is separate.** US guidance recommends colorectal cancer screening for adults 45–75 regardless of symptoms [8].

## 8. Questions to ask {#questions}

**H2:** What should you ask your gastroenterologist?

Pick your top 3:
1. What do you think is most likely causing my symptoms — and what else are you ruling out?
2. Which tests do I need, what are they looking for, and how will I get results?
3. Which symptoms should make me call you or seek urgent care?
4. Should I change what I eat? Would a referral to a dietitian help?
5. What should I keep tracking before the next visit — and in what format do you want it?
6. If tests come back normal, what's the plan?
7. When should I follow up?

Full list by situation (first visit, IBS, follow-ups): [questions to ask your gastroenterologist](/blog/questions-to-ask-gastroenterologist).

## 9. Don't wait for the appointment if…

**H2:** Which symptoms mean you shouldn't wait for your appointment?

Contact your doctor promptly or seek urgent care if you have:
- black, tarry stools, or blood in your stool or from your bottom — especially a lot of blood or clots [5][9]
- severe or constant abdominal pain [9][10]
- vomiting, especially with constipation or inability to pass gas [10]
- signs of dehydration, high fever, or six or more loose stools a day [9]
- unexplained weight loss [1]

See: [changes in bowel habits that need a doctor](/blog/when-to-see-doctor-bowel-changes).

## 14. FAQ

**H2:** GI appointment FAQ

**What does a GI appointment consist of?**
Usually a detailed history (symptoms, timing, medicines, family history), a physical exam of your abdomen, and a plan that may include blood, stool or breath tests, or a procedure such as a colonoscopy if needed [1].

**Should I eat before my gastroenterologist appointment?**
For a consultation-only visit you can usually eat normally. If a procedure is booked, follow the clinic's fasting and prep instructions exactly. Don't change your diet (e.g., going gluten-free) before celiac testing [2].

**How long should I track symptoms before seeing a GI doctor?**
About 2 weeks of daily logs is a practical minimum; longer if symptoms are occasional. Don't delay seeing a doctor for red-flag symptoms (§9).

**What if my colonoscopy or tests come back normal?**
That's reassuring — it means no dangerous cause was found — but it doesn't mean your symptoms aren't real [7]. Ask what the working diagnosis is, what the treatment plan is, and what to track next.

**What does a fecal calprotectin test check for?**
It's a stool test that helps distinguish inflammation in the gut (as in inflammatory bowel disease) from IBS; the ACG suggests it for suspected IBS with diarrhea [6].

**How do I describe my poop to the doctor?**
Use the Bristol type number, how many times a day, urgency, and any blood, mucus or color change. See [how to describe your poop to a doctor](/blog/how-to-describe-poop-to-doctor).
