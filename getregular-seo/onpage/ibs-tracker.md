## 2. Why track IBS?

**H2:** Why keep an IBS log?

**Answer:** IBS is diagnosed and managed from patterns over time — how often pain happens, how it relates to bowel movements, and how stool form and frequency change [1][2]. Symptoms come and go, triggers vary from person to person, and a log is how you and your clinician see what's actually happening.

- IBS is a disorder of gut–brain interaction: recurring abdominal pain with changes in bowel movements, without visible damage to the gut [3].
- Flares can follow alcohol, caffeine, spicy or fatty food, stress or antibiotics — or nothing obvious [4]. Many women with IBS have more symptoms during their periods [5].
- The NHS recommends keeping a diary of what you eat and any symptoms [6].

## 6. Honest limits

**H2:** What the IBS tracker won't do

> - **It can't tell you whether you have IBS.** Only a clinician can diagnose IBS, usually from your symptom pattern, history and an exam, sometimes with blood or stool tests to rule out other conditions [1][9].
> - **Patterns aren't causes.** IBS has many drivers — food, stress, sleep, hormones, infections. Treat a pattern as a question for your care team.
> - **AI can be wrong** about photos and patterns. Edit meal ingredients when they're off; choose Bristol types manually if you prefer.
> - **It's not a FODMAP database or a diet plan.**
> - **It doesn't replace your GI, GP or dietitian.**

**Red-flag box:** **Don't just track — see a doctor promptly if you have:** blood in your stool, bleeding from your bottom or black, tarry stools · unexplained weight loss · low iron/anemia · a lump in your tummy · symptoms that started later in life or a family history of colon cancer, IBD or celiac disease · diarrhea that wakes you at night [1][4][10][11]. Having an IBS diagnosis doesn't mean new symptoms should be ignored.

## 9. FAQ

**H2:** IBS tracker FAQ

**What's the best way to track IBS symptoms?**
Log meals, symptoms (with start time and severity), bowel movements by Bristol type, and context like stress, sleep and your cycle — every day for at least 2–4 weeks. Paper works; an app saves time and does the counting. Our [IBS food diary guide](/resources/ibs-food-diary) shows both.

**Can I use it if I haven't been diagnosed?**
Yes. Many people start logging before a GI appointment. If you've had symptoms for more than 4 weeks, see a doctor [4] — and bring your log. See the [GI appointment checklist](/resources/gastroenterologist-appointment-prep).

**How long does an IBS flare last?**
It varies from hours to days, and from person to person. Logging start and end times shows you your own typical flare length. See [IBS flare-up tracking](/blog/ibs-flare-up-tracking).

**Does it tell me if I have IBS-C or IBS-D?**
No. It logs the stool types your doctor uses to describe subtype. IBS-D, for example, means more than a quarter of stools on abnormal days are loose or watery and less than a quarter are hard [3]. See [IBS-C vs IBS-D](/blog/ibs-c-vs-ibs-d).

**Can I track my period with my IBS symptoms?**
Yes — log period dates, and cycle data is included in the Doctor Report. See [IBS and your period](/blog/ibs-and-period).

**Does it include a low-FODMAP food list?**
No. Use Monash University's FODMAP app for food content, ideally with a dietitian, and log your meals and symptoms in Get Regular.

**Is it on Android?**
No — iPhone and iPad only (as of 19 Oct 2026).
