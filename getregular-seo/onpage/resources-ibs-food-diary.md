## 1. Answer box

> **Quick answer:** An IBS food diary is a daily log of what and when you eat and drink, your symptoms (with the time they start and how bad they are), your bowel movements (Bristol stool type), and context like stress, sleep and your period. Keep it for at least 2–4 weeks, then look for patterns that repeat — and take it to your doctor or dietitian rather than cutting foods on your own.

## 2. Intro

If you have IBS, you've probably been told to "keep a food diary." Fewer people explain what to write, how long to keep it, or how to read it once you have it. That's the gap this guide fills.

The NHS lists keeping a diary of what you eat and any symptoms as one of the first things to do if you've been diagnosed with IBS [1], and your GI doctor or dietitian will ask about the same details when they look for patterns [2]. A good diary makes that conversation faster and makes you easier to believe.

## 3. Key takeaways

- Log **timing**, not just food: when you ate, when symptoms started and when you went.
- Rate symptoms 0–10 and record your **Bristol stool type** — it's the language your doctor uses.
- Add **stress, sleep and cycle day**: IBS flares aren't only about food [1][3].
- Keep it **2–4 weeks** before drawing conclusions; repeated patterns matter, single bad days don't.
- A diary shows **possible** triggers to test — ideally with a dietitian, especially before a low-FODMAP diet [4][5].

## 4. Why keep a food diary for IBS?

**H2:** Why does a food diary help with IBS?

**Answer:** Because IBS symptoms come and go and have more than one driver, and memory blurs them. A diary turns "it's always bad" into specifics: which meals, how long after, which stool types, and what else was going on.

Irritable bowel syndrome (IBS) is a disorder of gut–brain interaction: a group of symptoms — repeated abdominal pain plus changes in bowel movements (diarrhea, constipation or both) — without visible damage to the digestive tract [3]. About 12% of people in the US are estimated to have IBS [3].

Three reasons a diary earns its keep:
1. **Triggers differ from person to person.** The NHS notes flare-ups can be triggered by alcohol, caffeine, spicy or fatty food, stress and anxiety, or frequent antibiotics — and sometimes by nothing obvious [6].
2. **Doctors diagnose IBS from symptom patterns over time.** They look at how often pain happens, whether it relates to bowel movements, and how stool frequency and form change [2][7].
3. **Diet changes need a baseline.** If your doctor suggests more fiber, avoiding gluten or a low-FODMAP diet, you'll want "before" data to compare against [4].

> **Not diagnosed yet?** A diary is still worth keeping, but see a doctor first if you've had symptoms for more than 4 weeks [6] — and promptly if you have any red flags (§11).

## 5. What to log {#what-to-log}

**H2:** What should you write in an IBS food diary?

**Answer:** Five things, every day: what you ate and drank (with portion and time), symptoms (time started and severity), bowel movements (time and Bristol type), daily context (stress, sleep, exercise, cycle day) and any medicines or supplements.

| Field | What to write | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| Date and time | Clock time of each meal, drink, symptom and bowel movement | Timing is how you link a symptom to what came before it |
| Meal / drink | Specific foods, how it was cooked, sauces and drinks ("pasta with garlic-cream sauce, 1 glass wine") | "Pasta" alone hides the likely suspects (garlic, cream, wine) |
| Portion | Rough size: small / normal / large, or cups/handfuls | Many FODMAP effects depend on dose [5] |
| Symptom | Pain, bloating, gas, urgency, nausea, feeling of incomplete emptying | IBS has more than one symptom; they may have different patterns [3] |
| Onset time | When the symptom started | Separates "within the hour" from "next morning" |
| Severity 0–10 | 0 = none, 10 = worst imaginable | Lets you compare days and see whether a change helps |
| Bowel movement | Time, Bristol type 1–7, urgency (yes/no), straining (yes/no), mucus (yes/no) | Stool form defines IBS subtype [2][7] |
| Stress 0–10 | One rating per day, plus a word on why | Stress is a recognized flare trigger [1][6] |
| Sleep | Hours, and good/poor | Poor sleep days often cluster with bad gut days — worth checking in your own log |
| Cycle day | Day 1 = first day of period | Many women with IBS have more symptoms during their period [3] |
| Meds / supplements | Name, dose, time (incl. laxatives, antidiarrheals, antibiotics, iron) | Several can change bowel habits [8] |
| Notes | Ate out, ate fast, travelled, ill, exercised | Context explains outliers |

**What you can skip:** calorie counts and gram-perfect weights. Precise enough to repeat beats perfect.

**Bristol type in one line:** types 1–2 are hard or lumpy, 3–4 are formed and easy to pass, 5–7 are soft to watery [9]. See the full [Bristol stool chart](/resources/bristol-stool-chart).

## 6. How long to keep it {#how-long}

**H2:** How long should you keep an IBS food diary?

**Answer:** At least 2 weeks, and ideally 4. Two weeks lets common meals repeat; four weeks covers a full menstrual cycle and gives you enough flares to compare.

- **Before a GI appointment:** 2 weeks is the useful minimum — see the [GI appointment checklist](/resources/gastroenterologist-appointment-prep).
- **During a low-FODMAP diet:** keep logging through all three steps. Monash University describes Step 1 (low-FODMAP) as lasting 2–6 weeks, followed by reintroduction challenges of about 3 days each, with the whole reintroduction phase taking around 6–8 weeks [5][10].
- **After a change:** log for at least 1–2 weeks after any change (new medicine, new fiber supplement) before judging it.

3. Plain meals (rice, chicken, salmon, oats with black coffee) were followed by type 4 stools.

## 8. How to read your diary {#read-it}

**H2:** How do you find patterns in an IBS food diary?

**Answer:** Start from your worst days and work backwards in time, then check whether the same suspects show up before your good days too. A real pattern repeats — and is mostly absent on symptom-free days.

**Step-by-step (render as numbered steps):**
1. **Mark your flare days** (severity ≥ 6, or Bristol 6–7 / 1–2 when that's unusual for you).
2. **Look back 0–24 hours** from each symptom onset. List every food, drink, stressor and sleep note in that window.
3. **Count repeats.** Which items appear before most flares?
4. **Check the good days.** If the suspect appears just as often before good days, it's probably not the main driver. Foods you eat daily often look guilty by coincidence.
5. **Check dose and combinations.** Small amounts may be fine while larger portions aren't, and two suspects together may matter more than either alone [5].
6. **Check non-food context.** Stress ≥ 7, short sleep, cycle days 1–3, illness and travel.
7. **Write one testable question** — "Do I get urgent type 6 stools after garlic-heavy dinners?" — and take it to your doctor or dietitian.

**What a diary can't tell you:** whether you have IBS or something else, or the right diet for you. The ACG recommends a *limited* trial of a low-FODMAP diet for IBS [4], and Monash and the AGA recommend doing it with a dietitian's guidance [5][11]. Don't stay on a restrictive diet indefinitely.

More on telling real patterns from coincidence: [IBS trigger foods: how to test yours](/blog/ibs-trigger-foods).

## 9. IBS subtype and your diary

**H2:** Can a food diary show whether you have IBS-C, IBS-D or IBS-M?

**Answer:** It can show the stool pattern your doctor uses to name the subtype. Subtype is based on stool form on days with abnormal bowel movements: IBS-D if more than a quarter are loose or watery (Bristol 6–7) and less than a quarter hard; IBS-C the reverse; IBS-M if more than a quarter are each [3][7].

| Subtype | Hard/lumpy stools (Bristol 1–2) | Loose/watery stools (Bristol 6–7) |
|---|---|---|
| IBS-C | more than 25% | less than 25% |
| IBS-D | less than 25% | more than 25% |
| IBS-M | more than 25% | more than 25% |

*Percentages are of bowel movements on days with at least one abnormal bowel movement [3]. Only a clinician can diagnose IBS or its subtype.* Read: [IBS-C, IBS-D or IBS-M? How a 2-week stool log shows your pattern](/blog/ibs-c-vs-ibs-d).

## 11. When to see a doctor

**H2:** When should you see a doctor about IBS symptoms?

See a GP or doctor if you think you may have IBS and symptoms have lasted more than 4 weeks [6]. **Seek prompt medical advice** — don't just keep a diary — if you have:
- blood in your stool, bleeding from your bottom, or black, tarry stools [2][12]
- unexplained weight loss [2][6]
- anemia, or signs like unusual tiredness, shortness of breath or pale skin [2][6]
- a lump or swelling in your tummy [6]
- symptoms that start later in life, or a family history of colon cancer, inflammatory bowel disease or celiac disease [2][13]
- diarrhea that wakes you from sleep [16], a high fever, or frequent vomiting [14]

Being told you have IBS doesn't mean new or different symptoms should be ignored. See [changes in bowel habits that need a doctor](/blog/when-to-see-doctor-bowel-changes).

## 15. FAQ

**H2:** IBS food diary FAQ

**How do I keep a food diary for IBS?**
Write down every meal, drink and snack with the time and rough portion; every symptom with the time it started and a 0–10 rating; every bowel movement with its Bristol type; and one line of daily context (stress, sleep, cycle day, medicines). Do it within an hour of each event for 2–4 weeks.

**What are the most common IBS trigger foods?**
They vary by person. The NHS lists alcohol, caffeine and spicy or fatty foods among common triggers [6], and foods high in FODMAPs — such as some fruits, onion, garlic, wheat, dairy and sweeteners ending in "-ol" — affect many people with IBS [8]. Your diary shows which, if any, matter for you.

**Why is my IBS worse at night?**
Bigger evening meals, alcohol, the day's stress and lying down can all play a part — log the times to see your own pattern. But diarrhea or pain that wakes you from sleep is worth discussing with a doctor, because diarrhea at night is a recognized alarm feature that can point to something other than IBS [16].

**Should I start a low-FODMAP diet on my own?**
It's better with a dietitian. The ACG recommends a limited trial of a low-FODMAP diet for IBS [4], and Monash University advises working with a FODMAP-trained dietitian through all three steps [10]. Keep your diary going throughout.

**Can you have normal poop with IBS?**
Yes. Many people with IBS have normal bowel movements on some days and abnormal ones on others [3]. That's why logging over weeks, not days, matters.

**Is IBS diagnosed with a test?**
There's no single test for IBS. Doctors diagnose it from your symptom pattern and history, sometimes with blood or stool tests to rule out other conditions [2][15].
