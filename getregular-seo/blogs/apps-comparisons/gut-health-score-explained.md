> **Quick answer:** A gut health score is a number an app or test company creates to summarise your digestion, usually on a 0–100 scale. There's no standard, medically validated gut health score. App scores are typically built from your own logs, such as stool form (Bristol type), how often you go, and symptoms, often weighted toward recent days. A score can show trends over time; it can't diagnose a condition or measure your microbiome.

People search "what is my gut health score" after an app hands them a number with little explanation. That's a fair complaint. App Store reviewers of one IBS app, for example, wrote that its symptom score "makes no sense" and that no FAQ explained how it was calculated. This guide explains how these numbers are usually built, including ours, and what they leave out.

**Key takeaways**
- "Gut health score" has no shared definition; every company builds its own.
- Most app scores lean on stool form, which tracks loosely with how fast food moves through your gut [1], plus frequency and symptoms.
- Direct-to-consumer microbiome test scores have little proven clinical value, according to a 2025 international expert consensus [2].
- Trends across weeks are more useful than any single day's number.
- A good score doesn't rule out disease. Red-flag symptoms need a doctor whatever the number says.

## What is a gut health score?

A gut health score is a composite number that condenses several gut-related inputs into one figure, so you can see at a glance whether things look better or worse than usual. The inputs, the formula and the scale differ by company.

There are four main kinds:

| Score type | Built from | Example of how it's described | What it's good for | Main limit |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Stool-log score** | Stool form, frequency, sometimes time on the toilet | Throne: 0–100%, from stool "quality" and "frequency", past 30 days, recent data weighted more, with a personal baseline [3] | Tracking regularity trends | Says nothing about causes |
| **Food score** | Ingredients or nutrients in a meal | Scores for fibre, processing, additives | Comparing meals | Doesn't know how *your* gut reacts |
| **Symptom/log composite** | Stool + symptoms + meals | App-specific (Get Regular's is below) | One daily summary of your own logs | Formula often not published |
| **Microbiome test score** | Sequencing of a stool sample | Diversity or "balance" indices | Research | Little proven clinical usefulness [2] |

## How do apps calculate a gut health score?

Most apps calculate a gut health score by converting each log into points (for example, Bristol type 3–4 scores higher than 1 or 7), averaging over a recent window, and weighting newer days more heavily. Some then compare you with your own baseline rather than a population average.

The building blocks usually look like this:

1. **Stool form.** The Bristol Stool Form Scale runs from type 1 (separate hard lumps) to type 7 (watery). It was developed because stool form correlates with whole-gut transit time [1]. Types 3–4 are generally treated as the middle of the range ([what healthy poop looks like](/blog/what-does-healthy-poop-look-like)).
2. **Frequency.** How often you go, compared with a typical range or your own normal ([how often you should poop](/blog/how-often-should-you-poop)).
3. **Symptoms.** Bloating, pain, urgency, straining, logged with severity.
4. **Time window and weighting.** A 7- or 30-day window smooths out one bad day; recency weighting lets the score respond to change [3].
5. **Personal baseline.** Some apps learn your normal over time [3].

Throne's help center, for example, describes its inputs, range, window and weighting but not the exact formula or weights [3]. That's common. If an app won't tell you the inputs and window, treat its score as a mood ring, not a measurement.

> **Original asset: the "score scrutiny" checklist.** Before trusting any gut health score, find answers to these six questions in the app's help pages or policy:
> 1. **Inputs:** which logs feed it (stool, food, symptoms, sleep, other)?
> 2. **Window:** how many days does it cover?
> 3. **Weighting:** do recent days count more?
> 4. **Baseline:** is it compared with you or with everyone?
> 5. **Missing data:** what happens on days you don't log, or days with no bowel movement?
> 6. **Method:** is it a published rule, a machine-learning model, or a general AI model's judgement, and has anyone checked it against clinical measures?

## Can you actually measure gut health?

No single test measures "gut health," because it isn't one thing. Clinicians assess specific questions instead: stool pattern and symptoms, blood or stool tests for inflammation or infection, and scopes or imaging when needed. An IBS diagnosis, for instance, rests on a symptom pattern plus history, with tests to rule out other problems [7].

Microbiome tests are the most common claim to "measure gut health." A 2025 international consensus of microbiome experts concluded that evidence supporting the clinical usefulness of microbiome testing is scarce, and that direct-to-consumer tests are offered without consensus on regulation or proven value in clinical practice [2]. A diversity index from a stool kit is a research measure, not a health grade.

What *can* be measured at home, imperfectly, is your stool form and frequency and your symptoms over time. Even then, self-reporting is noisy. In a study of people with diarrhea-predominant IBS, patients' own Bristol ratings agreed only moderately with an AI's ratings of photos of the same stools, and the AI agreed well with expert gastroenterologists [6]. That's one small study, but it shows that consistent recording matters as much as the formula.

## How to read your score without overreacting

Read a gut health score as a trend line, not a verdict. A single low day means little; a slide over two or three weeks, with the logs to explain it, is worth attention.

- **Check the logs behind a change.** Did your Bristol types drift toward 1–2 or 6–7? Did you skip logging?
- **Compare weeks, not days.** A 7-day window already smooths daily noise.
- **Don't chase the number.** Changing your diet to please a score can backfire. Test one change at a time, ideally with a clinician.
- **Bring trends, not scores, to your doctor.** "Type 6 most days for three weeks, waking at night" is more useful than "my score dropped to 54."

## When to see a doctor

A reassuring score doesn't rule anything out. See a doctor if you have blood in your stool or black, tarry stool, unintended weight loss, anemia, or a family history of colorectal cancer, IBD or celiac disease [7]; symptoms that wake you at night, fever, or severe pain; or a change in bowel habits that lasts more than a few weeks or starts after age 50.

## FAQ

**What is a good gut health score?**
It depends entirely on the app's scale and formula. Within one app, a score that is stable or improving over several weeks, with Bristol types mostly 3–4 and no worrying symptoms, is the encouraging pattern. Don't compare numbers across apps.

**How do I check my gut health score?**
Use an app that publishes its inputs and window, log consistently for at least a week, and read the trend. There's no medical blood or stool test that produces a "gut health score."

**Is a gut health score calculator accurate?**
Online calculators and app scores are summaries, not measurements, and none we found has been clinically validated. Their usefulness depends on honest, consistent logging and on how transparent the method is.

**Can a microbiome test tell me how healthy my gut is?**
Not reliably for clinical decisions. Expert consensus says the clinical usefulness of microbiome testing is not yet established and consumer tests lack regulation and proven value [2].
