## What does mySymptoms do well, and what does it cost?

mySymptoms is a full-featured food, symptom and lifestyle diary with a food database, barcode scanning, correlation analysis and clinician sharing, sold by subscription. Its US listing and website describe the following [1][2].

| Feature | What the listing says |
| --- | --- |
| **Logging** | Food, drink, medication, stress, exercise and environmental factors; symptom intensity, duration and notes; energy, sleep and bowel movements (Bristol scale) |
| **Food input** | Search a food database or scan barcodes; add your own foods and meals |
| **Analysis** | "Correlation analysis" of foods and symptoms, histograms, trend charts and an event review list |
| **Sharing** | PDF or CSV export; a "Clinic" web app for sharing with a clinician |
| **Privacy statements** | Account starts with an anonymous username; data encrypted in transit and at rest in the cloud; says it never shares or sells personal data |

| **Platforms** | iPhone, iPad and Android (Google Play listing found) |
| **Rating** | 4.6 from 4,049 US ratings; last updated 20 Aug 2026 |

The strengths are real. It has been around since 2010 [1], the rating count is large, and the combination of barcode scanning and a clinician portal is something most competitors here don't list. Some reviewers who like it mention the report they took to an appointment.

## Why do people look for a mySymptoms alternative?

People usually leave for one of four reasons: cost, logging effort, analysis quality or export problems. The themes below come from the team's own reading of a sample of 150 US App Store reviews of mySymptoms (collected in 2026), not from a statistical survey, so treat them as common complaints rather than proportions.

1. **Cost and the trial.** The listing says the app is subscription-based [1]. Some reviewers describe reaching a paid screen straight after creating an account and feeling surprised by it. Check the "In-App Purchases" list and trial terms on any listing before you start.
2. **Logging effort.** Ingredient-level diaries ask for more input. Reviewers describe typing every ingredient as a chore, and a barcode scanner that doesn't recognise some foods (home cooking and restaurant meals don't have barcodes anyway).
3. **Analysis that doesn't add much.** Some reviewers say the correlation view told them nothing new or flagged everyday foods. That's a limit of any simple correlation, not only this app: foods you eat daily will always sit before symptoms. [Why that happens, and how to avoid the trap](/blog/best-food-symptom-tracker-apps).
4. **Export not working at the wrong moment.** A few reviewers describe emailed reports failing just before a doctor's visit. Test your first export early, whichever app you pick.

If none of those apply, you probably don't need an alternative. Many people use mySymptoms happily for years.

Bowelle is the lighter, cheaper and more private-by-design option; mySymptoms is the deeper, more structured one. They suit different temperaments, and both are on iPhone and Android.

| | mySymptoms [1][2] | Bowelle [3][4] |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Philosophy** | Detailed diary with food database and correlation reports | "Quickest, most easy-to-use food & symptom diary"; single-screen entry |
| **Price** | 7-day trial, then $9.99/mo, $39.99/6 mo, $59.99/yr | Free download; optional premium purchases ($2.49 to $17.99 listed) |
| **Account** | Anonymous username; name and email only needed to share with a clinician | No registration; FAQ says data is stored on your device, not Bowelle's servers |
| **Food entry** | Database search, barcode, custom foods | Free text, saved lists, optional photos |
| **Analysis** | Correlation analysis, histograms, event review | Overlapping charts and daily averages; you read the patterns yourself |

Choose mySymptoms if you want the software to do some of the pattern work and you're happy to type. Choose Bowelle if you'd rather log in seconds and read your own charts. Some Bowelle reviewers say they wanted more automatic "intelligence", which is the trade you make for simplicity.

## Before you switch: how do you keep your history?

Export everything from your old app first, because none of the listings we read mention importing another app's diary. mySymptoms lists PDF and CSV export [1]; a CSV is the more useful copy because you can open it in a spreadsheet later.

1. **Export a CSV and a PDF** of your full history, and save both outside the app.
2. **Note your notable dates:** first occurrence of each symptom, medication starts, diet changes. These matter more to a clinician than months of daily rows.
3. **Overlap for one to two weeks** if you can: log in both apps so your pattern isn't interrupted.
4. **Cancel through your Apple ID or Google Play subscriptions**, not by deleting the app, which does not stop billing.
5. **Ask for deletion** of your account data if you're done with the service; each developer's privacy policy explains how ([what to check in any gut app's privacy policy](/blog/gut-health-app-privacy)).

## How to track food and symptoms in any app

Good tracking works the same way in every app: log when it happens, log symptoms separately from meals, and keep going long enough to see a pattern. NHS guidance notes that food intolerance symptoms usually start a few hours after eating, and that a clinician may ask you to keep a food and symptoms diary [11].

1. Log each meal and drink when you have it, with a photo or a few words. Include sauces, garlic, onion and dairy.
2. Log symptoms with their own start time and a 0 to 10 severity.
3. Log bowel movements with a Bristol type, and "no bowel movement" days too.
4. Keep going for two to four weeks before reading patterns, then compare your worst days with your best.
5. Treat any pattern as a hypothesis to test with a clinician or dietitian ([reading your own food-symptom patterns](/blog/food-symptom-patterns)).

## When to see a doctor

A diary can organise your symptoms, but it can't rule out other conditions. NIDDK lists rectal bleeding, bloody or black, tarry stools, weight loss and anemia as symptoms that may suggest a problem other than IBS [12]. See a doctor promptly if you have:

- Blood in your stool, or black, tarry stool
- Unintended weight loss
- Anemia (your clinician may find it on a blood test)
- Severe or worsening abdominal pain, fever, or symptoms that wake you at night
- A new or lasting change in bowel habits, especially after age 50 or with a family history of colorectal cancer, inflammatory bowel disease or celiac disease

## FAQ

**Is there a free alternative to mySymptoms?**
Yes. Bowelle, Plop and Bearable are free to download, and each describes what its free version includes [3][5][6][7]. Free versions differ in export, history and analysis, so read each listing's in-app purchase list before relying on one for a doctor's visit.

**How much does mySymptoms cost?**
As listed in the US App Store on 19 Oct 2026: a 7-day free trial, then $9.99 a month, $39.99 for six months or $59.99 a year [1]. The listing describes the app as subscription-based.

**Is mySymptoms HIPAA compliant?**
Its listing says the app meets HIPAA and GDPR standards when you share a diary with a clinician [1]. HIPAA generally covers health plans, clearinghouses, most providers and their business associates, and OCR says a developer of a consumer app that people download to track their own data is generally not one of them [13]. If compliance matters to you, ask the developer which part of the service the claim covers, and read the privacy policy.

**Does mySymptoms work on Android?**
Yes. A mySymptoms listing exists on Google Play (found on 19 Oct 2026), so it isn't limited to iPhone.

**Which is better, mySymptoms or Bowelle?**
Neither wins outright. mySymptoms offers a food database, barcode scanning and correlation reports for a subscription; Bowelle offers fast, account-free logging that's free to download. See the head-to-head table above.
