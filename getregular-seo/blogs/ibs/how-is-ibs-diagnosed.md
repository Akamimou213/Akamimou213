> **Quick answer:** IBS is diagnosed by a clinician, not by a single test. The doctor takes a detailed history, examines you, and checks your symptoms against standard criteria. Under Rome IV, that's belly pain at least weekly for 3 months, linked to bowel movements or to changes in stool frequency or form, starting at least 6 months ago. They also look for red flags and order a few targeted tests, such as a celiac blood test if you have diarrhea. Rome V criteria, published in 2026, loosen some thresholds.

If you think you might have IBS, you're probably wondering two things: "how do they know?" and "what if it's something else?" Both are good questions. Many people describe feeling brushed off with a label, while others are worried a test will miss something serious.

Modern IBS diagnosis is designed to handle both concerns: a confident, symptom-based diagnosis when the picture fits, and investigation when something doesn't. This guide explains the criteria, which tests are and aren't routine, the warning signs that change the plan, and how to arrive at your appointment with a record that makes the conversation faster.

**Key takeaways**
- There's no blood, stool or imaging test that confirms IBS. The diagnosis is clinical [3][4].
- The ACG recommends a "positive diagnostic strategy": diagnose from the symptom pattern plus limited tests, rather than testing for everything first [1].
- Typical targeted tests are a celiac blood test, and inflammation markers if you have diarrhea symptoms [1]. Colonoscopy isn't routine under 45 without warning signs [1].
- Red flags (bleeding, weight loss, anemia, older age at onset, family history) change the plan [1][3].
- Online symptom checkers and apps can help you prepare, but they can't diagnose IBS.

## How is IBS diagnosed?

IBS is diagnosed when a clinician confirms a typical symptom pattern and finds no features that point to another condition. NIDDK describes the process: your doctor reviews your symptoms, medical and family history, and does a physical exam, then sometimes orders tests to rule out other problems [3].

The American College of Gastroenterology recommends a **positive diagnostic strategy** over a "diagnosis of exclusion" [1]. In practice that means:

1. **History:** your symptoms, how often and how long, how pain relates to bowel movements, your stool pattern, medicines, diet and family history.
2. **Exam:** including a belly exam, and sometimes a rectal exam.
3. **Red-flag check:** anything that suggests a different diagnosis.
4. **Limited, targeted tests** based on your symptoms and subtype.
5. **A diagnosis and plan**, often starting treatment right away.

Why not test for everything? In a trial of 300 primary care patients described in the guideline, a positive strategy with only basic blood tests performed as well as extensive testing on symptoms, quality of life and patient satisfaction. No cases of inflammatory bowel disease, celiac disease or cancer were found with either approach at one year, and costs were lower [1]. Cleveland Clinic adds that your provider will make sure your colon cancer screening is up to date [9].

## What are the Rome IV criteria for IBS?

The Rome IV criteria are the international symptom criteria for IBS, published by the Rome Foundation in 2016 and used in the ACG's 2021 guideline [1][2]. They are:

| Rome IV requirement | What it means in plain terms |
|---|---|
| Recurrent abdominal pain, on average at least 1 day a week in the last 3 months | Belly pain that keeps coming back, not a one-off |
| Associated with **2 or more** of: (1) related to defecation, (2) a change in stool frequency, (3) a change in stool form | The pain is tied to bowel movements, or comes with going more or less often, or with harder or looser stools |
| Criteria met for the last 3 months, with symptoms starting at least 6 months before diagnosis | It's been going on for a while |

Bloating is common in IBS but isn't required for the diagnosis [1]. Once IBS is diagnosed, your stool pattern on abnormal days sets your subtype (IBS-C, IBS-D, IBS-M or IBS-U). Our guide to [IBS-C vs IBS-D](/blog/ibs-c-vs-ibs-d) shows how a 2-week stool log is used.

## What changed in Rome V (2026)?

In 2026 the Rome Foundation published Rome V, the updated classification of bowel disorders [5]. For IBS, reviews of the new criteria highlight two main changes: abdominal *discomfort* is reintroduced alongside pain, and symptoms must be intermittent rather than continuous [6]. Medscape reports that the pain or discomfort frequency threshold was relaxed from at least once a week to at least 3 days a month over the past 3 months [7].

What that means for you:

- **More people now meet the criteria.** In one 2026 study of 685 people with suspected IBS at a hospital clinic in China, 35% met Rome IV, 65% met Rome V, and Rome IV missed nearly half of the people Rome V identified [8].
- **The basic approach hasn't changed.** It's still a clinical, symptom-based diagnosis with a red-flag check and limited testing.
- **Your clinician decides which version to use.** Many US practices and guidelines still reference Rome IV, which the 2021 ACG guideline is built on [1].

If you were told you "didn't quite meet the criteria" in the past, it may be worth revisiting with your doctor.

## Which tests are used to diagnose IBS?

Tests in IBS are used to rule out other conditions, not to prove IBS. Which ones you get depends on your symptoms, age and risk factors. Here's what the ACG guideline recommends for adults [1]:

| Test | When it's typically used | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Celiac disease blood test | IBS with diarrhea symptoms (strong recommendation) [1] | Celiac disease can mimic IBS. Keep eating gluten until tested [10] |
| Fecal calprotectin (or lactoferrin) and C-reactive protein (CRP) | Suspected IBS with diarrhea and no alarm features [1] | Screen for inflammatory bowel disease |
| Complete blood count | Commonly part of the basic workup [1] | Checks for anemia |
| Stool tests for infection | **Not routine.** Only with risk factors such as travel or untreated water (e.g., for Giardia) [1] | Most chronic IBS isn't caused by an ongoing infection |
| Colonoscopy | **Not routine under 45** without warning signs. Considered with alarm features, for age-based colon cancer screening, or in some older people with diarrhea [1] | Looks for IBD, microscopic colitis, polyps or cancer |
| Food allergy or IgG "sensitivity" panels | **Not recommended** routinely. IgG panels aren't validated [1] | Low accuracy, many false positives |
| Breath tests | Sometimes, for suspected lactose or fructose malabsorption or bacterial overgrowth [9] | Depends on symptoms |

The NHS describes a similar approach in the UK: there's no test for IBS, but a GP may order a blood test for celiac disease and stool tests for infection and IBD [4]. If food seems central to your symptoms, our guide to [IBS vs food intolerance](/blog/ibs-vs-food-intolerance) explains how those overlap.

### Can an online "IBS symptom checker" tell me if I have IBS?

No. A symptom checker can tell you whether your symptoms *resemble* the IBS criteria. It can't examine you, interpret red flags in context, or order tests. Use it, at most, as a prompt to book a visit and to organize what you'll say. The same goes for tracking apps, including ours.

## What to bring to your appointment

Bring a short, factual summary of your symptoms. It maps directly onto what the clinician needs to decide. NHS guidance suggests writing down the details of your symptoms before your appointment so you don't forget them [4].

**One-page pre-visit summary (template):**

| What the clinician asks | What to note |
|---|---|
| How often do you have belly pain? | Pain days per week, averaged over the last 4 weeks |
| Is it related to bowel movements? | Better, worse or unchanged after going |
| Has stool frequency changed? | Typical bowel movements per day or week, now vs before |
| Has stool form changed? | Share of hard (Bristol 1–2) vs loose (6–7) stools on abnormal days |
| When did it start? | Month and year, and any trigger (infection, travel, antibiotics, stress) |
| Red flags? | Any blood, black stools, weight loss, night-time symptoms, fever |
| Family history? | Colon cancer, IBD, celiac disease |
| Medicines and supplements | Including laxatives, antidiarrheals, recent antibiotics |
| What have you tried? | Diet changes, fiber, over-the-counter products, and whether they helped |

**Illustrative 4-week summary** (invented example, not real data):

| Item | Summary |
|---|---|
| Pain days | 2–3 per week, usually eases after a bowel movement |
| Bowel movements | 1–3 per day. On abnormal days, about 40% type 6–7 and 10% type 1–2 |
| Onset | March 2026, about 2 weeks after a stomach bug |
| Red flags | None noted. Weight stable |
| Tried | Cutting coffee to 1 cup helped urgency a little |

A summary like this takes the guesswork out of "how often?" and "since when?". It also helps you avoid forgetting things under appointment pressure, which many people describe. ## When to see a doctor

See a doctor if you have ongoing belly pain with changes in your bowel habits. The NHS suggests booking if symptoms have lasted more than 4 weeks [4]. These red flags call for prompt assessment, as they're not explained by IBS until proven otherwise [1][3][9]:

- rectal bleeding or blood in the stool, or black, tarry stool
- unexplained weight loss
- anemia (for example, on a blood test, or symptoms like breathlessness and unusual tiredness)
- diarrhea or pain that wakes you at night
- fever, vomiting, or severe pain that doesn't ease after a bowel movement
- symptoms starting at an older age (many guidelines use 50 as a guide)
- a family history of colon cancer, inflammatory bowel disease or celiac disease

## FAQ

### Do I have IBS or something worse?

Only an assessment can answer that. The reassuring part is that in people without red flags, a careful history with limited tests is a reliable way to diagnose IBS [1]. If your symptoms change or new red flags appear, go back. A diagnosis can be revisited.

### What kind of doctor diagnoses IBS?

Usually a primary care doctor. They may refer you to a gastroenterologist if the picture is unclear, symptoms are severe, or tests are needed. A dietitian may join later for diet-based treatment.

### Can blood tests show IBS?

No blood test confirms IBS [3]. Blood tests are used to look for other conditions, such as celiac disease, anemia and inflammation [1].

### Do I need a colonoscopy to be diagnosed with IBS?

Not usually, if you're under 45 and have no warning signs [1]. A colonoscopy may be recommended if you have red flags, are due for colon cancer screening based on age, or have features suggesting another condition.

### How long does it take to get diagnosed with IBS?

The criteria require symptoms that began at least 6 months earlier (Rome IV) [2], though NIDDK notes a doctor may diagnose IBS after a shorter time [3]. The diagnosis itself can often be made at one or two visits, especially if you bring a clear symptom record.
