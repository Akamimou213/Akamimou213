If your gut seems to fall apart every month at the same time, you're not imagining it. Many people with IBS notice that the days before and during their period bring more cramping, bloating and loose stools. Some also notice constipation in the week or so before.

The tricky part is telling period-related IBS flares from other monthly causes, and from period pain itself. This guide explains what's known, what's still uncertain, and how to track your cycle alongside your gut so you can plan around it.

## Does IBS flare up during your period?

For many people with IBS, yes. NIDDK states that women with IBS often have more symptoms during their periods [1]. Cleveland Clinic notes that symptoms may worsen predictably with the menstrual cycle [4].

The best summary of the research is a 2010 meta-analysis. Ten of the 13 studies it reviewed found that, on average, 40–60% of women had more gut symptoms during menses than at other times of the cycle [2]. Interestingly, this happened in women *without* IBS too. The effect was similar, but symptoms were more severe in women with IBS [2].

So a period-related flare isn't a sign that your IBS is getting worse. It's a common, partly predictable pattern, which means you can plan for it.

## Why is IBS worse during your period?

The leading explanation is hormonal. Estrogen and progesterone both drop just before a period starts, and the gut has receptors for both.

- **Pain sensitivity.** A review of the research found that women with IBS showed higher visceral (gut) pain sensitivity during menses than at other cycle phases. Their period cramps were also more intense [3].
- **Hormone withdrawal.** The rise in gut symptoms around menses and early menopause happens when ovarian hormones are low, which suggests hormone withdrawal plays a role [3].
- **Prostaglandins.** These are chemicals released by the uterus around menstruation that can also affect the gut. Some researchers think they contribute to looser stools during periods, though this hasn't been firmly established [7].
- **Overlap with period pain.** In women with both IBS and painful periods, it can be hard to tell whether pain comes from the uterus or the bowel [7].

Stress and sleep often shift around a period too. One diary study found that daily stress tracked with gut symptoms in women with IBS, even after accounting for cycle phase [8]. That's why it helps to log stress alongside your cycle (more in our guide to [IBS and stress](/blog/ibs-and-stress)).

## Which IBS symptoms change across the cycle?

The most consistent changes are around menses. Some people also see constipation and bloating earlier, in the second half of the cycle.

| Cycle phase (typical 28-day cycle) | What studies most often report | Strength of evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Menses (period days) | More loose stools, bloating and belly pain [2]. More diarrhea than constipation reported as worse [2] | Fairly consistent across studies |
| Late luteal (the few days before a period) | Rising pain and discomfort [3] | Moderate |
| Luteal (after ovulation) | Constipation reported more often in one small study [7] | Limited |
| Follicular (after the period) | Fewer symptoms on average than during menses [2] | Moderate |

These are group averages. Your own pattern might be different, and many studies relied on recall rather than daily diaries [2]. That's a good reason to collect your own data.

## How to track IBS across your menstrual cycle

To see a cycle pattern, log your gut symptoms daily for at least two full cycles, then line them up by cycle day rather than calendar date.

**What to log each day:**

1. **Cycle day** (day 1 = first day of bleeding).
2. **Worst belly pain and bloating**, each 0–10.
3. **Bowel movements by Bristol type** (types 1–2 hard, 6–7 loose).
4. **Period cramps**, separately from gut pain if you can tell them apart.
5. **Context:** sleep, stress (0–3), alcohol, unusual meals, pain relievers taken.

**How to read it:** after two cycles, average each symptom by cycle phase. If the pattern repeats, you'll know when to expect it.

**What you can do with that pattern:**
- Plan gentler, regular meals in the expected flare window. Our guide on [what to eat during an IBS flare-up](/blog/ibs-flare-what-to-eat) has ideas.
- Ease off known triggers like alcohol, large fatty meals and extra caffeine in those days.

- Bring the summary to your doctor or gynecologist. Hormonal or medical options may be worth discussing.

## When period-related gut symptoms need a doctor

See a doctor if your period-linked gut symptoms are severe, getting worse, or don't fit your usual IBS. Endometriosis deserves special mention. A meta-analysis found that people with endometriosis are about 3 times more likely to have IBS, and about 1 in 4 people with endometriosis also have IBS [5]. The two can be confused because both cause belly pain and bowel symptoms.

Ask about endometriosis if you have [6]:

- very painful periods, especially if they're getting worse over time
- pain during or after sex
- painful bowel movements or painful urination during your period
- trouble getting pregnant

Get prompt medical care for any of these [1][9]:

- blood in your stool (not menstrual blood), or black, tarry stool
- unexplained weight loss or signs of anemia, such as unusual tiredness or shortness of breath
- very heavy bleeding, bleeding between periods, or new bleeding after menopause
- bowel symptoms that wake you at night, fever or vomiting
- new bowel symptoms after age 50, or a family history of colon cancer, inflammatory bowel disease or celiac disease

## FAQ

### Why do I get diarrhea on my period?

Looser stools during menses are common, with or without IBS [2]. Falling hormone levels and prostaglandins are likely contributors [3][7]. If it's severe, bloody, or comes with fever, see a doctor.

### Why am I constipated before my period?

Some women notice harder stools in the second half of the cycle, after ovulation. The evidence is thinner here than for period-time diarrhea [7]. Tracking two cycles will show whether it's a reliable pattern for you.

### Does IBS get better after menopause?

Research is limited. Reviews link low ovarian hormone levels in early menopause to more gut symptoms in some women [3]. New bowel symptoms after 50 should always be checked by a doctor first.

### Can hormonal birth control help period-related IBS?

Possibly for some people, but the evidence specific to IBS is limited. It's a question for your doctor or gynecologist, and your two-cycle log will make that conversation more useful.
