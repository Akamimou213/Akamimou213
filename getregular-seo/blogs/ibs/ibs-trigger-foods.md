> **Quick answer:** There's no single list of foods everyone with IBS should avoid. The foods guidelines most often flag are large, fatty, spicy or processed meals, alcohol and fizzy drinks, more than about 3 caffeinated drinks a day, and foods high in fermentable carbs (FODMAPs) such as onion, garlic, wheat, some fruits, milk and legumes. Rather than cutting them all, test suspected triggers one at a time and keep the foods you tolerate.

Search for IBS symptoms and foods to avoid and you'll find lists that would leave you eating plain rice forever. Most people with IBS do react to *something* they eat. But the something is personal, often dose-dependent, and usually a much shorter list than the internet suggests.

This guide covers the usual suspects and why each can bother an IBS gut. It also gives you a simple way to test your own triggers without cutting out half your diet.

**Key takeaways**
- Food-related symptoms are very common in IBS. One review reports that 85–90% of people with IBS notice symptoms worsening with certain foods [5].
- Guideline-backed first steps are modest: regular meals, less fat, spice, alcohol, fizzy drinks and caffeine, and a cap on fresh fruit portions [1].
- For many people, FODMAPs are the main food factor. A short, dietitian-guided low-FODMAP trial followed by reintroduction is the evidence-based approach [3][4].
- Test one suspect at a time. Broad elimination can harm nutrition and doesn't tell you which food mattered [4].

## IBS symptoms: which foods should you avoid first?

Start with the changes most guidelines agree on, not a long ban list. The NHS's IBS diet advice is a good baseline [1]:

- don't delay or skip meals, and don't eat too quickly
- don't eat lots of fatty, spicy or processed food
- have no more than 3 portions of fresh fruit a day (a portion is 80 g)
- have no more than 3 cups of tea, coffee or other caffeinated drinks a day
- don't drink lots of alcohol or fizzy drinks
- drink plenty of fluids, around 8 to 10 drinks a day

If those steps don't help enough, the next layer is the usual suspects below. Here's the overview:

| Usual suspect | Why it may affect IBS | What guidance says | How to test it |
|---|---|---|---|
| Onion, garlic, wheat, rye | High in fructans (a FODMAP) [3] | Part of a low-FODMAP trial [2][3] | Dietitian-guided FODMAP challenge |
| Apples, pears, mango, watermelon, cherries | High in fructose and/or polyols [3] | Swap for lower-FODMAP fruit such as orange, kiwi, strawberries [3] | One fruit, measured portion, 3 days |
| Cow's milk, ice cream, yogurt | Lactose [3] | Lactose-free milk and hard cheeses are lower-FODMAP alternatives [3] | Lactose-free swap for 2 weeks, then rechallenge |
| Beans, lentils, chickpeas | GOS (a FODMAP) [3] | Part of a low-FODMAP trial [3] | Small portion, then larger |
| Sugar-free sweets and gum ("-ol" sweeteners) | Polyols such as sorbitol [2][3] | Low-FODMAP diets limit them [2] | Read labels and remove for 2 weeks |
| Fatty, fried or very spicy meals | Can stimulate the gut strongly | Cut down [1] | Compare similar meals with less fat |
| Coffee and other caffeine | Stimulates the bowel | Max 3 caffeinated drinks a day [1] | Cut to 1 a day for a week, then add back |
| Alcohol and fizzy drinks | Gut irritation, gas | Cut down [1] | Alcohol-free week, then reintroduce |
| Too much fiber, too fast (especially insoluble, like bran) | Gas and bloating | Prefer soluble fiber and increase slowly [2][4] | Add 2–3 g a day and log symptoms [2] |

The FODMAP examples come from Monash University, whose researchers developed the low-FODMAP diet [3]. Monash is the best place to check where a specific food falls.

## Why do some foods trigger IBS symptoms?

Most food triggers in IBS aren't allergies. They're foods that create more gas, water or movement in the gut, felt by a gut that's more sensitive than average.

- **FODMAPs** draw water into the bowel and are fermented by gut bacteria into gas. In IBS, that stretch can cause pain and bloating [4].
- **Fat, caffeine and alcohol** can speed up or stimulate the bowel.
- **Gluten** may bother some people with IBS who don't have celiac disease [2]. In many cases the culprit may actually be fructans, the FODMAPs found in wheat, rather than gluten itself [4].
- **True food allergies are uncommon in IBS.** They usually cause rapid, reproducible reactions within minutes of eating a food. The ACG doesn't recommend routine allergy testing unless symptoms suggest a real allergy, and IgG "food sensitivity" panels haven't been validated [4].

Dose matters too. Small amounts of a trigger may be fine while larger portions cause trouble [5]. That's why "I ate it once and was fine" doesn't settle the question.

## Can I eat dairy with IBS?

Many people with IBS can eat some dairy, especially lower-lactose options. Monash lists lactose-free milk, hard cheeses, brie, camembert and feta as low-FODMAP alternatives to cow's milk, ice cream and regular yogurt [3].

People with IBS are more likely than others to *report* lactose intolerance. A meta-analysis found they were about 3.5 times as likely to describe themselves as lactose intolerant, but no more likely to actually maldigest lactose on testing [6]. In other words, the IBS gut may react more strongly to the same lactose. A lactose-free trial or a breath test (ask your doctor) can tell you more than guessing [5]. Our guide to [IBS vs food intolerance](/blog/ibs-vs-food-intolerance) explains how to tell the two apart.

## How to test your trigger foods one at a time

The best way to test IBS trigger foods is one suspect at a time, with a clear before-and-after comparison and the same logging every day. Here's a simple method for non-FODMAP suspects like coffee, fried food or alcohol. For FODMAP groups, follow a dietitian's reintroduction plan. Our [low-FODMAP reintroduction tracker](/blog/low-fodmap-reintroduction-tracking) shows how to log it.

**One-food test card**

1. **Baseline (5–7 days):** eat as usual and log meals, stool (Bristol type) and worst symptoms (0–10).
2. **Remove (1–2 weeks):** cut out only the suspect food. Change nothing else if you can.
3. **Reintroduce (2–3 days):** add it back in a normal portion, at a similar time each day.
4. **Compare:** did symptoms rise after reintroduction, beyond your usual day-to-day swings?
5. **Repeat once:** a second round guards against coincidence.

**Illustrative example** (invented data to show the method):

| Phase | Coffee per day | Average worst pain (0–10) | Loose stools (Bristol 6–7) per day | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Baseline, 7 days | 3 | 4.1 | 1.4 | Busy work week |
| Remove, 10 days | 0–1 (decaf) | 2.6 | 0.5 | Mild headache days 1–2 |
| Reintroduce, 3 days | 3 | 3.9 | 1.3 | Loose stools within ~1 hour |
| Repeat, 3 days | 3 | 4.0 | 1.0 | Same pattern |

A pattern like this suggests coffee is worth limiting, perhaps to one cup, rather than proving it's "the" cause. Watch for confounders: in this example, the baseline week was also stressful. ## Why cutting everything out backfires

Cutting out many foods at once rarely gives you an answer and can cause new problems.

- **You can't tell what worked.** If you drop dairy, gluten, coffee and onions together and feel better, you don't know which change mattered.
- **Nutrition suffers.** The ACG notes that long-term over-restriction on a low-FODMAP diet may cause micronutrient deficiencies [4]. A recent review notes that people often cut out trigger foods without testing or clinical guidance [5].
- **Eating gets stressful.** Many people with IBS describe an exhausting cycle of second-guessing every meal. Stress can feed IBS symptoms too.
- **It can mask celiac disease.** Doctors don't recommend going gluten-free before celiac testing, because it can affect the results [8].

The NHS advises seeing a GP if you find you need to avoid lots of different foods to control symptoms. You may be referred to a dietitian [1].

## When to see a doctor

Food changes are for IBS that a doctor has already confirmed. See a doctor first, or again, if you have [4][7]:

- blood in your stool, or black, tarry stool
- unexplained weight loss
- diarrhea that wakes you up at night, fever or vomiting
- signs of anemia, like unusual tiredness or shortness of breath
- symptoms that started after 50, or a family history of colon cancer, inflammatory bowel disease or celiac disease
- reactions within minutes of eating, such as hives, lip or throat swelling, or wheezing (possible food allergy, which needs urgent care if severe)

## FAQ

### What is the number one trigger food for IBS?

There isn't one that applies to everyone. FODMAP-rich foods, fatty meals, caffeine and alcohol are common culprits [1][3], but individual triggers vary. A personal log tells you more than any ranking.

### Are eggs OK for IBS?

Monash lists eggs as a low-FODMAP protein option [3], and many people with IBS tolerate them. How they're cooked matters: fried in lots of fat may sit differently than boiled or poached.

### Is rice good for IBS?

Monash lists rice-based options such as rice pasta and plain rice cakes among its low-FODMAP alternatives [3], and many people find plain rice easy on the gut during flares. For more flare-friendly ideas, see [what to eat during an IBS flare-up](/blog/ibs-flare-what-to-eat). Pair it with fiber from tolerated sources, since the ACG suggests soluble fiber helps overall IBS symptoms [4].

### Can gluten cause IBS symptoms?

Gluten-containing foods can worsen symptoms for some people with IBS who don't have celiac disease [2]. Wheat is also high in fructans, which may be the real trigger for some [4]. Get tested for celiac disease before cutting gluten out [8].
