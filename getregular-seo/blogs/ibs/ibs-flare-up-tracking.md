> **Quick answer:** An IBS flare-up is a stretch of days when your usual symptoms (belly pain, bloating, diarrhea, constipation or both) get noticeably worse. There's no medical definition of how long one lasts. Some people have a bad afternoon, others a rough week or longer. Common triggers include certain foods, alcohol, caffeine, stress, poor sleep and the menstrual cycle, and flares can also happen for no obvious reason. Tracking the 72 hours before each flare is the most useful way to find your own pattern.

If you have IBS, you probably know the feeling. You were fine on Monday, and by Wednesday you're cramping, bloated and planning your day around bathrooms. Then it seems to fade as mysteriously as it came.

Here's the frustrating part: medicine doesn't define a "flare" precisely, so nobody can tell you exactly how long yours should last. What you *can* do is define it for yourself and log it the same way every time. After a few flares, the ones that felt random often turn out to follow a pattern.

**Key takeaways**
- IBS symptoms normally come and go, with better and worse days. A flare is simply a run of worse days [1][2].
- No guideline sets a "normal" flare length, so write down your own definition and compare each flare against it.
- Triggers commonly named in guidance include alcohol, caffeine, spicy or fatty food, stress and anxiety, and the menstrual cycle. Sometimes there is no obvious cause [1][3].
- A flare that looks different from your usual pattern, or comes with blood, weight loss, fever or night-time symptoms, needs a doctor rather than a diary [4][7].

## What is an IBS flare-up?

An IBS flare-up is a period when your IBS symptoms are clearly worse than your usual baseline. The NHS describes it simply: there may be days when symptoms are better and days when they're worse, and the worse days are flare-ups [1].

Irritable bowel syndrome is a disorder of gut-brain interaction. It involves recurring belly pain linked to bowel movements and changes in stool frequency or form, without visible damage to the gut [4]. So a flare usually means *more* of what you already get, not something brand new.

### What do IBS flare-up symptoms feel like?

Flare symptoms are your normal IBS symptoms turned up. The NHS lists the main ones as [1]:

- stomach pain or cramps, often worse after eating and better after a poo
- bloating, with a full or swollen feeling
- diarrhea, sometimes with sudden urgency
- constipation, with straining or a feeling of not emptying fully

Other common symptoms include gas, mucus in the stool, tiredness, nausea and backache [1]. People with IBS often describe flare pain as cramping that comes in waves. If the pain is new, constant, severe, or doesn't ease at all after a bowel movement, don't assume it's a flare (see [When to see a doctor](#when-to-see-a-doctor)).

## How long does an IBS flare-up last?

There's no set length. Some flares last a few hours, some a few days, and some drag on for weeks. Cleveland Clinic notes that IBS symptoms may go away and later return, sometimes frequently and sometimes only during flares [2]. No clinical guideline gives a standard flare duration, and you should be wary of any page that quotes one precise number as if it applied to everyone.

What tends to stretch a flare out:

- **An ongoing trigger.** If the thing driving symptoms is still there (a stressful week, a run of poor sleep, repeated trigger meals), symptoms tend to persist.
- **Knock-on effects.** Constipation can keep bloating and pain going until things move. Diarrhea can leave the gut irritated and you dehydrated.
- **Worry about the next flare.** Many people on IBS forums describe an anxiety loop: symptoms raise stress, and stress keeps symptoms going. Research supports a two-way link (more on that in our guide to [IBS and stress](/blog/ibs-and-stress)) [5].

**Can an IBS flare-up last weeks?** Yes, for some people. But a "flare" lasting weeks that feels different from your usual IBS is exactly the situation where a doctor should take another look, rather than you waiting it out.

## What causes an IBS flare-up?

Flares usually come from one trigger, or several small ones stacking up, acting on a gut that's more sensitive than average. The NHS names alcohol, caffeine, spicy or fatty food, stress and anxiety, and regular antibiotic use as possible triggers, and adds that flares can happen for no obvious reason [1]. NIDDK notes that women with IBS often have more symptoms during their periods [3].

| Trigger type | Examples | Typical lag before symptoms (varies by person) |
|---|---|---|
| Meal-related | Large or fatty meals, alcohol, caffeine, high-FODMAP foods, eating fast or skipping meals [1][6] | Minutes to a day or two |
| Stress and mood | Deadlines, conflict, travel, anxiety about symptoms [5] | Same day, often later that day |
| Hormonal | The days just before and during a period [3] | Follows the cycle |
| Sleep and routine | Short nights, shift changes, irregular meals | Usually the next day or two |
| Illness and medicines | Gut infections, antibiotics [1] | Days to weeks |

A 2026 study had 357 adults with IBS rate their stress and symptoms three times a day for a week. Moments of higher-than-usual stress went with worse symptoms, and afternoon stress predicted worse evening symptoms [5]. That's useful for tracking: the lag between a stressor and symptoms can be hours, not days.

### Why does my IBS flare up "randomly"?

Flares often feel random because the cause is spread across several days and several small things. A single coffee might be fine. Coffee plus a short night plus a takeout dinner plus the day before your period might not be. Your memory tends to blame the last meal, which is rarely the whole story. A written log is how you see the stack.

## How to track an IBS flare-up (the 72-hour look-back)

The most useful way to track a flare is to define it in advance, log a few simple things every day, and then review the 72 hours before each flare starts. Logging only on bad days misses the build-up. Our [IBS food diary guide](/resources/ibs-food-diary) covers daily logging in detail. Here's the flare-specific layer.

### Step 1: Write your personal flare definition

Pick one rule and keep it for at least two months. For example:

- "Pain of 6/10 or worse on two days in a row", or
- "Three or more loose stools (Bristol type 6–7) in a day", or
- "No bowel movement for three days plus bloating I'd rate 7/10 or worse."

A fixed definition stops a mild day from counting as a flare one week and not the next.

### Step 2: Log five things daily (about two minutes)

1. Meals and drinks, with rough times and portions.
2. Each bowel movement, with its Bristol stool type.
3. Your worst pain and bloating, rated 0–10.
4. Context: sleep hours, a 0–3 stress rating, period days, alcohol, exercise, new medicines.
5. A one-line note on anything unusual (travel, a big event, eating out).

### Step 3: When a flare starts, run the look-back

Look at the 72 hours before day one of the flare and fill in this worksheet:

| Look-back question | Day −3 | Day −2 | Day −1 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Anything eaten that you rarely eat? | | | |
| Large, late or high-fat meal? | | | |
| Alcohol or more caffeine than usual? | | | |
| Sleep under your usual hours? | | | |
| Stress 2–3 out of 3? | | | |
| Period due or started? | | | |
| Missed or rushed meals? | | | |
| Illness, travel or new medicine? | | | |

### Worked example (illustrative data)

The table below is invented to show the method. It isn't real patient data.

| Day | Food and drink highlights | Stool (Bristol) | Worst pain (0–10) | Sleep | Stress (0–3) | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Thu (−3) | Usual meals, 2 coffees | 4 | 2 | 7.5 h | 1 | — |
| Fri (−2) | Work lunch, 3 coffees, wine with dinner | 5 | 3 | 6 h | 3 | Project deadline |
| Sat (−1) | Late brunch, large takeout curry | 6, 6 | 4 | 5.5 h | 2 | Period due Monday |
| Sun (flare day 1) | Toast, rice, broth | 7, 6, 6 | 7 | 7 h | 2 | Cramping, urgency |
| Mon (flare day 2) | Plain meals | 6, 5 | 6 | 7 h | 2 | Period started |
| Tue | Usual meals | 5 | 3 | 7.5 h | 1 | Easing |

The obvious suspect is the curry. But the look-back shows four other things in the same 48 hours: a deadline, short sleep, extra caffeine and alcohol, and a period due. One flare can't tell you which mattered. If three of your next four flares also follow short sleep and high stress, but the curry shows up only once, that's a more useful lead. It's a possible pattern to test and discuss with your clinician, not proof.

### Step 4: Compare flares, not days

After three or four flares, put their look-back worksheets side by side and count how often each factor appears. Then compare that with how often the same factor shows up in weeks *without* a flare. A factor that appears before every flare but also on plenty of calm days is probably background noise.

## When to see a doctor

See a doctor if your flares are getting more frequent or severe, or if a "flare" doesn't look like your usual IBS. Get prompt or urgent care if you notice any of these alongside bowel symptoms [1][2][4][7]:

- blood in your stool, bleeding from your bottom, or black, tarry stool
- unexplained weight loss
- fever or vomiting
- diarrhea or pain that wakes you up at night
- severe belly pain that doesn't ease after a bowel movement or passing gas
- signs of anemia, such as unusual tiredness, shortness of breath or pale skin
- a hard lump or swelling in your belly
- symptoms that started after age 50, or a family history of colon cancer, inflammatory bowel disease or celiac disease

These aren't typical of IBS and need checking. Our guide to [when a change in bowel habits needs a doctor](/blog/when-to-see-doctor-bowel-changes) goes through them in more detail.

## FAQ

### How do I calm an IBS flare-up fast?

There's no instant fix. Gentle steps that are reasonable to try include smaller, regular meals instead of skipping food, steady fluids, and easing off alcohol, caffeine and fatty or spicy food for a few days [6]. The ACG guideline suggests peppermint oil for overall IBS symptoms [4]. Ask a pharmacist about over-the-counter options that suit your symptoms, especially if you take other medicines.

### Is it normal to have IBS symptoms every day?

Some people with IBS do have symptoms most days, with flares on top. Still, daily symptoms that are new for you, getting worse, or affecting sleep, work or eating are worth a medical review. A treatment plan may help more than you'd expect.

### Can an IBS flare-up cause nausea or back pain?

Yes, both can come with IBS. The NHS lists nausea and backache among other IBS symptoms [1]. Vomiting, fever or severe pain is different, and you should get it checked.

### Should I go to the ER for an IBS flare-up?

Most flares don't need emergency care. Go urgently if you have heavy bleeding, black stools, severe pain that won't ease, a high fever, signs of dehydration, or pain together with persistent vomiting. If you're unsure, call your doctor's office or an urgent advice line.

### How often do IBS flare-ups happen?

It varies a lot. Some people flare a few times a year, others several times a month. Your own log is the only reliable answer, which is one more reason to keep a consistent flare definition.
