> **Quick answer:** A food intolerance is a non-allergic reaction to a specific food, such as lactose. It tends to follow that food and get worse with bigger portions. IBS is a gut-brain disorder with recurring belly pain linked to bowel changes. Its symptoms rise and fall with many things, including stress, hormones and sleep as well as food. You can have both, and they're easy to confuse. A doctor sorts them out with your history, sometimes breath or blood tests, and a structured food log.

"Is it IBS, or am I just intolerant to something?" is one of the most common questions people ask about their gut. Fair enough: bloating, gas, cramps and loose stools look the same either way.

But the two aren't the same thing, and the answer changes what helps. If it's a single intolerance, cutting back one food may fix most of it. If it's IBS, food is usually one lever among several. This guide explains the difference, shows what each tends to look like in a food diary, and covers the tests your doctor might use.

**Key takeaways**
- A food intolerance is a non-immune reaction to a food, often dose-dependent. A food allergy is an immune reaction and usually comes on fast [1][2].
- IBS is diagnosed from a pattern of belly pain plus bowel changes over months. It isn't defined by any one food [1][5].
- Overlap is common. One review reports that 85–90% of people with IBS say certain foods make symptoms worse [2], and people with IBS report lactose intolerance more often than others [3].
- Blood "food sensitivity" (IgG) panels aren't validated for IBS [1]. Breath tests and celiac blood tests, ordered by a clinician, are more useful.

## What's the difference between IBS and a food intolerance?

The core difference is what drives the symptoms. In food intolerance, a specific food or food component sets off the reaction. In IBS, the gut itself is more sensitive and reactive, so many inputs can set off symptoms.

| | Food intolerance | IBS | Food allergy (for comparison) |
|---|---|---|---|
| What it is | A non-immune reaction to a food or food component [1] | A disorder of gut-brain interaction with recurring belly pain and bowel changes [4] | An immune reaction (often IgE) to a food protein [1] |
| Typical trigger | One food or group (lactose, fructose, certain additives) [1] | Many: food, stress, hormones, sleep, infections [7] | A specific food protein |
| Dose effect | Often clear: small amounts OK, large amounts not [2] | Present for some foods, but symptoms also vary without food changes | Even tiny amounts can react |
| Timing | Usually hours after eating | Can come and go over days or weeks | Rapid, usually within minutes [1] |
| Core symptom | Gas, bloating, diarrhea, discomfort after the food | Belly pain at least weekly, linked to bowel movements, for months [5] | Hives, swelling, vomiting, breathing trouble |
| How it's assessed | Elimination and rechallenge, sometimes breath tests [2] | Clinical diagnosis using symptom criteria, plus limited tests [1][5] | Allergist history plus testing |

The ACG guideline lists several causes of food intolerance: natural chemicals in food (such as caffeine or capsaicin), enzyme shortfalls (such as lactase), sugar-transport problems (such as with fructose), and functional gut disorders [1]. That last one is the overlap. IBS itself can make you react to foods.

## Can food intolerance cause IBS, or be mistaken for it?

Both happen. A food intolerance can mimic IBS. And IBS can make ordinary foods feel like intolerances. Here are the main overlaps.

**Lactose.** Lactose intolerance is often mistaken for IBS because the symptoms overlap so much [2]. A meta-analysis found that people with IBS were about 3.5 times as likely as healthy people to *report* lactose intolerance and more likely to have symptoms on testing. Yet they were no more likely to have lactose maldigestion itself [3]. In other words, the IBS gut can react more to the same amount of lactose. A hydrogen breath test is the most reliable way to check [2].

**Fructose.** Fructose malabsorption has been reported in about 22% of people with IBS, more than in the general population [2].

**Gluten and wheat.** Some people with IBS feel worse after gluten without having celiac disease. The ACG notes this may often be a reaction to fructans, a FODMAP in wheat, rather than to gluten itself [1]. Celiac disease is different again. It's an immune disease that damages the gut and needs proper testing [1][6].

**FODMAPs in general.** Fermentable carbs cause gas and draw water into the gut in everyone. In IBS, the gut is more sensitive to that stretch, which is why a low-FODMAP diet helps many people [1]. That doesn't mean you're "intolerant" to every FODMAP. Reintroduction usually shows you tolerate several of them (see our [low-FODMAP reintroduction tracker](/blog/low-fodmap-reintroduction-tracking)).

**Reported vs reproducible reactions.** The ACG notes that up to 20% of people in the general population report adverse reactions to food, but only 2–3% have recurrent symptoms when the food is reintroduced under testing conditions [1]. A careful rechallenge matters.

## What do IBS and food intolerance look like in a food log?

A two-week food and symptom log often shows different patterns, though it can't diagnose anything on its own. The two summaries below are **illustrative**: invented to show the patterns, not real people.

| What the log shows | Pattern A: suggests a single-food intolerance | Pattern B: suggests IBS-type variability |
|---|---|---|
| Bad days (pain or bloating 6/10+) | 5 of 14 | 6 of 14 |
| Shared factor on bad days | Milky coffee or ice cream within ~2–6 h on 5 of 5 | No single food. Short sleep on 4, high stress on 4, period on 2, big takeout meal on 3 |
| Same food on good days? | Milk only in small amounts (a splash in tea) | Yes, most "suspect" foods also eaten on calm days |
| Dose effect | Bigger dairy portions mean worse symptoms | Inconsistent |
| Belly pain between meals or at random times? | Rarely | Often, including pain relieved by a bowel movement |
| Stool | Loose stools only on dairy days | Mix of hard and loose stools across the fortnight |

Pattern A is a good reason to ask your doctor about lactose testing or a supervised lactose-free trial. Pattern B is more typical of IBS, where food is one input among several. Plenty of people show a bit of both. For example, someone with IBS might also have a clear lactose problem. ## How do doctors tell them apart?

Doctors combine your history with a small number of targeted tests. There's no single blood test for either IBS or "food intolerance".

- **Symptom pattern.** IBS is diagnosed when belly pain recurs on average at least once a week for 3 months, linked to bowel movements or changes in stool frequency or form, with symptoms starting at least 6 months earlier, and nothing suggests another condition [1][5]. Our guide to [how IBS is diagnosed](/blog/how-is-ibs-diagnosed) covers this in full.
- **Celiac blood test.** The ACG recommends celiac testing for people with IBS and diarrhea symptoms [1]. Keep eating gluten until you're tested, because going gluten-free first can affect the results [6].
- **Breath tests.** These are used for lactose (and sometimes fructose) malabsorption [2].
- **What the ACG advises against.** Routine food allergy testing in IBS isn't recommended unless symptoms suggest a true allergy. Serum IgG food panels haven't been validated and can't be recommended [1]. Our guide on whether [food intolerance tests are worth it](/blog/food-intolerance-test-vs-food-diary) compares them with a food diary.

Please don't self-diagnose either one. A tidy log helps your clinician, but it can't rule out celiac disease, inflammatory bowel disease or other causes.

## How to track your symptoms to help sort it out

A useful log for this question has three features:

1. **Times on everything.** Note when you eat, when symptoms start and when you have bowel movements, so a 2–6 hour lag stands out.
2. **Portions.** A splash of milk and a milkshake are very different tests.
3. **Non-food context.** Sleep, stress, period days, alcohol and illness. Without these, every bad day gets blamed on the last meal.

## When to see a doctor

See a doctor before cutting out foods long-term, and promptly if you have [1][5]:

- blood in your stool or black, tarry stool
- unexplained weight loss or signs of anemia
- diarrhea that wakes you at night, fever or vomiting
- symptoms that began after 50, or a family history of colon cancer, inflammatory bowel disease or celiac disease
- fast reactions after eating, such as hives, swelling of the lips or throat, or trouble breathing (get emergency care if severe)

## FAQ

### Can a food intolerance turn into IBS?

Not directly. But they can coexist, and some gut infections can trigger both new food sensitivities and IBS. The ACG notes that Giardia infection is linked to food intolerances up to 3 years later [1]. If your symptoms started after a stomach bug, tell your doctor.

### Is it IBS or lactose intolerance?

It can be either, or both. If symptoms reliably follow larger servings of milk-based foods and settle on a lactose-free diet, lactose is likely involved. A breath test can confirm it [2]. If you still have pain and bowel changes on lactose-free days, IBS may also be part of the picture.

### Is it IBS or gluten intolerance?

Rule out celiac disease first, while you're still eating gluten [6]. If celiac tests are negative and wheat bothers you, fructans (a FODMAP) may be the cause rather than gluten itself [1]. A dietitian-guided FODMAP reintroduction can test this.

### Will a food sensitivity blood test tell me which foods cause my IBS?

The ACG says IgG food panels haven't been validated and can't be recommended [1]. They can also produce long lists of "reactive" foods that lead to needless restriction. A structured log and a supervised elimination and rechallenge are more informative.
