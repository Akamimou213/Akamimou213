> **Quick answer:** Yes, IBS can appear suddenly, most often after a bout of gastroenteritis (a "stomach bug" or food poisoning). This is called post-infection IBS, and it develops in roughly 1 in 10 people after infectious enteritis. IBS can also start later in life, but it's less common after 50. Sudden new bowel symptoms, especially after 50, need a doctor's check first to rule out other causes before anyone calls it IBS.

"I was fine my whole life, then one day my gut just changed." It's one of the most common stories people tell about IBS. Often it starts with a stomach bug, a trip abroad or a round of antibiotics. Sometimes there's no obvious trigger at all.

IBS really can arrive abruptly. But "sudden" is also exactly the situation where doctors want to look for other causes first. This guide explains how post-infection IBS works, why age matters, and what to write down from day one so your doctor has a clear history.

**Key takeaways**
- About 10% of people develop IBS within a year of infectious enteritis, and the risk is about 4 times higher than in people without such an infection [1].
- Risk is higher in women and after more severe infections, antibiotic use, and anxiety or depression at the time [1][2].
- Post-infection IBS often improves over time, though it can last for years [2].
- New bowel symptoms after 50, or with blood, weight loss or night-time symptoms, should be checked by a doctor before being labeled IBS [3][5].

## Can IBS appear suddenly?

Yes. The clearest example is post-infection IBS, where symptoms start right after a gut infection clears up [2]. Other people notice IBS building over a few weeks during a stressful period or after a medicine change, and it can feel sudden in hindsight.

There's a catch in the definition, though. Under the Rome IV criteria, IBS is diagnosed when belly pain has recurred on average at least once a week for the last 3 months, linked to bowel movements or to changes in stool frequency or form, with symptoms first starting at least 6 months before diagnosis [7]. NIDDK notes that a doctor may still diagnose IBS when symptoms have lasted a shorter time [5]. In other words, a week or two of new symptoms isn't IBS yet. It's a change that needs explaining.

## What is post-infection IBS?

Post-infection IBS (PI-IBS) is IBS that develops immediately after an episode of infectious gastroenteritis, in someone who didn't have IBS before [2]. The Rome Foundation's working team describes it as meeting the usual IBS criteria, plus symptoms starting right after the acute infection resolves [2]. It notes that remembering the exact date your symptoms began can itself be a clue [2].

**How common is it?** A meta-analysis of 45 studies covering more than 21,000 people with infectious enteritis found [1]:

| Finding | Figure |
|---|---|
| IBS at 12 months after infection | 10.1% |
| IBS more than 12 months after infection | 14.5% |
| Risk vs people without infection (first 12 months) | about 4.2 times higher |
| After parasite or protozoa infections (e.g., Giardia) | 41.9% developed IBS |
| After bacterial infections | 13.8% developed IBS |

**Who's more at risk?** Women, people who took antibiotics during the infection, people with anxiety, depression or other psychological distress, and those with more severe infections [1][2]. The ACG guideline also notes that Giardia is the most common such parasite in the US. Testing for it makes sense if you have risk factors like recent travel, untreated water, camping or daycare exposure [3].

**What subtype is it?** Studies most often report a mixed (IBS-M) pattern after infection, followed by diarrhea-predominant IBS (IBS-D) [2].

**Does it go away?** Often, gradually. The Rome working team found the risk of IBS after bacterial infection usually declines over time, and advises reassuring patients that symptoms may improve or resolve, particularly after viral infections like norovirus [2]. For some people, though, symptoms last for years [2].

## Can IBS appear later in life?

It can, but it's less common. NIDDK says people younger than 50 are more likely to develop IBS than people older than 50 [4]. UCLA Health notes that IBS generally occurs before age 50 and it's uncommon to develop it later in life [6].

That's why age changes the approach. The ACG guideline lists older age at symptom onset as an alarm feature, alongside bleeding, black stools, unintentional weight loss and a family history of inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) or colon cancer [3]. It also says colon cancer screening should be up to date based on your age, regardless of IBS symptoms [3].

If you're over 45 and haven't had colon cancer screening, new bowel symptoms are a good moment to raise it. The ACG guideline also notes that women over 60 with new diarrhea have a higher risk of microscopic colitis, a condition that can look like IBS-D but is diagnosed with biopsies during colonoscopy [3].

## What else can cause sudden bowel changes?

Several conditions can start suddenly and look like IBS. Your doctor will think about these, and some need tests:

| Possible cause | Clues a doctor may look for |
|---|---|
| Ongoing infection (e.g., Giardia) | Travel, untreated water, exposure to someone who's sick [3] |
| Celiac disease | Diarrhea, bloating, anemia, family history. The ACG recommends a celiac blood test when IBS comes with diarrhea [3] |
| Inflammatory bowel disease | Blood or mucus, night-time diarrhea, weight loss, raised inflammation markers [3] |
| Microscopic colitis | Watery diarrhea, especially in women over 60 [3] |
| Medicines | A new drug, antibiotic or supplement shortly before symptoms began |
| Colorectal cancer | Bleeding, black stools, anemia, weight loss, change in bowel habit, especially over 45–50 [3] |
| Food intolerance | Symptoms that reliably follow a specific food. See [IBS vs food intolerance](/blog/ibs-vs-food-intolerance) |

This list isn't for self-diagnosis. It's why "sudden IBS" deserves a proper assessment. For people under 45 with no warning signs, the ACG doesn't recommend routine colonoscopy for IBS symptoms, and favors a positive, symptom-based diagnosis with limited tests [3]. Our guide to [how IBS is diagnosed](/blog/how-is-ibs-diagnosed) explains that process.

## What to record from the day symptoms start

The most useful thing you can bring to a doctor is a clear timeline. Memory blurs within weeks, and onset details are exactly what helps tell post-infection IBS from other causes.

**Onset worksheet** (fill in once):

| Question | Your answer |
|---|---|
| Date symptoms started (as exact as possible) | |
| Illness in the 2 weeks before? (fever, vomiting, diarrhea, others sick) | |
| Travel, camping, untreated water, new daycare contact? | |
| Antibiotics or new medicines in the past 3 months? | |
| Bowel habit before vs now (frequency, Bristol type) | |
| Any blood, black stools, weight loss, night-time symptoms? | |
| Family history of colon cancer, IBD or celiac disease? | |
| Date of last colon cancer screening (if 45+) | |

**Then log daily for 2–4 weeks:** bowel movements by Bristol type, worst pain (0–10) and whether it eases after a bowel movement, meals with times, and notes on sleep, stress and medicines.

**Illustrative example** (invented to show the method):

| Week | Context | Typical Bristol types | Pain days per week | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Week 0 | Food poisoning: vomiting, fever, diarrhea for 3 days | 7 | — | Recovered by day 4 |
| Week 2 | Back at work | 5–6, some 2 | 3 | Cramps ease after bowel movements |
| Week 6 | — | 6, some 1–2 | 2 | No blood, weight stable |
| Week 12 | — | Mix of 2 and 6 | 2 | Still present, booked a GP visit |

A timeline like this, with a dated infection, no red flags and a mixed pattern that persists, gives a clinician much more to work with than "my stomach's been off since spring".

## When to see a doctor

Any new, persistent change in your bowel habits deserves a doctor's visit. The NHS suggests seeing a GP if you think you have IBS and symptoms have lasted more than 4 weeks [8]. Seek care promptly if you have [3][5][8]:

- blood in your stool, or black, tarry stool
- unexplained weight loss
- anemia, or symptoms like unusual tiredness, shortness of breath or pale skin
- diarrhea or pain that wakes you at night
- fever, or symptoms that began with a severe or prolonged infection
- a lump or swelling in your belly
- new symptoms after age 50, or a family history of colon cancer, IBD or celiac disease

## FAQ

### Can IBS appear out of nowhere?

It can seem that way. Often there's a trigger in the background, such as a gut infection, antibiotics, or a stressful stretch of life [1][4]. A timeline from the start of symptoms makes those links easier to spot.

### Can IBS appear at any age?

IBS can start at any age, including in childhood, but it's most likely to begin before 50 [4][6]. New symptoms later in life call for a check for other causes first.

### How long does post-infectious IBS last?

It varies. The risk tends to fall over time and many people improve, but some studies followed people with symptoms for 8 years or longer [2]. Your doctor can suggest treatments while you wait for it to settle.

### Can antibiotics cause IBS?

Antibiotic use during a gut infection is linked to a higher risk of developing post-infection IBS [1]. That doesn't mean you should avoid antibiotics you need. Just mention any recent courses to your doctor.

### Can a food intolerance start suddenly too?

Yes, new food sensitivities can appear after gut infections. The ACG notes Giardia infection has been linked to food intolerances up to 3 years later [3].
