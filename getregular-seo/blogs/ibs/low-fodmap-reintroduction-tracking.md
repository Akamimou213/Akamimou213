> **Quick answer:** A low-FODMAP reintroduction tracker should record, for each challenge day, the single test food, the serving size, the time you ate it, and your symptoms and stool for the rest of that day and the next. Monash University's method tests one FODMAP group at a time over 3 days with increasing servings, keeps the rest of your diet low-FODMAP, and takes a 2–3 day break between challenges. Review the log with a FODMAP-trained dietitian.

The elimination step of the low-FODMAP diet gets all the attention. The reintroduction step is where you actually learn something, and it's where many people stall. It takes weeks, the rules are fiddly, and a bad night's sleep or a stressful day can make a challenge look like a failure.

A clear log fixes most of that. This guide shows what to record, how to lay out each challenge, and how to spot results that need a retest. It assumes you're following a plan from a dietitian, which is how Monash University and the American College of Gastroenterology recommend doing it.

**Key takeaways**
- The low-FODMAP diet has three steps: a short low-FODMAP phase (2–6 weeks), reintroduction, then a personalized long-term diet [1][3].
- Reintroduction tests one FODMAP group at a time, usually over 3 days with increasing servings, with a 2–3 day washout or until symptoms settle [2].
- The strict phase is meant to be short-term. Long-term over-restriction may cause nutritional problems, so the ACG recommends a trained GI dietitian [5].
- A good log records dose, timing, symptoms, stool and confounders (stress, sleep, period, illness) so you and your dietitian can tell a real reaction from a bad day.

## How does low-FODMAP reintroduction work?

Low-FODMAP reintroduction is the second of three steps. You add back foods rich in one FODMAP group at a time to see which ones you tolerate. FODMAPs are fermentable carbohydrates: Fermentable Oligosaccharides, Disaccharides, Monosaccharides And Polyols [3].

| Step | What happens | Typical length | Goal |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Low FODMAP | Swap high-FODMAP foods for low-FODMAP alternatives | 2–6 weeks [1][3] | Get symptoms under control |
| 2. Reintroduction | Stay low-FODMAP in the background while challenging one FODMAP group at a time | About 6–8 weeks per Monash's 2020 guidance; Monash's 2026 info sheet says 8–12 weeks [2][3] | Find which FODMAPs you tolerate |
| 3. Personalization | Bring back tolerated foods; limit poorly tolerated ones only as much as needed | Long-term [3][4] | The least restrictive diet that keeps symptoms in check |

Monash lists six groups to challenge separately: fructose, lactose, sorbitol, mannitol, fructans and GOS (galacto-oligosaccharides) [3]. If symptoms didn't improve in Step 1, Monash notes that your IBS may not be FODMAP-sensitive and other therapies may be needed [1][4]. Not every food reaction is about FODMAPs either. Our guide to [IBS vs food intolerance](/blog/ibs-vs-food-intolerance) covers lactose, celiac disease and other causes.

Two points are easy to miss:

- **Step 1 is short on purpose.** Monash says a low-FODMAP diet "is not an elimination diet" in the cut-everything-out sense, because foods are swapped or eaten in low-FODMAP portions [1]. The ACG guideline recommends only a *limited* trial, notes that long-term over-restriction may lead to micronutrient deficiencies, and says the diet's complexity calls for a properly trained GI dietitian [5].
- **The evidence is real but not perfect.** The ACG rates its low-FODMAP recommendation as conditional, based on very low-quality evidence [5]. It works for many people, not everyone.

If a dietitian isn't available to you, say so to your doctor. The ACG notes that clinicians should then provide high-quality teaching materials so you can do the diet in a medically responsible way [5].

## What should a FODMAP reintroduction diary record?

A FODMAP reintroduction diary should record enough to answer one question for each challenge: did symptoms rise with the dose, beyond your normal day-to-day variation? Monash suggests recording challenge foods, doses and symptom responses, on paper or in its app's diary [2].

**Daily fields to log (template):**

| Field | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| Date and challenge day (1, 2, 3, or washout) | Keeps the sequence clear |
| FODMAP group being tested | One group at a time [2] |
| Test food and serving size, as set by your dietitian | Reactions are often dose-dependent [7] |
| Time eaten | Lets you line symptoms up with the dose |
| Rest of the day's meals (low-FODMAP?) | A slip-up muddies the result |
| Worst pain, bloating, gas and urgency (0–10 each) | Symptoms often differ by FODMAP group |
| Bowel movements and Bristol type | Shows diarrhea or constipation shifts |
| Confounders: sleep, stress, period, illness, travel, new medicines | Explains "false positives" |
| Notes | Anything odd, such as eating out |

Monash's practical guidance: wait until symptoms are well controlled before starting each challenge, eat the test food at about the same time each day, and keep the rest of your diet low-FODMAP throughout [2].

## Example: one fructose challenge, logged

The table below is **illustrative**. It shows how a filled log looks. Serving sizes are written as your dietitian might assign them (moderate, high, higher). We don't give gram amounts here, because the right test foods and portions should come from your dietitian or the Monash FODMAP app. Honey is used because Monash lists it as a high-FODMAP food [3].

| Day | Test food and serving | Time | Worst symptoms (0–10) | Stool (Bristol) | Confounders | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Baseline (2 days before) | none | — | Bloating 2, pain 1 | 4 | Normal week | Symptoms settled |
| Challenge day 1 | Honey, moderate serve | 8:00 | Bloating 2, pain 1 | 4 | — | No change |
| Challenge day 2 | Honey, high serve | 8:00 | Bloating 4, gas 4 | 5 | — | Mild gurgling, afternoon |
| Challenge day 3 | Honey, higher serve | 8:00 | Bloating 6, pain 4, urgency 5 | 6, 6 | Slept 6 h | Loose stools by midday |
| Washout day 1 | none (low-FODMAP) | — | Bloating 4, pain 2 | 5 | — | Settling |
| Washout day 2 | none | — | Bloating 2, pain 1 | 4 | — | Back to baseline |

What this example suggests: the moderate serve was fine, symptoms rose with the dose, and they settled within two washout days. A dietitian might read this as "fructose tolerated in moderate amounts". The short sleep on day 3 is noted, but symptoms were already climbing on day 2, so it's less likely to be the whole story. Only after this settles do you start the next group.

## How to read your challenge results

Read each challenge against your own baseline, not against a symptom-free ideal. Monash doesn't give a single numeric cut-off for a "reaction". Its guidance is to record your responses and work through them with your dietitian [2]. These are useful questions to bring to that conversation:

| What your log shows | Possible reading | Sensible next step to discuss |
|---|---|---|
| No change on all 3 days | Likely tolerated, at least at those amounts | Reintroduce foods from this group in Step 3 |
| Symptoms only at the highest serve | Tolerated in smaller amounts | Keep portions moderate |
| Symptoms from day 1 | Possibly sensitive to this group | Stop the challenge and wait for symptoms to settle. Consider a retest later |
| Symptoms with a big confounder (period, illness, stressful event) | Result unclear | Repeat the challenge in a calmer week |
| Symptoms that don't settle after several washout days | Something else may be going on | Pause and talk to your dietitian or doctor |

Monash also notes that FODMAP tolerance can change over time, so poorly tolerated foods are worth retesting after a few months [1][4]. A reaction now isn't a lifetime ban.

## When to see a doctor

Low-FODMAP is a diet for IBS that a clinician has diagnosed. Monash describes it as a diet for "medically diagnosed" IBS [4]. Before starting or continuing it, see a doctor if you have [5][6]:

- blood in your stool, or black stool
- unexplained weight loss, or weight loss during the diet
- diarrhea that wakes you at night, fever or vomiting
- symptoms that started after 50, or a family history of colon cancer, inflammatory bowel disease or celiac disease
- a growing list of foods you feel you have to avoid, or worry and fear around eating

Talk to your doctor before cutting out gluten. Doctors don't recommend starting a gluten-free diet before celiac testing, because it can affect the results [8].

## FAQ

### How long does FODMAP reintroduction take?

Monash's 2020 guidance says most people take about 6–8 weeks [2]. Its 2026 info sheet gives 8–12 weeks [3]. The washout days, retests and life getting in the way all add time, which is normal.

### Can I do the low-FODMAP diet without a dietitian?

Many people try, and many describe getting stuck in the strict phase for months. Monash and the ACG both recommend a trained dietitian [1][5]. If that isn't possible, use high-quality materials such as Monash's and keep Step 1 short.

### Can low FODMAP make IBS worse?

It can backfire if it's done too long or too strictly. The ACG notes possible micronutrient deficiencies with long-term over-restriction [5]. Restriction can also make eating stressful. That's why reintroduction and personalization matter as much as elimination. For non-FODMAP suspects like coffee or fatty meals, see our guide to [IBS trigger foods](/blog/ibs-trigger-foods).

### Do I need a FODMAP tracking app?

No. Paper works. What matters is logging dose, timing, symptoms and confounders the same way every day. For FODMAP content of foods, use a dedicated FODMAP resource such as the Monash app. A general food-symptom tracker is for logging, not for looking up FODMAPs.
