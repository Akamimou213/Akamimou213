**Key takeaways**
- The Bristol Stool Form Scale was developed at the Bristol Royal Infirmary and published in 1997. It's used because stool form reflects how fast material moves through the gut [1].
- Types 3–4 are generally considered ideal, 1–2 suggest constipation and 5–7 suggest diarrhea [2].
- A chart on its own describes one stool. Two weeks of logging describes *you*.
- The summary page turns 14 days into four numbers that a clinician can scan in under a minute.

## The 7 Bristol stool types at a glance

This is the reference that appears on page 1. The descriptions below are written in our own words. The scale itself comes from Lewis and Heaton's 1997 study, which found stool form correlated with whole-gut transit time better than stool frequency or weight did [1].

| Type | What it looks like | Usual reading [2] |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Separate hard lumps, like pebbles; hard to pass | Constipation (slow transit) |
| 2 | Sausage-shaped but lumpy | Constipation |
| 3 | Sausage-shaped with cracks on the surface | Ideal |
| 4 | Smooth, soft, like a snake | Ideal |
| 5 | Soft blobs with clear edges; passes easily | Toward loose |
| 6 | Mushy, fluffy pieces with ragged edges | Loose |
| 7 | Watery, no solid pieces | Liquid / diarrhea |

Sources don't all agree on type 5. Cleveland Clinic groups it with 6 and 7 [2], while a validation study treated types 3–5 as the "normal" band [3]. If you're unsure, write down what you see. The descriptions do the interpreting later.

**A practical tip:** people misclassify the "border" types most often. In that same study, fewer than 80% of type 2, 3, 5 and 6 models were classified correctly [3]. Read the words, not just the pictures. If you're split between two types, pick one and add a "?" note.

For a closer look at what "healthy" means, see [what healthy poop looks like](/blog/what-does-healthy-poop-look-like).

## How do you fill in the 14-day log?

Fill in one row each time you go, right after you go. It takes about 20 seconds.

1. **Date and time.** Rounded to the nearest 15 minutes is fine.
2. **Type (1–7).** If it's mixed, write the main type and "M".
3. **Effort.** E = easy, S = some straining, H = hard straining.
4. **Urgency.** N = none, H = had to hurry, B = barely made it.
5. **Notes.** Only if something's unusual: color other than brown, blood, mucus, floating or greasy, pain, "not fully empty".
6. **No BM today?** Tick the box. Don't leave the day blank.

| 1 | 07:30 | 4 | E | N | — |

| 5 | 07:15 | 4 | E | N | — |
| 6 | 12:40 | 6 | E | H | After takeout lunch; cramps |

## How do you total it up after 14 days?

On day 14, fill in the summary box on page 3. These four numbers are your baseline:

| Summary line | How to calculate | What it tells you |
|---|---|---|
| **Bowel movements per week** | Total BMs ÷ 2 | Frequency. Most adults fall between 3 a day and 3 a week [5] |
| **% in types 3–4** | (Type 3 + type 4 count) ÷ total BMs × 100 | How much of the time your stool is in the ideal range |
| **Hard days / loose days** | Days with any type 1–2; days with any type 6–7 | Which way you lean, if at all |
| **No-BM days** | Count of ticked boxes | Long gaps matter more than a single skipped day |

**Reading it (general guide, not a diagnosis):**
- Fewer than three bowel movements a week, or regular hard, lumpy or hard-to-pass stools, fits the usual description of constipation [6].
- Loose, watery stools three or more times a day, or more often than is normal for you, fits the definition of diarrhea [7].
- A mixed picture (hard days and loose days in the same two weeks) is worth mentioning to a doctor, especially with pain or bloating.

The summary page also has space for **three questions for your doctor**. Write them down as they come up during the two weeks.

## When to see a doctor

The red-flag checklist on page 3 covers these. Don't finish the 14 days first if you notice:

- Blood in your stool, or stool that looks black or very dark red [8]
- Pale, clay-colored or white stools for more than a few days, especially with yellow skin or eyes or dark urine [9]
- Bloody diarrhea, or bleeding that won't stop [8]
- A change in your usual bowel habit that lasts more than a couple of weeks
- Unintended weight loss, constant tiredness or breathlessness [8]
- Diarrhea that wakes you up, fever, or severe pain
- New symptoms after age 50, or a family history of bowel cancer, IBD or celiac disease

## FAQ

**Is the Bristol stool chart free to use?**
The scale is widely reproduced for patient education, and many health organizations offer free downloads. Ours uses original illustrations and our own wording of each description, with credit to the original 1997 study [1].

**Can I use the chart for my child?**
Children's clinicians often use a modified version with five types instead of seven [2]. Ask your child's pediatrician which version they prefer.

**What paper size is it?**
It comes in US Letter and A4. It's designed to stay readable in black and white.

**Should I bring the log to my appointment?**
Yes. Bring the summary page at least. It's quicker for a clinician to scan than 14 rows, and you can still hand over the full log if they want detail.

**How is this different from other bowel diaries?**
