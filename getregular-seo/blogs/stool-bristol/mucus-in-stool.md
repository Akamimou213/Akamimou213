> **Quick answer:** A small amount of clear or whitish mucus in your stool can be normal. Mucus lubricates the bowel and protects its lining. Bowel movements with white mucus are common with IBS and constipation. See a doctor if the mucus has blood in it, is yellowish or off-white in large amounts, or comes with diarrhea lasting more than three days, abdominal pain, cramps, vomiting or unexplained weight loss.

**Get medical advice promptly if you have mucus with any of these:** blood (red streaks, red or black stool), fever, diarrhea that won't settle, severe or ongoing belly pain, unexplained weight loss, or a change in bowel habit that's lasting. Go to urgent care or the ER for heavy bleeding, large clots, black tarry stool with dizziness, or vomiting blood. [Red flags in full ↓](#when-should-you-worry-about-mucus-in-your-stool)

Seeing what looks like jelly or "snot" on your poop is unsettling. Most of the time, a little mucus is your gut doing its normal job. Sometimes it's a sign of inflammation that needs checking.

This guide explains what's normal, what bowel movements with white mucus usually mean, how to tell mucus from things that look similar, and exactly what to note before you call a doctor.

**Key takeaways**
- Some clear mucus in stool is normal. It helps waste move along and protects the colon lining [1].
- White mucus is a common IBS symptom, and constipation is one of the more common causes of mucus overall [1][3].
- Mucus that's bloody, yellowish or off-white in large amounts deserves a doctor's attention [1].
- Mucus alone doesn't mean you have a serious illness [1], but what comes *with* it matters a lot.
- A short log (color, amount, how often, stool type, other symptoms) makes your appointment far more productive.

## Is mucus in stool normal?

Yes, in small amounts. Mucus is a jelly-like substance made by the lining of your intestines. It helps food waste move through your large intestine and protects the lining from bacteria [1]. A little clear mucus in the toilet or on the paper is normal [1].

It becomes worth noticing when there's suddenly a lot more, it changes color, or it comes with other symptoms. When something irritates the lining of your large intestine, it releases more mucus than usual, which can end up in your stool [1].

## What do bowel movements with white mucus mean?

White or whitish mucus is most often linked to **IBS** or **constipation**. Cleveland Clinic lists white-colored mucus in stool as a common IBS symptom [1]. The NHS includes "pooing mucus" among IBS symptoms [3]. Sticky stool with visible mucus "is very common in people with irritable bowel syndrome," says a Cleveland Clinic gastroenterologist [2].

White or yellow mucus can also be a symptom of **ulcerative colitis**. In **Crohn's disease**, mucus may look like white or yellow streaks on the stool [1]. That's why the context matters: white mucus in someone with long-standing, stable IBS is different from white mucus that's new and comes with diarrhea, blood or weight loss.

If you have IBS and mucus tends to appear during flares, see [IBS flare-up tracking](/blog/ibs-flare-up-tracking) for how to log flares and spot what comes before them.

## What causes mucus in stool?

Mucus can come with a range of conditions, from common and minor to ones that need treatment [1][2][4]:

| Cause | What the mucus is often like | Other clues |
|---|---|---|
| **Constipation** | Clear or white, with hard stools | Straining, Bristol types 1–2 |
| **IBS** | White, sometimes sticky | Cramping that eases after a bowel movement, bloating, changing stool form [3] |
| **Hemorrhoids or anal fissure** | Mucus, sometimes with bright red blood on the paper | Itching, pain when passing stool [4] |
| **GI infection** (bacterial, viral, parasitic) | Increased mucus | Diarrhea, cramps, sometimes fever |
| **Ulcerative colitis** | White or yellow; may contain blood or pus | Urgency, diarrhea, blood |
| **Crohn's disease** | White or yellow streaks | Pain, diarrhea, weight loss |
| **Proctitis** (inflamed rectum) | Mucus, sometimes blood | Rectal discomfort, urgency |
| **Diverticulitis** | Increased mucus | Usually left-sided pain, fever |
| **Food intolerances** (for example, lactose) | Increased mucus | Symptoms after specific foods [2] |
| **Colorectal cancer** | Bloody or dark black mucus | Change in bowel habit, weight loss, anemia [1][5] |

Remember: mucus in stool "doesn't mean you have a serious illness" [1]. It means something is irritating the lining. The goal is to find out what.

## Is it mucus, or something else?

Several things get mistaken for mucus. Telling them apart helps you describe what you're seeing.

| What you see | Likely what it is | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Clear or white jelly or strings, on stool or paper | Mucus | Normal in small amounts [1] |
| Small white specks or bits | Often undigested food | Usually not a concern if occasional |
| Whole stool pale, white, gray or clay-colored | Not mucus; a stool color change | Can point to a bile duct, liver or pancreas problem; see a doctor if it lasts several days [8] |
| Pink or red jelly | Mucus with blood | See a doctor [1][4] |
| Greasy film or oil on the water | Possibly fat in stool | Worth checking if frequent; see the [poop color chart](/blog/poop-color-chart) for related color clues |

## What should you note before you call a doctor?

Write down a few specifics. They help a doctor decide how urgent this is and which tests make sense. Track for a week or two if you're not in the red-flag group. If you are, call now and bring whatever notes you have.

**Mucus notes checklist**

- ☐ **Color** of the mucus: clear, white, yellow, pink/red, dark
- ☐ **Amount:** a streak, a coating, or mostly mucus
- ☐ **How often:** how many bowel movements per week include it
- ☐ **Stool type** (Bristol 1–7) at the same time; see the [Bristol stool chart](/resources/bristol-stool-chart)
- ☐ **Blood:** none, on the paper, mixed in, or black stool
- ☐ **Pain:** where, and whether it eases after a bowel movement
- ☐ **Urgency or night-time** bowel movements
- ☐ **Fever, weight change, tiredness**
- ☐ **Recent** antibiotics, travel or new foods, medicines or supplements
- ☐ **Family history** of colorectal cancer, IBD or celiac disease

**Example week (illustrative data)**

| Day | Bristol type | Mucus | Blood | Other |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Mon | 2 | Clear streak | None | Straining |
| Tue | — | — | — | No BM |
| Wed | 2 | White coating | None | Bloated |
| Thu | 4 | None | None | — |
| Fri | 3 | Small, clear | None | — |

This pattern, with mucus showing up alongside hard stools and straining, fits constipation. Still, it's worth mentioning at a routine visit. Our guide on [how to describe your poop to a doctor](/blog/how-to-describe-poop-to-doctor) shows how to present a log like this in under a minute.

**What a doctor may do.** That depends on your symptoms and age. For suspected IBS with diarrhea, the American College of Gastroenterology suggests a blood test for celiac disease and a fecal calprotectin stool test to help rule out inflammatory bowel disease [7]. Your doctor may also suggest a stool test for blood or other investigations [5]. Colorectal cancer screening is recommended for all adults aged 45 to 75 [9].

## When should you worry about mucus in your stool?

Contact a healthcare provider if [1]:

- There's **blood in the mucus**, or the mucus is **yellowish or off-white** in large amounts
- You have **abdominal pain or stomach cramps**
- You have **diarrhea that doesn't go away in three days**
- You have **nausea and vomiting**
- You have **unexplained weight loss**

Also see a doctor about:
- Any change in bowel habit that's not usual for you and lasts, or feeling you need to go again right after you've been [5]
- Tiredness or breathlessness, which can be signs of anemia [5]
- Mucus that wakes you at night, or new symptoms after age 50
- A family history of colorectal cancer, IBD or celiac disease

**Seek urgent care** for stools that are black and tarry or contain blood or pus, high fever, severe abdominal or rectal pain, or signs of dehydration [6]. Go to the ER for non-stop bleeding or large clots [5]. For a full checklist, see [when to see a doctor about bowel changes](/blog/when-to-see-doctor-bowel-changes).

## FAQ

**Can mucus in stool be normal?**
Yes. Some clear mucus is a normal part of how your bowel works [1]. It's the amount, the color and the symptoms that come with it that matter.

**Why do I get mucus in my stool after eating?**
Eating triggers bowel activity, so mucus may simply show up with a bowel movement after a meal. If it follows specific foods, a food intolerance is possible [2]. Log meals and mucus together for two weeks before drawing conclusions.

**Does mucus in stool mean IBS?**
It can be an IBS symptom [1][3], but mucus alone can't diagnose IBS. Doctors make an IBS diagnosis based on symptom patterns and, where needed, tests to rule out other conditions [7].

**What does mucus in stool look like?**
It's usually a clear, white or yellowish jelly-like substance, either clinging to the stool or floating in the toilet. Some people say it looks like snot [1].

**How much mucus in stool is normal?**
There's no set measure. "Some clear mucus" is described as normal, while "a flood of mucus" or a change in color is worth discussing with a provider [1].
