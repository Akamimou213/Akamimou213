> **Quick answer:** Healthy poop is usually Bristol stool chart type 4 (smooth, soft and snake-like) or type 3 (sausage-shaped with cracks). It's some shade of brown, passes without straining, and leaves you feeling empty. You don't need a perfect type 4 every time: in one population study, only about 56–61% of stools were "normal" types. Your pattern over a couple of weeks matters more than any one stool.

Type 4 has become the internet's poop gold standard, and it deserves the reputation. But plenty of people worry because they rarely see a "perfect" one, or they can't tell a 3 from a 4 from a 5.

This guide focuses on the healthy middle of the Bristol stool chart: what type 4 and type 3 look like, how to tell them from type 5, and a five-point checklist that goes beyond shape. For the full 7-type scale, see our [Bristol stool chart guide](/resources/bristol-stool-chart).

**Key takeaways**
- Types 3 and 4 are generally considered the most ideal: formed enough to hold together, not so hard they're difficult to pass [1].
- Type 5 is a gray zone. Some sources group it with loose stools [1], while a validation study treated types 3–5 as normal [3].
- Healthy poop isn't just shape. Color (any brown), effort, emptying and frequency count too [5][6].
- Even healthy people have plenty of non-ideal stools: only 56% of women's and 61% of men's stools were "normal" types in a UK study [4].
- Measure your baseline as the share of stools in types 3–4 over 14 days.

## What does Bristol stool chart type 4 look like?

Bristol type 4 is a smooth, soft stool shaped like a sausage or snake [1]. It holds together, has a smooth surface and passes easily. It's the stool most guides call ideal.

Why it matters: the Bristol scale was built on the finding that stool form reflects how long stool spends in the gut. In the original 1997 study, stool form tracked whole-gut transit time better than stool frequency or weight did [2]. A type 4 suggests material is moving at a comfortable pace: not so slow that the colon pulls out too much water, not so fast that too little is absorbed [1].

## Is type 3 or type 5 also normal?

Type 3 is normal. Type 5 depends on who you ask, and on how often you get it.

| | Type 3 | Type 4 | Type 5 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Looks like | Sausage-shaped with cracks on the surface | Smooth, soft, snake-like | Soft blobs with clear-cut edges |
| Passing it | Easy, maybe slight effort | Easy | Very easy; can feel a bit urgent |
| Usual reading | Ideal [1] | Ideal [1] | Toward loose; grouped with 5–7 by Cleveland Clinic [1], counted as normal in some research [3] |
| If it's most of your stools | Fine | Fine | Mention it if it's new, or comes with urgency or other symptoms |

**How to tell them apart:**
- **3 vs 4:** look at the surface. Visible cracks = 3; smooth = 4.
- **4 vs 5:** look at whether it's one formed piece or separate soft pieces. One piece = 4; separate blobs with defined edges = 5.
- **5 vs 6:** look at the edges. Clear-cut edges = 5; ragged, fluffy, mushy pieces = 6.

These are exactly the borders people get wrong. In a study where volunteers classified stool models, types 2, 3, 5 and 6 were each correctly identified less than 80% of the time [3]. Read the descriptions, not just the pictures.

If you're mostly in types 5–7, see our guide to [Bristol types 5, 6 and 7](/blog/bristol-stool-type-6). If you're mostly in types 1–2, see [Bristol types 1 and 2](/blog/bristol-stool-type-1-2).

## What else makes poop healthy besides shape?

Shape is the headline, but a healthy bowel movement checks several boxes. Use this five-point checklist:

**The healthy-poop checklist**

| ✓ | Check | What "healthy" looks like | Why |
|---|---|---|---|
| ☐ | **Form** | Bristol type 3 or 4 most of the time | Soft, well-formed and easy to pass [1][7] |
| ☐ | **Color** | Any shade of brown | Brown is the ideal adult color; the shade can vary [5] |
| ☐ | **Effort** | Passes without straining or pain | Hard, painful passing is a constipation sign [6] |
| ☐ | **Emptying** | You feel finished afterward | Feeling that not all stool has passed is another constipation sign [6] |
| ☐ | **Frequency** | Somewhere between 3 a day and 3 a week, steady for you | 95.6% of adults in one survey fell in this range [8] |

A few other things often come up:
- **Smell:** stool always smells. What's worth noticing is a sudden, strong change that persists.
- **Size:** not a precise measure, but stool that's consistently very thin and new for you is worth mentioning to a doctor.
- **Off-color days:** a green or reddish stool after spinach or beets is usually just food [5]. See the [poop color chart](/blog/poop-color-chart) for which colors need a doctor.

## Why don't I get a type 4 every time?

Because almost nobody does. Stool form shifts with what you ate, how much you drank, sleep, stress, travel and your menstrual cycle. Healthy populations show a wide spread.

In the UK study mentioned above, about 1,900 adults recorded their stool form. "Normal" stool types, defined as those least likely to cause symptoms, made up only 56% of all stools in women and 61% in men [4]. Stools at the constipated end of the scale were more common in women than in men [4].

So one type 2 after a long flight, or a type 5 after a big curry, isn't a problem. What's worth noticing is a **shift in your usual mix** that lasts.

**Can you nudge yourself toward type 4?** Common, low-risk steps include eating enough fiber (NIDDK cites 22 to 34 grams a day for adults, depending on age and sex) and drinking enough fluid so the fiber works. Increase fiber gradually so your body adjusts [9]. If your stools stay hard or loose despite this, talk to a clinician rather than experimenting indefinitely.

## How do you measure your own baseline?

Log the Bristol type of every bowel movement for 14 days. Then work out what share fell in types 3–4. That single percentage is a simple, useful baseline.

**Example: 14-day summary (illustrative data)**

| Bristol type | Count | Share |
|---|---|---|
| 1–2 | 3 | 18% |
| 3–4 | 11 | 65% |
| 5 | 2 | 12% |
| 6–7 | 1 | 6% |
| **Total** | **17** | **100%** (rounded) |

This person sits in the healthy middle about two-thirds of the time, with a slight lean toward harder stools. That's a reasonable, ordinary pattern. Repeat the 14 days after any change you make, such as more fiber or a new supplement, and compare the percentages.

## When to see a doctor

A non-ideal stool on its own isn't usually a concern. See a doctor if you notice:

- A change in your usual stool form or frequency that lasts more than a couple of weeks [10]
- Blood in your stool, or stool that's black, tarry or dark red [10]
- Pale, clay-colored or white stool for more than a few days
- Stools that are consistently hard and painful, or loose and urgent
- Unintended weight loss, ongoing tiredness or breathlessness [10]
- Symptoms that wake you at night, or new bowel symptoms after age 50
- A family history of colorectal cancer, IBD or celiac disease

More detail: [when to see a doctor about bowel changes](/blog/when-to-see-doctor-bowel-changes).

## FAQ

**Is type 4 poop the healthiest?**
Type 4 (and type 3) are considered the most ideal forms [1]. "Healthiest" is about your overall pattern: mostly 3–4, comfortable to pass, at a steady rhythm.

**Is type 3 poop OK?**
Yes. Type 3 sits alongside type 4 as ideal [1]. If your stools are mostly type 3 and you're straining, a little more fiber and fluid may help [9].

**Is type 5 poop bad?**
Not necessarily. It's soft but formed and some research counts it as normal [3]. If you're mostly type 5 and it's new, or it comes with urgency, keep a log and mention it to a doctor.

**What color should healthy poop be?**
Brown, in any shade from light to dark [5]. Occasional green, yellow or red tints after certain foods are common and usually fade once the food has passed.

**Why does my poop start as a type 4 and end as a type 5?**
Stool closer to the rectum has spent longer in the colon and is firmer. The later part may be softer. A mix within one bowel movement is common. Log the main type.
