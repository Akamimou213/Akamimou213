> **Quick answer:** A food and symptom correlation in your diary is only a lead. It becomes a believable pattern when symptoms follow the food repeatedly, in a consistent time window, more often than on days you skip it, worse with bigger amounts, and still after you rule out stress, sleep, your cycle and other foods eaten alongside. Even then, the real check is a planned removal and reintroduction.

You've kept the diary. You've got a hunch. Maybe it's garlic, maybe it's coffee, maybe it's "anything after 8 p.m." The question is whether that hunch is a real pattern or the human brain doing what it does best: finding faces in clouds.

This guide is the analysis step most food diary advice skips. It gives you a counting method you can do with a pen, a scorecard for judging a suspect, and the confounders that most often fool people.

**Key takeaways**
- Blaming food is extremely common. In one study, 84% of people with IBS linked their symptoms to at least one food [1].
- Expectation alone can produce real symptoms. In a 2025 blinded trial, people with IBS who believed gluten affected them reacted to a gluten-free sham about as often as to gluten or wheat [2].
- Always compare "days with the food" against "days without it." Counting only bad days creates false triggers.
- Check timing, dose and confounders before you believe a pattern, then confirm it with a planned test.

## Why do food diaries produce false patterns?

Food diaries produce false patterns because we notice hits and forget misses, we blame the most recent or most "suspicious" food, and many things that cause gut symptoms (stress, sleep, hormones, meal size) travel with what we eat. None of this means your symptoms aren't real. It means the explanation needs checking.

Three findings show how easily food gets blamed:
- **Self-report runs far ahead of confirmation.** In a survey of over 40,000 US adults, 19% believed they had a food allergy, but about 10.8% had symptoms convincing for one [4]. Allergy isn't intolerance, but the gap shows how often people's beliefs about food outrun the evidence.
- **Expectation matters.** In the 2025 McMaster trial, IBS patients who had felt better gluten-free were given wheat, gluten or a sham bar without knowing which. Symptoms worsened in 39% after wheat, 36% after gluten and 29% after sham, with no statistically significant difference. The authors concluded that expectations played a major role [2].
- **The suspect may be the wrong ingredient.** In an earlier double-blind study of people with self-reported gluten sensitivity, symptoms improved on a low-FODMAP diet, and gluten-specific effects showed up in only 8% of participants [3]. Wheat contains fructans as well as gluten, so a diary can't easily tell them apart.

The takeaway isn't "it's all in your head." It's that a diary alone can't separate a food effect from everything else. You need a method.

## How do you test a food and symptom correlation?

Test a correlation by counting. Compare how often symptoms follow on days you had the food versus days you didn't, using a fixed look-back window. If the rates are similar, the "pattern" is probably coincidence.

**Step 1: Pick one suspect and one symptom.** For example, "garlic" and "bloating 4/10 or worse."

**Step 2: Fix a time window before you look.** Decide in advance how far back from a symptom counts. FODMAP-related symptoms usually take at least about 4 hours [5], and many people find symptoms the same evening or next morning. A window like "4–24 hours after eating" is a reasonable starting rule. Choosing the window *after* seeing the data lets you find whatever you're looking for.

**Step 3: Fill in a two-by-two table** from at least 2–4 weeks of entries (IFFGD's diary is designed for 2–4 weeks [9]).

Here's an illustrative example from 28 days. **These numbers are invented for illustration.**

| | Bloating ≥4/10 within window | No bloating | Total days | Symptom rate |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Ate garlic | 7 | 3 | 10 | 70% |
| No garlic | 4 | 14 | 18 | 22% |

Symptoms followed 70% of garlic days but only 22% of garlic-free days. That's a difference worth testing.

Now compare a second suspect from the same diary:

| | Bloating ≥4/10 within window | No bloating | Total days | Symptom rate |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Had coffee | 10 | 15 | 25 | 40% |
| No coffee | 1 | 2 | 3 | 33% |

Coffee appeared before 10 of the 11 bloating days, which *feels* damning. But it was there on most days, and the symptom rate barely changes. This is how everyday foods become false triggers. Also notice that 3 coffee-free days is too few to say much either way.

**Step 4: Respect small numbers.** With a few weeks of data, a difference of one or two days can flip a result. Treat any count under about 5 in a cell as "not enough yet."

## The 6-point pattern scorecard

Use this scorecard on any suspect. The more boxes it ticks, the more seriously to take it. It's a reasoning aid, not a diagnostic test.

| # | Question | What a "yes" looks like |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Repeats?** | Symptoms followed the food at least 3 separate times |
| 2 | **Consistent timing?** | Symptoms start in a similar window each time (e.g., 4–8 hours later) |
| 3 | **Beats the comparison?** | Symptom rate is clearly higher on food days than non-food days (two-by-two table) |
| 4 | **Dose-response?** | Bigger portions or several servings in one meal bring stronger symptoms. Monash notes FODMAPs from several foods in one sitting can add up [6] |
| 5 | **Survives confounders?** | The pattern holds on low-stress, well-slept, non-period days too |
| 6 | **Reproduces when tested?** | Symptoms ease when the food is removed and return when it's reintroduced in a planned way |

Points 1–5 come from your diary. Point 6 needs a deliberate test: a short, structured removal and reintroduction of one food, ideally with a dietitian. Monash's FODMAP reintroduction guidance, for example, tests one food group at a time over three days at increasing portions, with breaks between challenges until symptoms settle [10]. Our guide to [tracking an elimination diet](/blog/elimination-diet-tracking) shows how to log it.

## What confounders can fake a food pattern?

A confounder is something that travels with a food and causes symptoms itself. These are the ones that most often fake a food trigger:

| Confounder | How it fakes a pattern | How to check it |
|---|---|---|
| Stress | Stressful days bring takeout, coffee and skipped meals | Rate stress 1–5 daily; re-count on low-stress days only. See [IBS and stress](/blog/ibs-and-stress) |
| Poor sleep | In a small study of women with IBS, poorer sleep predicted more next-day abdominal pain [7] | Log hours slept; compare symptom rates after good vs poor nights |
| Menstrual cycle | GI symptoms are commonly reported to increase before and during menses [8] | Log period days; don't test a new food during your highest-symptom days |
| Meal size and speed | Eating triggers the gastrocolic reflex, which can be stronger after very large or fatty meals [5] | Note "large" or "fast" meals; compare like with like |
| Stacking | One serving is fine, three FODMAP foods in a meal isn't [6] | Note everything in the meal, not only the suspect |
| Foods that travel together | Pizza = wheat + garlic + cheese + often alcohol | Look for days when the suspect appeared alone |
| Last-meal bias | The meal just eaten gets blamed for a reaction to an earlier one | Use a fixed look-back window (Step 2) |
| Expectation | Believing a food is a problem can make symptoms more likely [2] | Be wary of patterns that only appear when you *know* you ate the food |

## How to track patterns you can trust

Good analysis starts with good data. The diary habits that matter most: log meals and symptoms at the time (not from memory), record start times and a 0–10 severity, log good days as carefully as bad ones, and add a line of daily context. ## When to see a doctor

Don't keep analyzing a diary if you have warning signs. See a doctor promptly for:
- Blood in your stool, or black, tarry stools
- Unintended weight loss or anemia
- Symptoms that wake you at night, persistent fever, or severe pain
- A lasting change in bowel habits, especially starting after age 50
- A family history of colon cancer, inflammatory bowel disease or celiac disease

And if you're thinking about cutting several foods at once, talk to a dietitian first. Restricting many foods on the strength of diary correlations can leave you with a narrow diet and no clear answer.

## FAQ

**How many times does a food need to cause symptoms before I count it as a trigger?**
There's no official number, but three or more clear repeats in a consistent time window, with a higher symptom rate than on days without the food, is a reasonable bar for "worth testing." Confirmation still comes from a planned reintroduction.

**Why does a food bother me some days and not others?**
Dose, combination and context. A bigger portion, several FODMAP foods in one meal [6], a stressful day or a poor night's sleep [7] can all tip you over a threshold the same food doesn't reach on a calm day.

**Can an app tell me what my trigger foods are?**
No app can prove a food causes your symptoms. Apps can count associations faster than you can by hand, which is useful, but the results are hypotheses to test and discuss with a clinician.

**What is the nocebo effect with food?**
It's when expecting a food to cause symptoms makes symptoms more likely. Blinded trials, where people don't know what they're eating, are how researchers separate it from a true food effect [2].

**How long should I collect data before analyzing?**
At least two weeks, ideally four [9]. Fewer days means most foods appear too rarely to compare.
