> **Quick answer:** An elimination diet tracker is a log that follows you through three phases: a baseline (eat normally, log symptoms), elimination (remove the suspect food for a set period, usually 2–6 weeks) and reintroduction (add it back one food at a time, in rising amounts, while logging symptoms). The reintroduction log is where the answer comes from. Do it with a dietitian, and never use it to test a suspected allergy.

Most elimination diets don't fail during the elimination. They fail afterwards. People feel better, then reintroduce three foods over a weekend, feel worse, and end up avoiding all of them indefinitely. Or they never reintroduce anything at all.

Good tracking prevents that. This guide covers what to log in each phase, a sample reintroduction log, how to read the result, and the safety rules that matter more than any template.

**Key takeaways**
- Log a baseline first. Without "before" data, you can't tell whether elimination helped.
- Set the elimination length in advance, and stop if nothing changes. The AGA recommends a predetermined trial length [1].
- Reintroduce one food at a time, over a few days, with breaks between challenges [4].
- Restrictive diets should be guided by a professional with dietary expertise [1][2].
- Never self-test a suspected food allergy. Reintroducing an allergen belongs in an allergist's office [7].

## What is an elimination diet, and who should not do one?

An elimination diet is a short, structured experiment: you remove a suspected food (or food group) to see whether symptoms improve, then reintroduce it to see whether they return. The NHS describes this as one of the ways a dietitian may assess a suspected food intolerance [5]. The low-FODMAP diet is the best-known version for IBS, with three phases: restriction for no more than 4–6 weeks, reintroduction, then personalization [1].

It's a test, not a lifestyle. Cleveland Clinic notes the low-FODMAP elimination phase is not meant to be permanent [3], and avoiding many foods at once can cause nutritional deficiencies [6].

**Talk to a clinician before starting, and don't self-direct one, if you:**
- Suspect a true food allergy (hives, swelling, wheeze, or reactions within minutes). Only an experienced allergist should run a food challenge [7].
- Have, or have had, an eating disorder, or notice you're becoming anxious or rigid about food. The AGA says screening for disordered eating is critical before restrictive diets [1].
- Are at risk of malnutrition, are food insecure, or already eat a narrow range of foods [1].
- Suspect celiac disease. Get tested *before* going gluten-free, because a gluten-free diet can affect test results [8].
- Are pregnant, a child or teen, or have a medical condition that affects nutrition.

NICE recommends that single-food avoidance and exclusion diets be given only by a healthcare professional with expertise in dietary management [2]. In practice that usually means a registered dietitian.

## What should you track in each phase?

Track the same core fields in every phase (food, symptoms with start time and severity, stool, context) so the phases are comparable. Then add the phase-specific fields below.

| Phase | Typical length | Log every day | Add in this phase | You're done when |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Baseline | 1–2 weeks | Meals with ingredients, symptoms (0–10, start time), stool (Bristol type), sleep, stress, cycle | Your average symptom score per day; suspect foods noted | You have a clear "normal week" picture |
| 2. Elimination | Set in advance; low-FODMAP restriction no more than 4–6 weeks [1]; Cleveland Clinic cites 2–6 weeks [3] | Same as baseline | Any accidental exposure (what, when, how much) and what happened | You reach the planned end date, or symptoms have clearly improved |
| 3. Reintroduction | A few days per food; the full FODMAP reintroduction takes around 6–8 weeks [4] | Same as baseline | Challenge food, day, portion size, time eaten; washout days | Every eliminated food has been tested |
| 4. Personalization | Ongoing | Lighter logging | Tolerated amounts per food | You have a less restrictive, sustainable plan |

A few tracking rules:
- **Keep everything else steady.** Don't start a new supplement, medicine or exercise plan mid-test unless your clinician advises it.
- **Write down accidental exposures.** They're informative, not failures.
- **Compare like with like.** Compare average daily symptom scores across phases, not your best day with your worst.

## A sample food reintroduction log

Reintroduction is where elimination diets earn their keep. Monash's guidance is to test one food or FODMAP group at a time, at about the same time each day, over three days with increasing portions, then take a 2–3 day break, or until symptoms settle, before the next challenge, while staying on your elimination diet [4].

Here's an illustrative 3-day dairy (lactose) challenge followed by a washout. **Illustrative data, not a real case.** Baseline average symptom score during elimination: 1/10.

| Day | Challenge food and portion (time) | Symptoms (start, 0–10) | Stool | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Challenge day 1 | ½ cup milk with breakfast (8:00) | None | Type 4 | Normal sleep, low stress |
| Challenge day 2 | 1 cup milk with breakfast (8:00) | 13:30 bloating 3/10, settled by evening | Type 4 | |
| Challenge day 3 | 1 cup milk + 1 cup yogurt (8:00, 12:30) | 15:00 gas 4/10; 19:00 cramps 5/10 | Next day type 6 | Stopped challenge here |
| Washout 1 | Back to elimination diet | Bloating 2/10 morning | Type 5 | |
| Washout 2 | Elimination diet | None | Type 4 | Symptoms settled; next challenge can start |

What this suggests: a threshold, not an all-or-nothing reaction. Half a cup was fine; larger amounts weren't. That's a common and useful result, because it means "some dairy, in smaller amounts" may be on the table, rather than "no dairy ever." A dietitian can help turn that into a plan. ## How do you read elimination diet results?

Read the results by comparing symptom scores across phases and checking whether symptoms returned on reintroduction in a dose-related way. Improvement during elimination alone isn't enough; it has to come back when the food does.

| What happened | Likely interpretation | Next step |
|---|---|---|
| Symptoms improved in elimination and returned on reintroduction, worse with bigger portions | Good evidence for a personal, dose-related trigger | Find your tolerated amount with your dietitian; keep the rest of your diet broad |
| Symptoms improved in elimination but did *not* return on reintroduction | The food probably wasn't the main cause; improvement may reflect other changes or expectation | Add the food back; look for other drivers |
| Symptoms didn't improve in elimination | Diet change didn't help for this food | Stop at the planned end date, as the AGA advises [1]; discuss other options |
| Mixed or unclear results | Too much noise (stress, illness, cycle, several foods at once) | Repeat the challenge later when things are calm, one food at a time |

Expectation is a real factor. In a 2025 blinded trial, people with IBS who believed gluten caused their symptoms reacted to a gluten-free sham about as often as to gluten or wheat [9]. Unblinded home challenges can't rule that out, so treat one dramatic reaction cautiously and look for consistent, dose-related patterns. For more on separating signal from coincidence, see [how to spot real patterns in a food log](/blog/food-symptom-patterns).

## When to see a doctor

Stop and get medical advice if, during any phase, you notice:
- Blood in your stool, or black, tarry stools
- Unintended weight loss, or you're eating noticeably less overall
- Symptoms that wake you at night, persistent fever, or severe or worsening pain
- A lasting change in bowel habits, especially starting after age 50
- Growing fear of eating, skipped meals, or a shrinking list of "safe" foods

Get emergency help for hives, throat or lip swelling, wheezing or trouble breathing after eating.

## FAQ

**How long should an elimination diet last?**
Set the length before you start. For low FODMAP, the AGA advises restriction for no more than 4–6 weeks before reintroduction [1]. If nothing improves by the planned end date, stop and reconsider [1].

**Can I reintroduce more than one food at a time?**
It's best not to. Testing one food at a time, with breaks between challenges, is how you know which one caused a reaction [4].

**What if I accidentally eat an eliminated food?**
Log it with the time and amount, note any symptoms, and carry on. An accidental exposure can actually be informative.

**Is an elimination diet safe to do on my own?**
For a single, non-essential food over a short period, many people do. For multiple foods, low FODMAP, a history of disordered eating, or any suspected allergy, work with a dietitian or doctor [1][2][7].

**Should I keep tracking after reintroduction?**
Lightly, yes. Logging for a couple of weeks after personalizing your diet helps confirm your tolerated amounts hold up in everyday life.
