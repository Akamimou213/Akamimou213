> **Quick answer:** Yes. You can develop a food intolerance, or a reaction that looks like one, at any age, even to foods you've eaten for years. Common reasons include a natural drop in lactase (the enzyme that digests milk sugar), a recent stomach bug, a new medical condition such as celiac disease, or a change in your gut's sensitivity. Because "new" food reactions can also signal something that needs treatment, it's worth tracking and getting checked.

"I've eaten this my whole life. Why now?" It's one of the most frustrating questions in gut health. The reaction feels sudden, but the cause is often something that changed quietly: your body, your gut after an illness, a medication, or what else is on your plate.

This guide walks through the evidence-based reasons a food reaction can appear in adulthood, how to tell them apart, and a two-week way to check what's going on before you cut anything out.

**Key takeaways**
- Lactose intolerance often doesn't start until later childhood, the teen years or early adulthood, as lactase production falls [2].
- A gut infection can leave lasting symptoms: about 10% of people develop IBS in the year after infectious gastroenteritis [3].
- Celiac disease affects adults as well as children, and should be tested for *before* you go gluten-free [4][5].
- Food allergies can start in adulthood too. Nearly half of US adults with food allergy developed at least one as an adult [6].
- New symptoms after 50, weight loss or blood in your stool need a doctor, not a diary.

## Can food intolerance start suddenly?

Yes, a food intolerance can appear in adulthood, and it can feel sudden even when the underlying change has been gradual. Food intolerance is a digestive problem rather than an immune reaction, and its symptoms usually show up a few hours after eating [1]. That delay is part of why a "new" intolerance can feel random: the meal you blame may not be the one responsible. Our guide to [delayed food intolerance symptoms](/blog/delayed-food-intolerance-symptoms) explains the timing.

Many commercial sites answering this question sell intolerance tests. The causes below are the ones backed by medical sources, and none of them is diagnosed by an IgG blood panel.

## What causes a new food intolerance in adults?

The most common evidence-based causes are a natural decline in lactase, damage or irritation to the gut after an infection, a new underlying condition, and changes in the gut's sensitivity. Here's how they differ.

| Possible cause | What happens | Clues in your history | Who can check it |
|---|---|---|---|
| Lactase decline (primary lactose intolerance) | The small intestine makes less lactase after infancy; symptoms may not begin until later childhood, the teen years or early adulthood [2] | Symptoms after milk, ice cream or soft cheese, worse with bigger amounts | Doctor; hydrogen breath test or a dairy-free trial |
| After a gut infection or injury (secondary lactose intolerance) | Infections or conditions that injure the small intestine can reduce lactase [2] | Started after food poisoning, a stomach bug or illness | Doctor; often improves as the gut heals [2] |
| Post-infection IBS | About 10% of people develop IBS within 12 months of infectious enteritis, a risk about 4 times higher than in people without the infection [3] | Bloating, pain, changed stools that persist after an infection; reactions to many foods | Doctor or gastroenterologist |
| Celiac disease | An immune reaction to gluten in people with certain genes; it affects children and adults [4] | Diarrhea, bloating, fatigue, anemia, family history | Doctor: blood tests while still eating gluten, then usually a biopsy [5] |
| Adult-onset food allergy | Immune (IgE) reaction; 48% of food-allergic US adults reported developing an allergy as adults [6] | Hives, swelling, wheeze, vomiting, often within minutes to hours | Allergist |
| Alpha-gal syndrome | A tick-bite-linked allergy to a sugar in red meat and some dairy; symptoms usually 2–6 hours after eating [7] | Lives in or visited tick areas; reactions after beef, pork or lamb | Doctor or allergist |
| Context changes | More stress, poorer sleep, larger portions, new medicines or supplements | The food is the same but life isn't | Track it (below) |

A note on belief: a NIH consensus panel noted that many people who think they are lactose intolerant are not lactose malabsorbers [8]. That doesn't mean symptoms aren't real. It means the explanation may be different from the obvious one, which is a good reason to check rather than assume.

## Is it an intolerance, an allergy or something else?

The quickest way to tell is timing and type of symptoms. Intolerance mainly causes digestive symptoms hours later; allergy can involve skin, breathing and circulation, often fast.

| Feature | Food intolerance | Food allergy | Worth investigating for another condition |
|---|---|---|---|
| Typical timing | A few hours after eating [1] | Often within minutes to a couple of hours; alpha-gal is 2–6 hours [7] | Symptoms regardless of what you eat |
| Typical symptoms | Bloating, gas, tummy pain, diarrhea [1] | Hives, swelling of lips or throat, wheeze, vomiting, dizziness [7] | Weight loss, blood in stool, night symptoms, anemia |
| Dose matters? | Usually yes | Even small amounts can trigger a reaction | Often unrelated to food dose |
| Life-threatening? | No | Can be | Depends on the cause |
| What to do | Track and discuss with a doctor or dietitian | See an allergist; don't self-test | See a doctor promptly |

## Can food intolerances go away?

Some can. NIDDK notes that when secondary lactose intolerance is caused by an infection or condition that injured the small intestine, lactose tolerance may return once the underlying cause is treated [2]. Post-infection gut symptoms can also change over time [3]. Primary lactase decline tends to persist, but it's usually dose-dependent, so many people can still enjoy smaller amounts.

That's a strong argument against cutting foods out forever. Retesting a food after a few months, ideally with guidance from a dietitian, can widen your diet again.

## How to check what changed

Start a two-week log the moment a "new" reaction appears, while you still eat normally. IFFGD designs its daily diary for 2–4 weeks [9], long enough for the food to show up several times.

**Step 1: Answer the "what changed?" checklist** (write it at the top of your diary):
- Any stomach bug, food poisoning or travel illness in the past year?
- New medicines or supplements, or a change in dose?
- New stress, poor sleep, or a big change in routine?
- Bigger portions or a new way of eating (more beans, protein shakes, sugar-free products)?
- Any tick bites, especially before reactions to red meat?
- Family history of celiac disease or inflammatory bowel disease?

**Step 2: Log for 14 days.** Every meal (ingredients and rough amount), every symptom (start time, 0–10), every bowel movement (Bristol type), plus sleep, stress and cycle day.

**Step 3: Look back from each symptom** across the previous 24 hours, and compare days with the suspect food versus days without it. Our [14-day method for identifying food triggers](/blog/how-to-identify-food-triggers) walks through the review.

**Step 4: Take it to a clinician** before cutting the food out, especially if you suspect gluten (test first) or have any warning signs.

## When to see a doctor

A new food reaction is a reason to see a doctor, not just to change your diet, especially if you have [10]:
- New digestive symptoms starting after age 50
- Unintended weight loss or anemia
- Blood in your stool, or black, tarry stools
- Symptoms that wake you at night, persistent fever, or severe pain
- Ongoing diarrhea or a lasting change in bowel habits
- A family history of celiac disease, colon cancer or inflammatory bowel disease

Get emergency help for swelling of the lips, tongue or throat, trouble breathing, or fainting after eating.

## FAQ

**Why do I keep developing new food intolerances?**
Reacting to more and more foods can point to a more general cause, such as post-infection IBS, a sensitive gut, stress, or an untreated condition, rather than many separate intolerances. See a doctor before restricting further; a dietitian can help you avoid an ever-shrinking diet.

**Can you become lactose intolerant later in life?**
Yes. Lactase production drops after infancy, and symptoms may not begin until later childhood, the teen years or early adulthood [2]. Illness that injures the small intestine can also cause it at any age [2].

**Can stress cause a food intolerance?**
Stress doesn't create a true intolerance, but it can make the gut more reactive, so foods you usually tolerate may cause symptoms on stressful days. Logging stress alongside food helps you see this.

**Should I take a food intolerance test?**
Validated tests exist for specific conditions, such as a hydrogen breath test for lactose and blood tests for celiac disease ordered by a doctor. IgG food panels sold online aren't recommended for diagnosing intolerance; the NHS says there's limited evidence such tests give accurate results [1].

**Can a food intolerance start after COVID or food poisoning?**
Gut symptoms can persist after infections. Meta-analysis data show about 10% of people develop IBS after infectious enteritis [3]. If symptoms started after an illness, tell your doctor.
