> **Quick answer:** Most at-home food intolerance tests aren't worth it. They usually measure IgG antibodies to dozens of foods, and allergy societies in Europe, the US and Canada advise against using IgG for diagnosis because it mainly reflects that you've eaten a food, not that it harms you. Some tests are valid, like a hydrogen breath test for lactose or celiac blood tests ordered by a doctor. For everything else, a food diary plus a supervised elimination and reintroduction is the evidence-based route.

If you're tired of guessing, a test that promises to name your problem foods from a single finger-prick is tempting. Lots of people buy one. And on forums like Reddit and HealthUnlocked, many describe the same aftermath: a long list of "reactive" foods, a much narrower diet, and symptoms that didn't really change.

This article isn't anti-testing. It's about matching the test to the question. Below: what the major medical bodies actually say, which tests are useful, and what a food diary can show that a blood panel can't.

**Key takeaways**
- IgG to a food is a marker of exposure and tolerance, not intolerance, according to the Canadian Society of Allergy and Clinical Immunology [3].
- The European (EAACI), American (AAAAI) and Canadian (CSACI) allergy societies have all advised against IgG food testing for diagnosis [1][2][3].
- The NHS says some intolerance tests you can buy are not recommended and may push you to avoid foods unnecessarily [6].
- Valid tests exist for specific questions: lactose breath testing, celiac blood tests, and allergist-guided allergy testing [8][9][10].
- A food diary and a structured, supervised elimination and reintroduction is the standard approach for intolerances [6][7].

## Do food intolerance tests work?

Most direct-to-consumer food intolerance tests don't work for what they promise. The common kind measures IgG (or IgG4) antibodies in a finger-prick blood sample against a panel of foods, then labels foods with high results as ones to avoid. The problem is what IgG means. The Canadian allergy society's position statement says "the presence of specific IgG to food is a marker of exposure and tolerance to food" [3]. So foods you eat often tend to light up, which is why healthy people also test positive [3].

A Cleveland Clinic allergist puts it plainly: a high IgG level "doesn't really mean you have a food intolerance," and there aren't studies showing these at-home tests work [7].

Why do some people still feel better after following their results? Cutting many foods at once changes lots of things together (fewer FODMAPs, less processed food, more attention to eating), and expectation can play a role too. The improvement is real, but it doesn't confirm the test, and it often comes at the cost of a needlessly restricted diet.

## What do allergy experts say about IgG tests?

The major allergy bodies in Europe, the US and Canada have all advised against using IgG food testing for diagnosis. Here's what each actually said:

| Organization | Statement | Key points |
|---|---|---|
| **EAACI** (European Academy of Allergy and Clinical Immunology), 2008 | Task Force Report: "Testing for IgG4 against foods is not recommended as a diagnostic tool" [1] | Many people test positive with no symptoms; no controlled studies show diagnostic value; IgG4 testing considered irrelevant for the lab workup [1] |
| **AAAAI** (American Academy of Allergy, Asthma & Immunology), 2010 | Adverse Reactions to Foods Committee endorsement of the EAACI paper, approved by the AAAAI Board [2] | Antibody presence "does not indicate disease"; food-specific IgG4 may be "the normal human response" to eating food; misinterpreted results can lead to nutritionally inadequate diets [2] |
| **AAAAI Choosing Wisely**, 2012 | "Don't perform unproven diagnostic tests such as immunoglobulin G (IgG) testing…" [4] | "Specific IgG testing is not a test for an allergic condition" [4] |
| **CSACI** (Canadian Society of Allergy and Clinical Immunology), 2012 | Position statement on food-specific IgG testing [3] | "Strongly discourages" IgG testing to identify or predict food reactions; risks false diagnoses and unnecessary dietary restrictions [3] |
| **Choosing Wisely Canada** | Allergy & Clinical Immunology recommendations [5] | "Don't order specific IgG testing to panels of foods"; IgG indicates previous exposure, not hypersensitivity [5] |
| **NHS** (UK) | Food intolerance guidance [6] | Some tests you can buy "are not recommended"; limited evidence they're accurate; can lead to avoiding foods unnecessarily [6] |

A fairness note: the EAACI and AAAAI documents date from 2008–2012, and the AAAAI notes its statements shouldn't be taken as current policy more than five years after publication [2]. Still, we found no major allergy or gastroenterology organization that has since endorsed IgG testing for diagnosing food intolerance, and current public guidance from the NHS, Choosing Wisely Canada and Cleveland Clinic says the same thing [5][6][7].

## Which food tests are actually valid?

Valid food tests answer a specific question and are interpreted alongside your history by a clinician. Here's how common options compare:

| Test | What it measures | Useful for | Verdict |
|---|---|---|---|
| IgG / IgG4 food panels (at-home or practitioner) | Antibodies that reflect exposure to foods [3] | Not validated for diagnosing intolerance or allergy [1][3] | Not recommended |
| Hydrogen breath test | Hydrogen in your breath after a measured dose of lactose [8] | Lactose intolerance, ordered by a doctor [8] | Valid for that question |
| Celiac blood tests (then usually biopsy) | Antibodies that are often raised in untreated celiac disease [9] | Celiac disease; must be done *before* going gluten-free [9] | Valid; ask your doctor |
| Allergist-guided IgE tests (skin prick or blood) | Allergy antibodies, interpreted with your history [4] | Suspected food *allergy* [4] | Valid when guided by history; panels without history can mislead [4] |
| Oral food challenge | Your reaction to eating the food under supervision [10] | Confirming or ruling out allergy; "highly accurate" [10] | Allergist only [10] |
| Food and symptom diary + supervised elimination and reintroduction | Your own symptoms in response to removing and re-adding a food [6] | Food intolerance and IBS-type symptoms [6][7] | The standard approach |

If your question is "am I allergic?" (fast reactions, hives, swelling, wheeze), see an allergist. Our guide to keeping a [food allergy diary](/blog/food-allergy-diary) covers what to bring.

## What can a food diary show that a test can't?

A food diary shows *your* symptoms in relation to *your* actual eating: timing, amounts and context. That's exactly the information intolerance depends on, and exactly what a blood panel can't capture.

| Question | IgG test kit | Food and symptom diary |
|---|---|---|
| Does this food cause *my* symptoms? | No (measures exposure) [3] | Shows whether symptoms follow it, repeatedly |
| How much can I eat? | No | Shows dose patterns (fine with a little, not a lot) |
| How long after eating? | No | Captures timing, often hours later |
| Is it really stress, sleep or my period? | No | Can, if you log context |
| Is there a pattern in my stools? | No | Yes, if you log Bristol type |
| Is it accurate? | Not validated [1][3] | Shows associations, not proof; needs a structured test to confirm |
| Cost | You pay per kit | Free on paper |

To be honest about a diary's limits: it shows correlations, not causes, and it's easy to over-read. That's why the second step, a planned removal and reintroduction of one food at a time, matters. Cleveland Clinic advises doing elimination diets with a dietitian because avoiding multiple foods can cause nutritional deficiency [7].

## What to do instead of a test kit

1. **Rule out what needs ruling out.** See a doctor first if you have red-flag symptoms (below), suspect celiac disease (test before cutting gluten [9]) or suspect lactose intolerance (a breath test can help [8]).
2. **Keep a food and symptom diary for 2–4 weeks**, eating normally. Log ingredients, amounts, times, symptoms with start times, stools and context. Our [food diary for food intolerance](/blog/food-diary-for-food-intolerance) guide shows exactly what to record, and the printable is on our [food and symptom diary page](/resources/food-symptom-diary).
3. **Review it honestly.** Compare days with and without each suspect. The [14-day method for identifying food triggers](/blog/how-to-identify-food-triggers) walks through it.
4. **Test one suspect with a dietitian.** A structured elimination and reintroduction is how the NHS describes confirming an intolerance [6]. Our [elimination diet tracking guide](/blog/elimination-diet-tracking) covers what to log.

## When to see a doctor

See a doctor rather than buying a test if you have:
- Blood in your stool, or black, tarry stools
- Unintended weight loss or anemia
- Symptoms that wake you at night, persistent fever, or severe pain
- Ongoing diarrhea or a lasting change in bowel habits, especially if new after age 50
- A family history of celiac disease, inflammatory bowel disease or colon cancer
- Reactions within minutes of eating, hives, swelling, or breathing trouble (get emergency help for breathing problems)

## FAQ

**Are food sensitivity tests accurate?**
IgG-based food sensitivity tests aren't validated for diagnosing intolerance. Allergy societies say IgG mostly reflects exposure to foods you eat, so positive results are expected in healthy people [1][3].

**Can my doctor order a food sensitivity test?**
Doctors can order validated tests for specific questions, such as a hydrogen breath test for lactose intolerance [8], celiac blood tests [9], or allergist-guided allergy testing [4]. Most don't order IgG food panels, in line with Choosing Wisely recommendations [4][5].

**I followed my IgG results and feel better. Was the test right?**
Not necessarily. Cutting many foods changes several things at once, and expectation can affect symptoms. A dietitian can help you reintroduce foods one at a time to find out which (if any) truly matter, so you don't stay restricted unnecessarily.

**Is the hydrogen breath test the same as an intolerance test kit?**
No. A breath test measures how your body handles a specific sugar like lactose, and it's done through a doctor [8]. It answers one question; IgG kits claim to answer many and aren't validated [1][3].

**What does Reddit say about food intolerance tests?**
Forum discussions are mixed, with many people describing long lists of flagged foods and little improvement, and some reporting they felt better. Anecdotes can't settle accuracy; the position statements above are based on the evidence.
