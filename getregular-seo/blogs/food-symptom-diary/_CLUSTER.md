**Cluster id:** `food-symptom-diary` · **Pillar:** `/resources/food-symptom-diary` (printable diary) · **Product page:** `/food-symptom-tracker`

**Core message in every post:** a diary shows associations, not causes. Symptoms are often delayed by hours, and sometimes show up the next day. Confounders (stress, sleep, menstrual cycle, portion size, FODMAP stacking, foods eaten together, expectation) can fake a pattern. Suspected triggers get confirmed through a planned removal and reintroduction, ideally with a dietitian.

### Position statements verified for article 9 (IgG testing)
Each was checked against the primary document on 2026-10-19:
- **EAACI** (Stapel et al., *Allergy* 2008): "Testing for IgG4 against foods is not recommended as a diagnostic tool."
- **AAAAI** (May 2010): the Adverse Reactions to Foods Committee endorsed the EAACI paper, with Board approval. The PDF text was extracted and quoted. It states that it is not a formal position statement and is "not to be considered to reflect current AAAAI standards or policy after five years". The article discloses this.
- **AAAAI Choosing Wisely** (April 2012): "Don't perform unproven diagnostic tests such as immunoglobulin G (IgG) testing…"
- **CSACI** (Carr et al., *AACI* 2012, PMC3443017): "strongly discourages" food-specific IgG testing. It calls IgG "a marker of exposure and tolerance".
- **Choosing Wisely Canada:** "Don't order specific IgG testing to panels of foods."
- **NHS:** "Some tests you can buy that claim to diagnose food intolerances are not recommended."
