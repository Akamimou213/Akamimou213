> **Quick answer:** On the Bristol stool chart, type 1 is separate hard lumps, like nuts, and type 2 is a sausage shape made of hard lumps. Both usually mean stool has moved slowly through your gut. Slow transit gives the colon longer to absorb water. An occasional type 1 or 2 is common. Hard stools on more than a quarter of your bowel movements, for months, is one of the criteria doctors use for chronic constipation.

The Bristol Stool Form Scale gives everyone a shared vocabulary for poop. Types 1 and 2 are the hard end. If you're seeing them often, you're probably straining, maybe feeling like you didn't finish, and wondering what to change.

This guide explains what types 1 and 2 mean, how common they are, what usually causes them and how to tell whether yours are a blip or a pattern. For the full seven-type chart, see our [Bristol stool chart guide](/resources/bristol-stool-chart).

## Key takeaways

- Stool form tracks transit time. In the study that validated the scale, form correlated better with gut transit time than stool frequency did [1].
- Types 1–2 are the "hard" end of the scale [2]. Clinicians use them in the Rome IV criteria for constipation and IBS subtypes [3].
- An occasional type 2 can be normal. In a US survey, 90% of women with normal bowel habits reported stools between types 2 and 6 [4].
- What matters is the *share* of hard stools over weeks, plus straining, frequency and a feeling of incomplete emptying.
- Tracking two weeks of Bristol types gives you a percentage, which is far more useful than "it's been hard lately."

## What do Bristol type 1 and type 2 look like?

The Bristol scale has seven types, from hardest (1) to most liquid (7) [2].

| Type | Description (Bristol scale) | Common nicknames | Usually suggests |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1** | Separate hard lumps, like nuts (hard to pass) [2] | Pellets, rabbit droppings | Slowest transit |
| **2** | Sausage-shaped but lumpy [2] | Lumpy log | Slow transit |
| 3 | Like a sausage but with cracks on the surface [2] | — | Normal range |
| 4 | Like a sausage or snake, smooth and soft [2] | "Smooth sausage" | Normal range |

Types 3 and 4 are what most people aim for. More on that in [what healthy poop looks like](/blog/what-does-healthy-poop-look-like).

## What does Bristol stool type 1 mean?

Type 1 usually means stool has spent a long time in the colon. The scale was validated because stool form reflects how fast material moves through the gut. In the original Bristol study, stool form correlated more strongly with whole-gut transit time than stool frequency or output did [1]. Harder forms went with slower transit [1].

The colon absorbs water. The longer stool stays there, the drier and harder it gets. Type 1 often comes out in separate pieces, and many people strain to pass it.

**Type 2** sits one step closer to normal: still hard, but formed into a lumpy sausage. It's often described as milder constipation. On its own, a single type 2 doesn't mean much. Patterns do.

## How many hard stools is too many?

A useful threshold is **more than one in four bowel movements**, sustained over months.

That's the line in the Rome IV criteria, which clinicians use to diagnose functional constipation. Lumpy or hard stools (Bristol 1–2) on more than 25% of bowel movements is one of six criteria, and two or more are needed. The others include straining, a sense of incomplete evacuation, a feeling of blockage, needing manual help, and fewer than three spontaneous bowel movements a week. Symptoms must be present for the last three months, starting at least six months before diagnosis [3].

Rome IV also uses the same 25% line for IBS subtypes. IBS with constipation (IBS-C) means hard or lumpy stools on 25% or more of bowel movements and loose stools on fewer than 25% [3]. If belly pain is part of your picture, see [IBS-C vs IBS-D](/blog/ibs-c-vs-ibs-d).

Some perspective helps, too. In a US national survey of adults with normal bowel habits, 90% of men reported stools in the type 3–5 range, but 90% of women reported a wider type 2–6 range [4]. In a large British population study, stools rated as a normal type made up only about 56% of women's stools and 61% of men's [9]. An occasional hard stool is part of normal life.

**Reading it:** week 1 is well above the one-in-four line. Week 2 dipped below it. A single good week isn't a trend. Keep logging to see whether it holds, and bring a longer record to a doctor if it doesn't.

## What causes hard, lumpy poop?

Usually several everyday factors at once. NIDDK lists [5]:

- **Diet and fluids:** not enough fiber or liquids
- **Activity:** too little physical activity
- **Routine:** travel, changes in what or how much you eat, ignoring the urge to go
- **Medicines and supplements:** narcotic pain medicines, iron supplements, some antidepressants, antacids containing aluminum or calcium, diuretics, among others
- **Health conditions:** for example, diabetes, hypothyroidism and celiac disease
- **Life stages:** pregnancy and aging

If type 1–2 stools appeared suddenly and won't go away, especially after 50, see a doctor rather than troubleshooting alone.

## What to change if you keep getting type 1 or 2

Start with the low-risk basics, give each change about two weeks, and keep logging so you know what helped.

1. **Fiber, gradually.** NIDDK advises adding fiber a little at a time [6]. For supplements, psyllium has the strongest evidence. Doses above 10 g a day for at least four weeks look optimal [7]. If fiber seems to make things worse, see [can fiber make constipation worse](/blog/fiber-constipation).
2. **Fluids,** especially when you add fiber [6].
3. **A daily toilet window,** 15–45 minutes after breakfast, and go whenever you feel the urge [6].
4. **Move** most days [6].
5. **Review your medicines** with a pharmacist or doctor if constipation started after a new one. Don't stop anything on your own [6].
6. **If that's not enough,** the 2023 AGA–ACG guideline strongly recommends polyethylene glycol (PEG) for chronic idiopathic constipation, among other options [8]. Ask your doctor which fits you.

If you strain a lot even when stools are softer, mention it. Straining and incomplete emptying can point to a pelvic floor problem, which needs a different approach. NIDDK notes biofeedback can help retrain these muscles [6].

## How to track this

To know your hard-stool percentage, you need every bowel movement typed, not just the memorable ones. Record:

- **Bristol type** for each bowel movement
- **Straining** and **incomplete emptying** (yes/no)
- **No-go days**
- **What you're changing** (fiber, fluids, routine) and when

After two weeks, divide hard stools (types 1–2) by total bowel movements. That's your share. Our [constipation diary guide](/resources/constipation-diary) has a printable sheet. Our guide on [how to describe poop to a doctor](/blog/how-to-describe-poop-to-doctor) shows how to present it.

## When to see a doctor

Hard stools are usually a lifestyle-and-habits problem, but get checked if you have:

- Blood in or on your stool, or black, tarry stool
- Unintended weight loss
- New hard stools or constipation that persists, especially after age 50
- Hard stools alternating with very thin stools, or a lasting change in your usual pattern
- Severe belly pain, vomiting, a swollen belly or being unable to pass gas. Seek urgent care for these [5].
- Pain, bleeding or tearing when passing hard stools
- No improvement after a few weeks of self-care, or a family history of colon or rectal cancer [5]

## FAQ

### Is Bristol type 2 constipation?

Type 2 is on the hard side and often goes with constipation. One type 2 isn't a diagnosis, though. Clinicians look at whether hard stools make up more than a quarter of bowel movements, alongside other symptoms, over at least three months [3].

### Is type 1 poop serious?

Usually not on its own. It most often reflects slow transit [1]. It's worth attention if it's frequent, new, painful, or comes with bleeding, weight loss or other red flags.

### Can you poop every day and still be constipated?

Yes. Constipation includes hard or lumpy stools, straining and a feeling of incomplete emptying, not just infrequency [3][5]. Daily type 1 stools with straining can still count.

### Why is my poop hard even though I eat fiber?

Fiber may be too low, increased too fast, or taken without enough fluid. Some people have slow transit or a pelvic floor problem that fiber doesn't fix. Our [fiber and constipation guide](/blog/fiber-constipation) walks through it. A doctor can help if a fair trial hasn't worked.

### What's the difference between type 1 and type 2?

Type 1 is separate hard lumps. Type 2 is hard lumps stuck together into a sausage shape [2]. Type 1 generally reflects slower transit than type 2 [1].
