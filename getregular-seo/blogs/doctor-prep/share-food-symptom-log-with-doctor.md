> **Quick answer:** To share a symptom diary with your doctor, turn it into a one-page summary first: the date symptoms started, how often they happen, typical stool type and frequency, red flags (or their absence), and one or two patterns you'd like checked. Bring the summary printed or as a PDF, keep the full log on hand for detail, and ask how your practice prefers to receive files before uploading anything to a portal.

You've done the hard part: weeks of logging. Then the appointment comes, you hand over 40 pages or scroll through your phone, and the doctor has twelve minutes. The log is only useful if it gets read.

This guide covers what to share, which format works, how to get data out of common apps, and how to keep your privacy while you do it.

## Key takeaways

- Lead with a one-page summary and bring the full log as backup. Clinicians skim; make the important parts easy to spot.
- Written records beat memory. In one study, recall of stool consistency matched a one-week diary only 12.5% of the time [2].
- Ask your clinic how they want files: printed, uploaded to the patient portal, or brought on paper.
- Data you put in a consumer app you chose is generally outside HIPAA. Check the app's privacy policy and sharing settings [3][4].
- Share patterns as questions ("Could onion be a trigger?"), not conclusions.

## Why share your food and symptom log at all?

A log turns "it's been bad for a while" into dates, counts and timing, which is what doctors use to decide on tests and treatment. AHRQ advises patients to bring medical information and written questions to appointments [1].

Memory is a weak substitute. In a study of 100 patients with fecal incontinence, recall agreed with a one-week stool diary for only 30% of patients on bowel movement count, 54% on urgency and 12.5% on stool consistency [2]. In patient forums, many people say they felt taken more seriously once they arrived with a record instead of impressions. That matches the first rule of any appointment: make the important facts easy to find.

## What should the one-page summary include?

The summary should answer the questions a clinician asks in the first few minutes, in the order they're likely to ask them. Here's a template you can copy:

| Section | What to write |
|---|---|
| Main concern | One sentence: "Loose stools and cramping after meals for 3 months." |
| Started | Date or month, and anything that happened around then (illness, travel, new medicine) |
| Bowel pattern | Average frequency, typical Bristol type(s) [6], range, urgency, straining |
| Symptoms | Which ones, how many days out of the period, typical severity (0–10), timing relative to meals |
| Red flags | Blood, black stool, weight loss, night-time symptoms, fever: say "none" if none |
| Possible patterns | 1–3 observations phrased as questions |
| What you've tried | Diet changes, supplements, medicines, and the result |
| Medicines & supplements | Full list with doses |
| Your questions | Top 3 |

**Illustrative completed summary** (example data):

> **Period covered:** 1–28 Sept (28 days)
> **Main concern:** Urgent loose stools and lower-belly cramps, since June.
> **Bowel pattern:** 2–4 BMs/day; Bristol 6 on 19 of 28 days; type 7 on 4 days; urgency on 15 days.
> **Symptoms:** Cramps on 14 of 28 days, typically 4/10, usually within 2 h of lunch or dinner. Bloating on 10 days.
> **Red flags:** No blood, no black stool, no night-time symptoms. Weight stable.
> **Possible patterns (to discuss):** Worse on 6 of 7 days with onion/garlic-heavy dinners? Better on weekends?
> **Tried:** Psyllium 1 tsp/day for 2 weeks, slightly firmer stools. Lactose-free milk for 2 weeks, no change.
> **Questions:** Do I need celiac or calprotectin tests? Is a dietitian referral right for me? What should make me call sooner?

For a printable version of the symptom side, use our [GI symptom checklist](/blog/gi-symptom-checklist). Our guide to a [symptom diary your doctor will use](/blog/symptom-diary-for-doctor) explains how to log so the summary is easy to write.

## Which format is best: PDF, printout or portal?

Use whatever your clinician can open quickly in the room. When in doubt, bring a printout *and* have a PDF ready.

| Format | Pros | Cons | Best for |
|---|---|---|---|
| Printed one-pager | Instant, no tech, easy to annotate | Can get lost; may not reach your chart | Every first visit |
| PDF via patient portal | Can be added to your record; reviewed ahead of time | Not every practice accepts uploads; file size limits | Follow-ups; when the office asks for it |
| Email | Easy | Often discouraged for health info; may not reach the clinician | Only if your practice says it's fine |
| Showing your phone | Full detail on hand | Slow; hard to skim; privacy of other app data | Answering a specific follow-up question |
| Raw spreadsheet | Complete | Rarely read in an appointment | Research-minded clinicians who ask for it |

Practical tips:
- **Call ahead.** Ask: "Can I upload a one-page symptom summary before my visit, and will the doctor see it?"
- **Name the file clearly**, for example "Lastname_GI_symptom_summary_Sept2026.pdf".
- **Put the summary first** and any detail after it.
- **Bring it to every visit** in the same format, so changes are easy to compare.

## How do you export a food diary from an app?

Most diary apps offer some combination of a printable report, a PDF, a data export or sharing with another user. Check the app's help center for the current method, as these change.

**MyFitnessPal.** As of 19 October 2026, its help center describes three options: a printable report from the website's Food section (choose a date range, then print or save as PDF), diary sharing with another MyFitnessPal user, and a data export for Premium subscribers [5]. Calorie apps capture food well but usually not stool type or gut symptoms, so add those separately.

**Paper or notes apps.** Photograph or scan the pages, then write the one-page summary by hand. The summary matters more than the scan.

## What about privacy when you share health app data?

Your doctor's office is covered by HIPAA. A consumer app you chose yourself generally is not. HHS explains that once health information reaches an app that isn't a HIPAA covered entity or business associate, at your direction, HIPAA no longer protects that copy [3]. Most health apps outside HIPAA do fall under the FTC's Health Breach Notification Rule. Since 2024 amendments, that rule requires them to notify you of breaches, including sharing your health data without your authorization [4].

Before you share:
- **Read the app's privacy policy**, especially who processes your data and whether it's used for advertising.
- **Share the minimum.** A PDF summary exposes less than giving someone access to your whole account.
- **Use the portal** rather than personal email if your practice offers it.
- **Check photo settings.** If you're uncomfortable sharing stool photos, use manual entry. Get Regular offers manual Bristol entry and an enhanced privacy mode for stool photos.
- **Remove what's irrelevant**, such as unrelated notes, before printing.

## Sharing with a dietitian

Dietitians usually want more food detail than doctors: portions, ingredients, meal times and drinks. Bring the same one-page summary plus 1–2 weeks of full food entries. See [what to bring to a dietitian appointment](/blog/dietitian-appointment-prep).

## When not to wait for a summary

A log is for patterns, not emergencies. Don't wait to compile one if you have blood in your stool, black or tarry stools, severe or constant belly pain, persistent vomiting, a high fever, signs of dehydration, or weight loss you can't explain. Contact your doctor or get urgent care. Our [gastroenterologist appointment prep guide](/resources/gastroenterologist-appointment-prep) includes the full red-flag list.

## FAQ

**Should I bring my food diary to the doctor?**
Yes, but lead with a one-page summary. Most clinicians won't read pages of raw entries in a short appointment, but they'll use a clear overview and may ask to see the detail.

**Can my doctor see my health app data directly?**
Usually not, unless you share it. Consumer apps generally aren't connected to your medical record. Export or print a summary and give it to your clinician, or upload it to your patient portal if your practice allows that.

**Can you print a food diary from MyFitnessPal?**
According to MyFitnessPal's help center (checked October 2026), you can create a printable report from the Food section of its website and print it or save it as a PDF. Data export is listed as a Premium feature [5].

**Is it safe to email my symptom log to my doctor?**
Ask your practice first. Many prefer the patient portal because email is less secure. If you do email, send only what's needed and confirm the right address.
