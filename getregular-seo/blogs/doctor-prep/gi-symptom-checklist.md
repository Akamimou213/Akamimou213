> **Quick answer:** A GI symptom checklist puts what your gastroenterologist needs on one page: when symptoms started, bowel frequency and Bristol stool type, pain location and timing, red flags (blood, black stool, weight loss, night symptoms), medicines and supplements, family history, what you've tried, and your top three questions. Fill it in from 2–4 weeks of tracking, not from memory, and bring it to your appointment.

The best GI symptom tracker is the one that gets the right facts in front of your doctor. This page is a printable checklist you can fill in before a gastroenterologist appointment, plus a short guide to filling it in accurately.

Print it, or copy it into your notes app. Then spend a few weeks gathering the answers.

## Key takeaways

- One page beats ten. Lead with the facts a gastroenterologist asks first.
- Use the Bristol Stool Scale (types 1–7) to describe stool form [1]. It's the shared language of GI clinics.
- Fill in red flags explicitly, including "none". The ACG lists blood, black stool, unintentional weight loss, older age at onset and family history as alarm features [2].
- Base the answers on 2–4 weeks of tracking, not recall.
- Bring a complete list of medicines and supplements, including over-the-counter ones [6].

## What should a GI symptom checklist include?

A GI symptom checklist should cover the main symptom and its timeline, bowel habits, pain details, red flags, background history and your questions. Those are the areas clinicians ask about when assessing abdominal symptoms [3]:

| Section | Why your gastroenterologist needs it |
|---|---|
| Timeline | New vs long-standing problems are investigated differently |
| Bowel habits | Frequency and Bristol type changes point toward constipation, diarrhea or mixed patterns [1][4][5] |
| Pain | Location, character and what relieves it narrow the possible causes [3] |
| Red flags | Change how urgently, and with which tests, you're investigated [2] |
| Medicines & supplements | Many cause gut side effects; some affect tests |
| History | Family history of colorectal cancer, IBD or celiac disease changes risk [2] |
| What you've tried | Saves repeating failed treatments |
| Questions | Ensures your priorities get covered |

## How do you fill in the checklist accurately?

Fill it in from a log, not from memory, and use the same scales throughout. A few tips for the trickier sections:

**Bristol type.** Pick the type that best matches most of your stools over the period, and note the range ("mostly 6, sometimes 7"). Stool form on the Bristol scale relates to how quickly stool moves through your gut [1]. Our guide on [how to describe your poop to a doctor](/blog/how-to-describe-poop-to-doctor) has examples.

**Frequency thresholds.** Write down actual numbers, not "a lot" or "not much". For reference, NIDDK describes constipation symptoms as including fewer than three bowel movements a week and hard, dry or lumpy stools [4], and describes diarrhea as loose, watery stools three or more times a day [5].

**Pain.** Location, character, timing and what changes it are the details clinicians ask about [3]. If pain is a main symptom, see [how to describe abdominal pain to your doctor](/blog/how-to-describe-abdominal-pain).

**Red flags.** Don't skip this section. Writing "none" is useful information. If you tick any box, mention it at the very start of the appointment.

**Illustrative completed section 2** (example data):

> Usual frequency before: 1/day. Now: 3–4/day for 7 weeks. Bristol mostly 6, range 5–7. ☑ Urgency ☑ Mucus. No accidents. No days without a BM.

## What should you bring with the checklist?

Bring the completed checklist plus anything that supports it:

- Your symptom and food log, or its summary
- Results and reports from previous tests
- Your medicine bottles or a full list, as AHRQ recommends [6]
- Insurance card and referral, if your plan requires one
- A support person, if you'd like help remembering what's said

For a fuller pre-visit plan, including what to expect at the appointment, use our [gastroenterologist appointment prep guide](/resources/gastroenterologist-appointment-prep).

## How to track your answers before the appointment

Start 2–4 weeks before your visit. Each day, log bowel movements (time, Bristol type, urgency, blood or mucus), symptoms with a 0–10 rating and the time, meals with times, and notes on medicines, sleep, stress or your period. At the end, count: how many days with each symptom, the most common Bristol type, and any red flags.

## When to see a doctor sooner

If you tick any red flag in section 4, don't wait for a routine appointment. Call your doctor's office and say which one. Get help right away for constipation with rectal bleeding, constant belly pain, inability to pass gas, vomiting or fever [4], or diarrhea with black or bloody stools, severe pain, high fever or signs of dehydration [5]. ## FAQ

**What is a GI symptom tracker?**
A GI symptom tracker is a log of digestive symptoms over time: bowel movements, stool type, pain, bloating and other symptoms, with their timing. It can be paper, a spreadsheet or an app. Its value is turning weeks of experience into numbers a doctor can act on.

**What should I tell a gastroenterologist at my first appointment?**
Lead with your main concern and when it started, then any red flags, then your bowel pattern and pain details, then medicines and what you've tried. The checklist above follows that order.

**Do I need to fast or stop eating gluten before a GI appointment?**
Not usually for a consultation, but follow any instructions from the clinic. Don't start a gluten-free diet before celiac testing, because it can affect the results [7].

**Can I use this checklist for a primary care visit?**
Yes. The same facts help your primary care doctor decide on first tests and whether you need a referral.
