> **Quick answer:** Constant bloating is most often linked to how your gut handles food and gas: food intolerances (lactose, fructose, fructans), constipation, IBS or a sensitive gut, swallowed air, hormonal shifts, and a reflex that pushes the belly out after meals. Less often it signals celiac disease, SIBO or, rarely, something serious such as ovarian cancer. Bloating that's persistent, worsening, or comes with weight loss, bleeding, vomiting or early fullness needs a doctor's visit.

If you're reading this, you may be tired of hearing "everyone gets bloated." You might look six months pregnant by dinner, own two sizes of jeans, or feel self-conscious about something nobody else seems to notice.

Your experience is real. Bloating is one of the most common reasons people see a gastroenterologist [2]. In a survey of nearly 89,000 Americans, about 1 in 7 had been bloated in the past week, women more than men [1]. And most who were bloated had never sought care for it, some because they were uncomfortable bringing it up [1].

## Key takeaways

- Bloating is a feeling (pressure, fullness); distension is a visible size change. They often, but not always, go together [2][8].
- Distension in IBS can reach 12 cm over a day [3]; it comes and goes, which is how it differs from body fat.
- Common causes include carbohydrate intolerances, constipation, IBS and gut hypersensitivity, and a belly-wall reflex [2][11].
- Most people don't need scans; tests are for red flags, worsening symptoms or an abnormal exam [2].
- Persistent bloating in women, especially over 50, should be checked for ovarian causes [6].

## Why do I look pregnant when I'm bloated?

You can look pregnant when bloated because of distension: a measurable increase in belly size. The AGA defines bloating as a sensation of fullness, pressure, trapped gas or tightness, and distension as a visible increase in girth, often described as "like a balloon" or "like being pregnant" [2].

Distension is not "imaginary" or "just weight." When researchers measured belly girth continuously for 24 hours in women with IBS, about half had distension beyond normal, reaching up to 12 cm [3]. That's enough to change how clothes fit by evening.

Surprisingly, it often isn't a huge amount of gas. In many people, the diaphragm contracts downward and the front belly muscles relax after a meal, pushing the abdomen forward [2][4]. The AGA notes this can happen with only about a 10% rise in gut gas [2]. It's a reflex, not a failure of willpower, and it can be treated.

**Bloating vs belly fat:** fat doesn't swing in and out between morning and night. If your belly is flatter on waking and bigger by evening, that's bloating and distension. If it's there all day, every day, and hasn't changed with meals or bowel movements, that's a reason to see a doctor (see red flags below).

## Why am I so bloated all the time?

Constant bloating usually has one or more of these causes, according to gastroenterology reviews [2][8][11]:

**1. Carbohydrates you don't absorb well.** Lactose, fructose, sugar alcohols and fructans (in wheat, onion, garlic) can ferment in the colon and cause gas and bloating [2]. In one large IBS cohort, fructose intolerance (60%) was even more common than lactose intolerance (51%) [2]. A short 2-week elimination is usually the first test [2].

**2. Constipation.** A backlog of stool is a very common driver of bloating. The AGA recommends treating constipation when it's present, and suggests pelvic-floor testing if difficult evacuation may be involved [2].

**3. IBS and a sensitive gut.** Functional bloating on its own affects about 3.5% of people (4.6% of women, 2.4% of men), and bloating is far more common, over 50%, in people with IBS, constipation or functional dyspepsia [2]. A sensitive gut feels normal amounts of gas more intensely [2].

**4. The belly-wall reflex (abdominophrenic dyssynergia).** Described above; often worse after meals. Diaphragmatic breathing and certain medicines are used to treat it [2].

**5. Swallowed air.** Eating fast, gum, fizzy drinks and smoking add air [8].

**6. Hormones.** Bloating can come with your period, pregnancy or perimenopause [5]. See [IBS and your period](/blog/ibs-and-period) for cycle tracking.

**7. Celiac disease.** Blood tests can rule it out; the AGA recommends serologic testing in people with bloating, ideally while still eating gluten [2].

**8. SIBO.** Small intestinal bacterial overgrowth can cause bloating, but the AGA doesn't recommend routine testing unless there are clear risk factors or severe symptoms [2].

**9. Less common but important.** Gastroparesis, ascites (fluid in the abdomen), bowel obstruction, and cancers of the ovary, colon or pancreas can present with bloating [5][8]. Experts stress that these "organic" causes should always be considered first [10].

## When to see a doctor about bloating

Most bloating doesn't need scans or scopes. The AGA advises imaging or endoscopy only for alarm features, recent worsening, or an abnormal exam [2]. These are the alarm features:

**See a doctor promptly if you have bloating plus:**
- Unintended weight loss
- Vomiting, especially persistent
- Blood in your stool, black or tarry stools, or vomiting blood
- Recent-onset or worsening abdominal pain or indigestion
- Chronic diarrhea
- Fever, or symptoms that wake you at night
- A family history of GI cancer, celiac disease or inflammatory bowel disease [2]
- Bloating that lasts more than a week, is very painful, or keeps getting worse [5]

**Especially for women:** the AGA notes that bloating and abdominal fullness are often presenting symptoms of ovarian cancer, with the highest risk in women 50 and older [2]. NICE advises testing when a woman, especially aged 50 or over, has any of these on a persistent or frequent basis, particularly more than 12 times a month [6]:
- persistent abdominal distension ("bloating")
- feeling full quickly and/or loss of appetite
- pelvic or abdominal pain
- needing to pee more urgently or more often

NICE also recommends ovarian testing for anyone aged 50 or over with new IBS-like symptoms in the past year, because IBS rarely starts at that age [6]. The NHS adds that you should tell your GP about any family history of ovarian or breast cancer, and go back if symptoms don't go away or get worse [7].

Ovarian cancer is uncommon, and most persistent bloating turns out to be something else. But this is exactly the situation where you should ask, and be heard.

**Screening reminder:** the USPSTF recommends average-risk adults start colorectal cancer screening at age 45 [9]. If you're 45 or older and haven't been screened, raise it at the same visit.

## How to track constant bloating

A two-week "bloat map" gives your doctor what a single appointment can't: your pattern.

**Record each day:**
1. Bloating on waking and at bedtime (0–10); is your belly visibly bigger?
2. Meals and drinks, with times and rough portions
3. Bowel movements and Bristol stool type
4. Period or cycle day, if relevant
5. Any red-flag symptoms (weight change, blood, vomiting, night symptoms, early fullness, urinary changes)
6. Medicines and supplements

## FAQ

### Why am I always bloated even when I don't eat much?
Constipation, a sensitive gut, swallowed air and hormones can all cause bloating independent of portion size [2][5]. If you feel full after only a few bites, or have lost weight, see a doctor and read [bloating after eating very little](/blog/bloating-after-eating-very-little).

### Is constant bloating a sign of something serious?
Usually not, but sometimes. Persistent bloating with weight loss, bleeding, vomiting, early fullness, pelvic pain or urinary changes, especially in women over 50, needs prompt evaluation [2][6].

### Do probiotics help constant bloating?
The AGA's 2023 guidance advises against using probiotics to treat bloating and distension [2]. Ask your clinician before spending money on them for this.

### Can stress or anxiety make me bloated?
Stress can heighten gut sensitivity, and brain-gut therapies like CBT and gut-directed hypnotherapy are recommended options for bloating [2]. That doesn't mean it's "all in your head"; it means the gut-brain connection is part of the treatment.

### How do I get rid of bloating fast?
Gentle walking, staying upright after meals, loosening tight clothing and slow belly breathing can ease an episode [2]. If you need relief most days, look for the cause with a log and a clinician.

### Should I see a gastroenterologist or a gynecologist?
Start with your primary care clinician, who can examine you, order basic tests, and refer you. Mention gynecological symptoms (pelvic pain, urinary changes, period changes) so both possibilities are considered.
