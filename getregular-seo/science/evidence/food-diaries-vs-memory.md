# What the research says about food diaries vs memory

> **Short answer:** Memory is a weak guide to gut symptoms. In a Swedish study, what people recalled on a questionnaire agreed poorly with what they wrote in a 7- or 14-day diary, especially for complex questions such as whether pain eases after a bowel movement [1]. A diary is a measuring tool, not a treatment: it can surface food patterns, but a suspected trigger still needs testing, because blinded trials show beliefs about trigger foods don't always hold up [5].

## The evidence at a glance

| Study | Type | Who | What it found | Limits |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Jones et al., *Eur J Gastroenterol Hepatol* 2019 [1] | Comparative study | 238 adults from the general population and 503 primary-care patients (10 health centres) | Agreement between recall questionnaires and 7- or 14-day stool and symptom diaries was generally poor. It was best for a simple item (any abdominal pain) and worse for complex ones (pain relieved by defecation) | Neither method is a gold standard; Rome III questions |
| Walter et al., *Dig Dis Sci* 2011 [2] | Population study with colonoscopy and 1-week diary | 268 adults from the Swedish population register; 23 excluded for organic disease | 2,194 bowel movements and 370 pain episodes logged. In the 28 people (12%) who met Rome II IBS criteria, only 4% of pain episodes improved after a bowel movement, and 29% started or worsened after a meal | One week of diaries; older Rome II criteria |
| Böhn et al., *Am J Gastroenterol* 2013 [3] | Questionnaire study | 197 IBS patients | 84% linked symptoms to at least one food. Most often named: fried and fatty foods (52%), dairy (49%), beans and lentils (36%). More trigger foods went with more severe symptoms and lower quality of life | Self-report only; no food challenges, so the beliefs weren't tested |
| Zia et al., *Clin Transl Gastroenterol* 2016 [4] | Pilot feasibility study | 11 IBS patients logging meals and symptoms in an app for 2 weeks | 24-hour compliance was 90% for meals and 94% for symptoms. 73% showed at least one strong link between a nutrient and later symptoms, and the patterns differed from person to person. Most did not have a clinically significant drop in symptom scores | Very small; no control group; links were statistical, not confirmed by challenge |
| Seiler et al., *Lancet Gastroenterol Hepatol* 2025 [5] | Randomized, double-blind, sham-controlled crossover trial | 28 adults with IBS who believed a gluten-free diet helped them | Symptoms worsened after wheat in 39%, gluten in 36% and a gluten-free sham in 29%, with no significant difference. The authors concluded expectations played a major role | Small, single centre, 7-day challenges |

## What this means

- **Write it down when it happens.** Recall and diaries disagree, and the gap grows as questions get more complex [1].
- **Memory can overcall links.** Recall-based criteria may overstate the link between pain and bowel movements compared with a diary [2].
- **Triggers seem personal.** In the app pilot, food–symptom patterns differed between people [4], so a generic "avoid" list may not fit you.
- **Treat a pattern as a lead, not a verdict.** Most people with IBS name trigger foods [3], but in a blinded test, people who believed gluten affected them reacted about as often to a sham [5]. Test a suspected trigger with a clinician or dietitian before cutting foods for good.

## What's still uncertain

- No large trial shows that keeping a diary, by itself, improves IBS symptoms. In the small pilot, most participants did not improve [4].
- Diaries are self-report too. The comparison study measured agreement, not which method is right [1].
- The best logging period isn't settled. Studies used 1 week [2] to 2 weeks [1][4].
- Expectation can shape symptoms [5], and a diary can't separate that from a true food effect. Blinded re-testing can.

## Track it

Log meals, symptoms and stool type as they happen for about 2 weeks. Note portion size and timing. Then look for repeats, not one-offs, and bring the log to your appointment.
