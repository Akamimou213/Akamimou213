# What the research says about gluten without celiac disease

> **Short answer:** Many people with IBS feel better off gluten, but blinded trials find that gluten itself triggers symptoms in only a minority of them. In a 2025 trial of people with IBS who believed a gluten-free diet helped, symptoms worsened about as often after a gluten-free sham (29%) as after gluten (36%) or wheat (39%) [1]. Celiac disease needs to be checked before cutting gluten: the ACG *suggests* celiac blood testing for people with IBS and diarrhea [5].

## The evidence at a glance

| Study | Type | Who | What it found | Limits |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Seiler et al., *Lancet Gastroenterol Hepatol* 2025 [1] | Randomized, double-blind, sham-controlled crossover trial | 28 adults with Rome IV IBS who had improved on a gluten-free diet | Worsening of at least 50 points on the IBS symptom score: wheat 39%, gluten 36%, sham 29%. No significant difference. Authors: expectations played a major role, and only some patients may benefit from restriction | Small, single centre, 7-day challenges |
| Biesiekierski et al., *Gastroenterology* 2013 [2] | Double-blind crossover trial | 37 adults with self-reported gluten sensitivity and IBS, celiac disease excluded | Symptoms improved on a reduced-FODMAP diet, then worsened to a similar degree with gluten or whey protein. Gluten-specific effects in only 8%. No diet-specific changes in any biomarker | Small; an order effect was seen |
| Skodje et al., *Gastroenterology* 2018 [3] | Double-blind, placebo-controlled crossover trial | 59 adults on a self-started gluten-free diet, celiac disease excluded | Fructans (2.1 g) raised symptom scores more than gluten (5.7 g). Gluten did not differ from placebo. Highest scores: 24 people on fructan, 22 on placebo, 13 on gluten | One centre; 7-day challenges; one type of food bar |
| Molina-Infante & Carroccio, *Clin Gastroenterol Hepatol* 2017 [4] | Analysis of 10 double-blind, placebo-controlled gluten-challenge trials | 1,312 adults | Only 38 of 231 people with suspected gluten sensitivity (16%) had gluten-specific symptoms, and 40% of those also reacted to placebo | Trials varied in dose (2–52 g/day), length (1 day to 6 weeks) and placebo |
| Lacy et al., ACG IBS guideline, *Am J Gastroenterol* 2021 [5] | Clinical practice guideline (GRADE) | Adults with IBS | *Suggests* celiac blood testing in IBS with diarrhea. *Recommends* a limited trial of a low-FODMAP diet | Covers IBS testing and treatment broadly, not gluten sensitivity itself |
| Rubio-Tapia et al., ACG celiac guideline, *Am J Gastroenterol* 2023 [6] | Clinical practice guideline | People with suspected celiac disease | Celiac disease is a permanent immune response to gluten. Blood antibody tests help screen; a biopsy is needed to confirm in most patients | Focused on celiac disease, not gluten sensitivity |

## What this means

- **Feeling better off gluten doesn't prove gluten was the cause.** In blinded tests, people reacted to a sham almost as often as to gluten [1], and many gluten "reactors" also reacted to placebo [4].
- **Fructans may matter more than gluten for some people.** Wheat foods that contain gluten often contain fructans, a type of FODMAP. In one trial, fructans, not gluten, raised symptoms [3]. The ACG recommends a limited low-FODMAP trial for IBS [5].
- **A true gluten-specific reaction looks uncommon, not impossible.** Rates ranged from 8% [2] to 16% [4] of people who believed they were sensitive.
- **Test for celiac disease first.** Celiac tests look for the immune response to gluten [6], and the ACG suggests testing people with IBS and diarrhea [5]. Ask about testing before you cut gluten. ## What's still uncertain

- There is no biomarker for non-celiac gluten sensitivity [3]. Trials found no diet-specific biomarker changes [2].
- Trials are small and short, and they differ in gluten dose, length and placebo, including placebos that themselves contained fermentable carbs [4].
- Who the minority with a real gluten or wheat effect are, and how to identify them, is still open [1].

## Track it

Before changing your diet, log wheat and gluten foods, symptoms and stool type for about 2 weeks, and ask about celiac testing. If you later try cutting back, keep logging so you can see what actually changed.
