# What the research says about food poisoning and IBS

> **Short answer:** Yes, a gut infection such as food poisoning can be followed by IBS, though most people in the studies did not develop it. A 2017 meta-analysis of 45 studies found that 10.1% of people developed IBS within 12 months of an infectious enteritis, and their risk was 4.2-fold higher than in people without such an infection [1]. Risk was higher in women, after more severe illness, with antibiotic use during the infection, and with anxiety or depression [1].

## The evidence at a glance

| Study | Type | Who | What it found | Limits |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Klem et al., *Gastroenterology* 2017 [1] | Systematic review and meta-analysis | 45 cohort studies, 21,421 people with infectious enteritis, followed 3 months to 10 years | IBS in 10.1% at 12 months and 14.5% after more than 12 months. Risk 4.2-fold higher in the first 12 months, 2.3-fold higher after that. 41.9% after protozoa or parasites vs 13.8% after bacterial infection | "Considerable" heterogeneity between studies; observational data |
| Barbara et al., Rome Foundation working team, *Gastroenterology* 2019 [2] | Systematic review with Delphi consensus | People with post-infection IBS (PI-IBS) | PI-IBS develops in about 10% after infectious enteritis. Risk factors: female sex, younger age, psychological distress before or during the illness, and severity. "No evidence-based, effective pharmacologic strategies" for treatment | Treatment algorithm is consensus-based; mechanisms "incompletely understood"; several authors report industry ties |

## What this means

- **It's a real, recognized pattern.** A meta-analysis and a Rome Foundation report (with an overlapping author) both put the rate at about 1 in 10 after infectious enteritis [1][2]. Foodborne illness affects about 15% of the US population each year [1].
- **The type of infection matters.** Rates were much higher after protozoal or parasitic infections (41.9%) than after bacterial ones (13.8%) [1].
- **Some factors raise the odds.** Women, people with severe illness, antibiotic users during the infection, and people with anxiety, depression or somatization had higher risk [1]. Younger age was also a risk factor [2]. These raise odds; they don't decide who gets IBS.
- **Timing helps your doctor.** Symptoms that started after a gut infection are a useful clue. The Rome Foundation report offers a diagnosis and treatment algorithm for clinicians [2]. See [doctor visits](/learn/doctor-visits).

## What's still uncertain

- **Why it happens.** Changes in the gut microbiome and in epithelial, serotonin and immune factors appear to be involved, but these are "incompletely understood" [2].
- **Best treatment.** There are no evidence-based, effective drug treatments specific to PI-IBS; current advice is consensus-based [2].
- **How long it lasts.** Studies followed people for 3 months to 10 years. IBS was still found in 14.5% after more than 12 months, but the abstract doesn't report how many people recover over time [1].
- **How exact the numbers are.** Studies differed a lot in design and setting, so the pooled rates are estimates [1].

## Track it

If gut symptoms started or changed after a stomach bug or food poisoning, note the date of the illness, any antibiotics, and how your stool type (Bristol scale) and symptoms have changed since. A clear timeline is useful to bring to a doctor.
