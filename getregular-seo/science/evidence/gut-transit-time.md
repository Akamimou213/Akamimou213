# What the research says about gut transit time

> **Short answer:** There's no single normal number. In one marker study of 43 healthy adults, the normal range for whole-gut transit ran from 12 to 120 hours [1]. Colonic transit alone averaged about 35 hours in 73 healthy adults, and women tended to be slower than men [2].

## The evidence at a glance

| Study | Type | Who | What it found | Limits |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Evans et al., *Int J Colorectal Dis* 1992 [1] | Normal-range study, radiopaque markers | 25 healthy men and 18 healthy women | Men's and women's results were similar enough to combine into one normal range. Its limits were 12 and 120 hours | Small; one London hospital; tracks plastic markers, not food |
| Metcalf et al., *Gastroenterology* 1987 [2] | Method study, radiopaque markers | 73 healthy adults | Total colonic transit 35.0 ± 2.1 h (mean ± SE): right colon 11.3 h, left colon 11.4 h, rectosigmoid 12.4 h. Men were faster through the whole colon. Age and a small fiber dose had no significant effect | Colon only; reports a mean, not a full range; 1980s data |
| Wang et al., *Aliment Pharmacol Ther* 2015 [3] | Multicenter study, wireless motility capsule | 215 healthy volunteers, USA and Sweden | Women's whole-gut transit was longer (median 263 minutes). Test protocol and country changed results. Whole-gut and colonic times clustered at values 24 hours apart | Capsule, not food; one author from the capsule maker; normal values aren't in the abstract |
| Nandhra et al., *Neurogastroenterol Motil* 2020 [4] | Normative study, 3D-Transit electromagnetic capsule | 111 healthy volunteers, UK and Denmark, ages 21–88 | Older age was linked with longer colonic and whole-gut transit; higher BMI with shorter whole-gut transit. Times clustered about 24 hours apart | One author from the device maker; normal values aren't in the abstract |
| Degen & Phillips, *Gut* 1996 [5] | Repeat-measure physiology study | 32 healthy volunteers, standardized diet and exercise, tested twice | Colonic transit was faster in men. Group averages matched across tests, but single tests in the same person sometimes varied considerably | Small; one center |
| Asnicar et al., *Gut* 2021 [6] | Single-arm study within ZOE's PREDICT 1, blue dye marker | 863 healthy adults | Blue-dye transit time was linked to gut microbiome makeup (AUC 0.82 for telling transit classes apart), more strongly than stool form or frequency | Sponsored by ZOE (Zoe Global Ltd), which helped design the study; several authors are ZOE employees or consultants. Shows microbiome links, not health outcomes |
| Lewis & Heaton, *Scand J Gastroenterol* 1997 [7] | Volunteer study with radiopaque markers | 66 volunteers | Stool form correlated with whole-gut transit time (r = −0.54), better than frequency (r = 0.35) | Volunteers; moderate correlation |

## What this means

- **"Normal" is wide.** Healthy people spanned 12 to 120 hours in one normal-range study [1], so day-to-day differences are expected.
- **The colon is a big part of it.** Colonic transit alone averaged about 35 hours in healthy adults [2].
- **Sex, age and body size shift it.** Women tended to be slower [2][3][5]. Older age went with longer transit and higher BMI with shorter [4].
- **One test is a rough snapshot.** Results cluster in 24-hour steps [3][4], and the same person can vary between tests [5].

## What's still uncertain

- What transit time is best for health isn't established. The blue-dye study linked transit to microbiome makeup, not to health outcomes [6].
- Numbers depend on the method (markers, capsules, dye) and the test protocol [3], so values from different studies aren't directly comparable.
- The blue-dye study was run with ZOE, a commercial company. That doesn't make it wrong, but it isn't independent [6].
- Most normal-range studies are small and in healthy volunteers [1][2][5].

## Track it

You can't see transit time directly. Stool form is the everyday stand-in with the best evidence [7]: harder types suggest slower transit, looser types faster. Log your Bristol type for 2 weeks and watch the trend. If you try a colored-food test at home, treat it as one rough reading, since single measurements vary [5].
