# What the research says about gut microbiome tests

> **Short answer:** Not yet as a guide to diagnosis or treatment. A 2025 international expert consensus found that evidence for the clinical usefulness of microbiome testing is scarce, said there is "insufficient evidence to widely recommend the routine use" of these tests in clinical practice, and discouraged people from ordering them without a clinician's recommendation [1]. When a US government lab sent the same standardized stool material to seven direct-to-consumer services, results differed between companies about as much as stool from different people differs [2].

|---|---|---|---|---|
| Porcari et al., *Lancet Gastroenterol Hepatol* 2025 [1] | International consensus statement (expert panel, Delphi voting) | Microbiome testing in clinical practice | Evidence for clinical use is "scarce." Consumer tests are sold "without any consensus on their regulation or any proven value in clinical practice." The panel discouraged ordering tests without a clinical recommendation, and discouraged testing companies from giving treatment advice. Any change in care should be made by the patient's own clinician | Expert consensus, not a trial. Several panel members disclose ties to microbiome or nutrition companies |
| Servetas et al. (NIST), *Commun Biol* 2026 [2] | Laboratory evaluation | 7 direct-to-consumer gut microbiome services, all given a standardized NIST-made human stool material | "Major discrepancies" within and across services. Variability between providers was on the same scale as variability between different donors. Authors blame differing methods and weak quality control | Tests one reference material, not people's health outcomes; company names not given in the abstract |
| Rodriguez et al., *Microbiome* 2024 [3] | Expert workshop review of real kits | 1 stool sample sent to 6 companies (5 in Europe, 1 in the US); 21 experts from 8 countries reviewed the reports | Experts flagged "over-promising messages," unclear methods and unreliable results, and judged report advice "premature" and of "limited clinical utility" | European focus; small number of kits |

## What the consensus actually says

The consensus doesn't call microbiome science useless. It sets minimum standards for when testing is done, and it is clear that routine clinical use isn't supported yet [1]. From the full text, the panel agreed that:

- Test providers should make customers "clearly aware of the currently limited evidence" for clinical use.
- Patients shouldn't order tests directly "without a clinical recommendation."
- There is "not enough information to report strict healthy reference ranges" for species, and "insufficient evidence" to include any dysbiosis index. Reporting the Firmicutes-to-Bacteroidetes ratio is discouraged.
- Multiplex PCR and bacterial culture "cannot be considered microbiome testing."
- Testing companies shouldn't give treatment advice based on the results.

## What this means

- **A consumer test isn't a reliable guide to what's wrong or what to eat.** There are no agreed healthy ranges, and the panel discouraged treatment advice from test providers [1].
- **The same stool can get different answers.** In the NIST study, which company ran the test mattered about as much as whose stool it was [2].
- **"Personalized" advice in reports is ahead of the science.** Experts reviewing real reports called it premature [3].
- **If your doctor suggests one, ask what question it answers.** The consensus frames testing as part of clinical care, with your clinician deciding what results mean, while stressing that the evidence is still limited [1].

## What's still uncertain

- Which conditions, if any, will benefit from microbiome testing. The panel called for dedicated studies before routine use [1].
- Whether standards will make results comparable. NIST's authors say standards are needed first [2].
- US regulation: these tests "straddle the line" between medical devices and wellness products [2].

## Track it
