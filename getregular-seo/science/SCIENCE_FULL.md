## What does Get Regular track, and why? {#what-we-track}

**We track the four things that describe your digestion day to day, because each has research showing it's worth recording.**

| What you log | How | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| **Stool form** | Bristol Stool Scale type 1–7, from a photo or picked by hand | Stool form is a practical guide to how fast stool moves through your gut. In a study at the Bristol Royal Infirmary, changes in stool form tracked changes in measured transit time more closely than changes in how often people went [1] |
| **How often you go** | Each bowel movement is time-stamped; you can log no-go days | It gives your normal range a baseline. Of US adults who say their bowel habits are normal, 95.9% go between 3 times a day and 3 times a week [2]. Knowing your own range makes a change easier to spot |
| **Meals** | Photo or text, with editable ingredients | Food is a common suspect. In a Swedish study of 197 people with IBS, 84% linked their symptoms to at least one food [3]. What people suspect isn't always the real cause, so a meal log lets you test a hunch instead of guessing |
| **Symptoms** | One tap when they happen (bloating, pain, urgency and more) | Memory is unreliable. In population and primary-care samples, what people recalled on a symptom questionnaire often didn't match what they had recorded in a daily diary [4] |

The app also lets you log drinks, supplements, medications, time on the toilet, notes and period dates, which add context to the four core logs. See [how our AI works](/science/how-our-ai-works#inputs) for the full list.

## How does Get Regular's AI work? {#ai}

**The AI reads what you log and summarizes it. It doesn't diagnose.** It estimates ingredients and nutrients from meal photos and descriptions, suggests a Bristol type from stool photos (or you pick the type yourself), and turns your recent logs into daily scores, a Weekly Digest of possible patterns and answers in Gut Check chat. To do this, your logged content is sent to OpenAI's API without your name, email or account ID, per our privacy policy. Photos aren't permanently stored on our servers.

Published studies have tested AI on stool and meal photos with mixed results. Those studies tested other systems, not ours, and Get Regular hasn't published its own accuracy study.

**Read more:** [How Get Regular's AI works: inputs, data flow and limits →](/science/how-our-ai-works)

## What we don't claim {#dont-claim}

*(Render as a bordered box, visible without scrolling on desktop below the "Research" section; never collapsed.)*

> **What we don't claim**
>
> - **We haven't published our own studies.** Every study on this site was run by independent researchers. None of them tested Get Regular.
> - **Our scores and AI aren't clinically validated.** Input from a gastroenterologist isn't the same as a validation study.
> - **Get Regular doesn't diagnose, treat or prevent any condition.** It isn't a medical device and can't tell you whether you have IBS, an intolerance or any other condition.
> - **We don't publish accuracy figures for our AI.** Not until we've tested it and published the method.
> - **A pattern isn't a cause.** Insights describe possible patterns in your own logs, to test and discuss with a clinician.
> - **Our scores aren't population norms.** They summarize your logs and don't rank you against anyone else.
> - **We don't measure your microbiome** or use lab tests.
>

**H1:** How Get Regular's AI works

> **In short:** Get Regular uses AI to read what you log — meal photos and descriptions, stool photos, symptoms, drinks, medications and supplements — and to summarize your recent history as daily scores, a Weekly Digest of possible patterns, and answers in Gut Check chat. To do that, your logged content is sent to OpenAI's API for analysis. The AI can be wrong, it doesn't diagnose anything, and a pattern it finds is a hypothesis to test, not proof. Get Regular hasn't published a study of its own AI's accuracy.

## 3. What the AI does {#what-it-does}

**H2:** What does the AI actually do with my logs?

1. **Reads meal photos and text.** It estimates the ingredients and nutrients in a meal — including fiber and other gut-relevant compounds — and you can edit the ingredient list when it's wrong.
2. **Classifies stool photos on the Bristol Stool Form Scale.** The Bristol scale is a 7-type chart of stool form, developed at the Bristol Royal Infirmary in the UK, that tracks with how fast stool moves through the gut [1]. If you'd rather not take photos, you choose the type yourself. See the [Bristol stool chart](/tools/bristol-stool-chart). **[CONFIRM: can users override the AI's suggested type?]**
3. **Summarizes your recent history** as daily scores (below) and a weekly Weekly Digest that describes possible patterns across your food, stool and symptom history.
4. **Answers questions in Gut Check** (Premium) — for example "what's been different on my bloated days?" or "what should I eat tomorrow?" — using your own logs and core memories.
5. **Suggests things to try** — food and supplement suggestions, meal tweaks, personalized Recipes (Premium) and Experiments (Premium), such as "try eliminating dairy for a week", with a protocol and daily check-ins.

**Language rule we follow:** the app talks about *possible patterns* and *things associated with* your symptoms — never "the cause" or "your trigger".

## 5. Limits {#limits}

**H2:** What can't the AI do?

- **It doesn't diagnose.** It can't tell you whether you have IBS, a food intolerance, celiac disease, inflammatory bowel disease or anything else. Those need a clinician, and sometimes tests [2][3].
- **It can misread photos.** Lighting, angle, sauces, hidden ingredients and portion size all affect meal estimates; lighting, mixed stool types and toilet-bowl water affect stool classification. Studies of the Bristol scale itself show people disagree most around types 2/3 and 5/6 [4] — the borderline cases are hard for software too. Published tests of general-purpose AI on meal photos found large errors in portion and nutrient estimates (see [below](#research)) [11][12].
- **Patterns aren't causes.** A food you eat every day will often appear "before" symptoms by coincidence. Delayed reactions, stress, sleep, your cycle and illness all blur the picture. Treat a pattern as a hypothesis, then test it. See [correlation or coincidence?](/learn/food-symptom-patterns)
- **It only knows what you log.** Gaps, late entries and guesses reduce accuracy.
- **Suggestions aren't medical advice.** Food and supplement suggestions are general. Check with a doctor or pharmacist before starting supplements, especially if you take medication, are pregnant or have a medical condition. Don't cut out several foods without guidance [3].
- **It doesn't spot emergencies.** It won't alert you to red-flag symptoms. If you have blood in your stool, black or tarry stools, severe or constant abdominal pain, unexplained weight loss, persistent vomiting, or high fever, contact a clinician or emergency services [5][6].
- **It's not a FODMAP database** and doesn't know a food's FODMAP content.

**Uncertainty:** **[CONFIRM: does the app show confidence or "not sure" states (e.g., "Food not recognized")? If yes, describe here.]** Since version 2.0.1, a bug that wrongly marked meals as "Food not recognized" was fixed.

## 5a. What published research says about AI and photos {#research}

**H2:** Can AI read a stool photo or a meal photo accurately?

*(Render as a bordered evidence box, same component as the topic-page study tables.)*

> **In short:** Researchers have tested AI on stool photos and meal photos, with mixed results. One stool-photo app agreed well with gastroenterologists in a small study, and general-purpose chatbots identified foods well but misjudged portions and nutrients. **These studies tested other systems, not Get Regular.** Get Regular hasn't published its own accuracy study, so we don't quote an accuracy figure for our app.

|---|---|---|---|
| Pimentel et al., *Am J Gastroenterol* 2022 [10] | A smartphone app's trained AI classifying stool photos on the Bristol scale, in adults with diarrhea-predominant IBS | In the validation phase (14 people), the AI agreed well with two expert gastroenterologists on Bristol type (intraclass correlation 0.78–0.85). It classified daily stool form as constipation, normal or diarrhea better than people's own reports. In a second phase (25 people), AI and self-reports agreed only moderately (0.61) | A different, purpose-trained app (one author is from Dieta Health, the company behind it). | O'Hara et al., *Nutrients* 2025 [11] | ChatGPT-4 estimating foods, weights and 16 nutrients from 114 meal photos from a national diet survey | Identified the foods well (93% precision) and ranked meals by nutrient content adequately. | Fridolfsson et al., *Curr Dev Nutr* 2025 [12] | ChatGPT-4o, Claude 3.5 Sonnet and Gemini 1.5 Pro on 52 standardized food photos in 3 portion sizes | ChatGPT and Claude were off by about 36% on average for energy (calories); Gemini was worse. ## How does the 7-day window work? {#window}

**The score looks back over your past 7 days of entries and counts recent days more than older ones** [6]. That's the whole published definition. In practice it means:

- **One unusual day doesn't define your score.** It's blended with the rest of the week.
- **Changes show up within days.** Because recent entries count more, a shift this week moves the score sooner than an equal shift from six days ago.
- **Logs older than 7 days don't count** toward today's score. Your Backlog keeps the full history.
- **Backdated entries count.** You can log entries up to 7 days in the past, so a forgotten log can still be added to the window. **[CONFIRM: backdated entries recalculate past and current scores]**

## How does the gut-health streak work? {#streak}

**A gut-health streak counts consecutive days with stool in the Bristol 3–4 range** (app release notes). Days you log as no-go days don't break the streak.

**Why we're careful with streaks:** 3–4 is a narrower band than most research uses. Studies often group types 3–5 as normal [2], and many healthy people regularly pass type 2 or type 5 [3]. Breaking a streak doesn't mean something is wrong. It's a motivation tool, not a health threshold.

## Our editorial standard {#standard}

These are the rules every article, tool and science page follows. The full internal version is the Get Regular editorial standard.

- **Answer first, in plain language.** Every page opens with a short, direct answer you could quote on its own.
- **Every health claim has a source.** We cite clinical guidelines first (for example ACG and AGA guidelines, Rome V criteria), then systematic reviews, trials and studies. Government and medical sources (NIDDK, NHS) cover red-flag advice. See [how we choose evidence](/science/evidence#how-we-choose).
- **We read the source before we use its numbers.** Numbers come from the study abstract or full text, re-checked at each review.
- **No invented numbers.** No made-up statistics, user counts, ratings, testimonials or "normal" ranges.
- **Patterns, not causes.** We say a food or habit "may be associated with" symptoms. We never say an app "finds your triggers" or "diagnoses" anything.
- **We only describe features we can confirm.** Product facts come from the App Store listing, our privacy policy and terms. Unconfirmed features aren't mentioned.
- **Red flags on every symptom page.** Each symptom page says when to see a doctor (for example blood in the stool, black or tarry stool, unexplained weight loss, symptoms that wake you at night, or a lasting change in bowel habits).
- **Fair comparisons.** When we compare apps, we state facts with the date we checked and say that Get Regular publishes the article.
- **Dated and reviewed.** Every page shows when it was last updated and, if a clinician reviewed it, who and when.

## What clinical review doesn't mean {#limits}

- **It isn't clinical validation.** Get Regular hasn't published a study testing its scores or AI against clinical measures. See [our research program](/science/research).
- **It doesn't make the app a medical device.** Get Regular is a wellness tracking app. It doesn't diagnose, treat or prevent any condition.
- **It doesn't cover every AI output.** The AI generates insights, suggestions and chat answers for each user. No clinician reviews those one by one. See [how our AI works](/science/how-our-ai-works#limits).

## Why does a tracking app want to do research? {#why}

**Because much of what we know about normal bowel habits comes from questionnaires and short diaries, and people's day-to-day logs could add to it.**

- The largest US study of normal bowel habits used survey answers from about 4,800 adults (NHANES 2009–2010) [1].
- A classic Bristol population study asked people to record just three consecutive bowel movements [2].
- When people recall their gut symptoms on a questionnaire, the answers often don't match what they wrote in a daily diary, especially for complex symptoms such as pain relieved by a bowel movement [3].

People who log in Get Regular record stool type, frequency, meals and symptoms day by day, over weeks or months. With consent, de-identified and pooled, that kind of data might help answer practical questions, for example how much bowel habits vary from week to week in the same person. We don't know yet whether our data will be good enough to answer them. App users aren't a random sample of the population, logging is uneven, and stool types come from AI photo analysis or self-report. Any study we publish will say so.

**What we won't do:** call anything "our research" or "our data shows" until a study is finished and its method is public.

## How would my data be protected? {#privacy}

**Research would use only de-identified, pooled data from people who opted in.** These are the principles we're committing to. **[CONFIRM: legal and privacy review of each principle before publishing]**

1. **Consent first.** Only data from people who turned on research consent. Nothing collected before you opted in is used. **[CONFIRM]**
2. **De-identified.** Names, emails, account IDs and device IDs are removed before analysis. Photos and free-text notes and chat messages are never part of a research dataset. **[CONFIRM]**
3. **Aggregate results only.** We publish group-level numbers, never one person's logs. Results for small groups are suppressed so no one can be singled out. **[CONFIRM: minimum group size, e.g., no cell with fewer than N people]**
4. **Kept apart from marketing.** Research data isn't shared with advertisers or marketing tools. Our privacy policy already says we don't sell, rent or share your personal or health information with advertisers [4].
5. **Independent ethics review for studies.** Any study we publish in a journal will go through an independent ethics review (an institutional review board, or IRB) or an approved exemption, and will say so. **[CONFIRM: plan for IRB review]**
6. **Your rights still apply.** The rights in our privacy policy (including CCPA/CPRA, the Washington My Health My Data Act and GDPR) apply to research data too [4]. **[CONFIRM with legal]**

"De-identified" doesn't mean risk-free. Health data can sometimes be re-identified when combined with other data, which is why we pool it and suppress small groups. Full detail: [privacy policy](/legal/privacy-policy).

## Are the free tools validated? {#tool-validation}

**No. Our free [bloat profile quiz](/tools/why-am-i-bloated) and [stool chart](/tools/bristol-stool-chart) are self-checks, not validated questionnaires.** The quiz matches your answers to the bloating patterns described in the American Gastroenterological Association's 2023 clinical practice update, and sends anyone with a warning sign to a doctor before anything else. It gives no score, has no clinical cut-offs and can't diagnose anything. The stool chart uses the 7-type idea from Lewis & Heaton 1997 in our own words and drawings.

**What validating the quiz would take** (a plan, not something we've done) **[CONFIRM: whether and when the team will run this]**:

## What this page is

This is the library behind everything we write about digestion. When an article says "most people go between three times a day and three times a week" or "fiber helps some people with constipation," the study behind that sentence is listed here with a link to its PubMed record, so you can read the abstract yourself.

Three things to know before you browse:

1. **None of these are Get Regular studies.** We haven't published research of our own. When we do, it will appear on [our research program page](/science/research) and be labeled as ours.
2. **A study being listed here doesn't mean it proves something about the Get Regular app.** For example, the AI studies in this library tested other apps and general-purpose chatbots, not ours. See [how our AI works](/science/how-our-ai-works).
3. **The short answers live on the topic pages.** This page is the reference list. For a plain-language summary of what a group of studies shows, start with the [topic syntheses](#topics).

## How we choose evidence {#how-we-choose}

We use the strongest kind of evidence that answers the question, in this order:

| Order | Evidence type | What it is | How we use it |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Clinical guidelines and consensus statements** | Recommendations from professional bodies such as the American College of Gastroenterology (ACG) and American Gastroenterological Association (AGA), usually built on systematic reviews | First source for "what should I do?" We quote the strength of a recommendation as the guideline states it (for example, "conditional" vs "strong") |
| 2 | **Systematic reviews and meta-analyses** | Studies that gather and pool the results of many earlier studies | Prevalence ("how common is…") and overall effect sizes |
| 3 | **Randomized trials** | People are randomly assigned to a treatment or a comparison | Whether a specific change (a fiber, a food) does something |
| 4 | **Other studies** | Clinical, observational, population and validation studies | Normal ranges, how measurement tools perform, how often things happen |
| 5 | **Narrative reviews** | Expert summaries of a field | Background and mechanisms, never as the only support for a number |

**Our rules:**

- **Every record is checked against PubMed.** Titles, authors, journals, years and DOIs come from PubMed's records through `science/build_evidence_library.py`. Nobody types them in.
- **We read the abstract before we use a number,** and we re-read it each time a page is reviewed. The date we last checked is shown on each topic page.
- **We say what a study can't show.** Small samples, healthy volunteers, short follow-up, a single clinic, or authors who work for the company whose product is tested all go in the "limits" column on topic pages.
- **Guidelines beat single studies.** If one exciting study disagrees with a current guideline, we report both and say which one we'd weigh more.
- **Evidence-type labels come from PubMed's publication types,** not from our own grading. A label tells you what kind of study it is, not how good it is.
- **We remove or update studies** when a newer guideline replaces them (for example, citing Rome V instead of Rome IV for bowel-disorder criteria from 2026 on).

Found a citation that doesn't say what we say it says? Tell us at hello@getregular.com with "Evidence error" in the subject. See [how we handle corrections](/science/clinical-review#report-an-error).

# What the research says about the Bristol Stool Scale

> **Short answer:** The Bristol Stool Form Scale is a 7-point scale for stool shape and consistency, and it works as a rough guide to how fast stool moves through the gut. In 66 volunteers, changes in stool type tracked changes in transit time better than bowel movement frequency or stool weight did [1]. People rated stool models correctly 81% of the time, but they disagreed more near the borders between types [2].

**Last reviewed:** [SET AT PUBLISH] by [CONFIRM: clinician, with consent] · **Evidence checked:** 20 Oct 2026

**The 7 types, in our own words:** 1 hard, separate pellets · 2 a hard, lumpy log · 3 a formed log with a cracked surface · 4 a smooth, soft log · 5 soft pieces with defined edges · 6 mushy, loose pieces · 7 liquid. See the full [Bristol stool chart](/tools/bristol-stool-chart).

## What this means

- **It measures form, and form is a stand-in for speed.** Harder types go with slower colonic transit and looser types with faster [1][3]. It says little about the stomach or small bowel [3].
- **"Normal" is a range, not one type.** One study grouped types 3–5 as normal [2]. In US adults with normal habits, women's usual range ran wider than men's [4].
- **Expect some fuzziness at the edges.** Telling type 2 from 3, or 5 from 6, is where people disagree most [2]. A pattern across many entries tells you more than any single rating.
- **Changes beat snapshots.** The scale was most informative when tracking change in the same person over time [1].

## What's still uncertain

- Whether training or clearer wording would make ratings more accurate. The validation authors suggested testing this [2].
- Where "normal" ends. Sources draw the line differently, especially around type 5 [2][4]. [CONFIRM reviewer: type-5 framing]
- Most data come from healthy volunteers or small patient groups [1–3]. How well self-ratings track transit in older adults or people taking gut-active medicines is less clear.
- The correlations are moderate [1], so the scale can't replace a transit test when a clinician needs one.

# What the research says about how often you should poop

> **Short answer:** There's no single right number. In a US national survey of 4,775 adults who called their bowel habits normal, 95.9% reported between 3 and 21 bowel movements a week, which supports the common "three a day to three a week" rule [1]. Once a day is the most common pattern, but in a UK population study only 40% of men and 33% of women had a regular daily cycle [2].

**Last reviewed:** [SET AT PUBLISH] by [CONFIRM: clinician, with consent] · **Evidence checked:** 20 Oct 2026

## The evidence at a glance

| Study | Type | Who | What it found | Limits |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Mitsuhashi et al., *Am J Gastroenterol* 2018 [1] | National survey (NHANES 2009–2010) | 4,775 US adults who reported normal bowel habits | 95.9% reported 3–21 bowel movements a week. | Heaton et al., *Gut* 1992 [2] | Prospective population study | 838 men and 1,059 women in East Bristol, UK (72.2% of a random sample) | Once daily was the most common habit but still a minority. | Walter et al., *Scand J Gastroenterol* 2002 [3] | Postal survey | Random sample of 2,000 adults aged 31–76 in one Swedish county; 80.5% responded | 67.8% reported one bowel movement a day; 95.6% fell between 3 a day and 3 a week. ## What this means

- **A wide range is common.** About three a day to three a week covers most adults in both the US and Swedish surveys [1][3].
- **Daily isn't required.** Most people in the Bristol study were not on a regular daily cycle [2].
- **Women tend to go less often.** More women than men reported fewer than 3 a week in Sweden [3], and a third of women in Bristol went less than daily [2].
- **Frequency is only half the picture.** Stool form tracked transit time better than frequency did [5]. Log your [stool type](/science/evidence/bristol-stool-scale) as well as your count.

- These studies show what is *common*, not what is *healthy*. Falling outside the range doesn't by itself mean something is wrong, and falling inside it doesn't rule out a problem [1].
- Most data are self-reported, and some are decades old [2][3][4].
- The US survey found links between frequency and sex, income, medications and fiber, not causes [1].
- A change from your own usual pattern may matter more than where you sit in the population range. [CONFIRM reviewer: wording]

# What the research says about gut transit time

> **Short answer:** There's no single normal number. In one marker study of 43 healthy adults, the normal range for whole-gut transit ran from 12 to 120 hours [1]. Colonic transit alone averaged about 35 hours in 73 healthy adults, and women tended to be slower than men [2].

**Last reviewed:** [SET AT PUBLISH] by [CONFIRM: clinician, with consent] · **Evidence checked:** 20 Oct 2026

## The evidence at a glance

| Study | Type | Who | What it found | Limits |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Evans et al., *Int J Colorectal Dis* 1992 [1] | Normal-range study, radiopaque markers | 25 healthy men and 18 healthy women | Men's and women's results were similar enough to combine into one normal range. Its limits were 12 and 120 hours | Small; one London hospital; tracks plastic markers, not food |
| Metcalf et al., *Gastroenterology* 1987 [2] | Method study, radiopaque markers | 73 healthy adults | Total colonic transit 35.0 ± 2.1 h (mean ± SE): right colon 11.3 h, left colon 11.4 h, rectosigmoid 12.4 h. Men were faster through the whole colon. Age and a small fiber dose had no significant effect | Colon only; reports a mean, not a full range; 1980s data |
| Wang et al., *Aliment Pharmacol Ther* 2015 [3] | Multicenter study, wireless motility capsule | 215 healthy volunteers, USA and Sweden | Women's whole-gut transit was longer (median 263 minutes). Test protocol and country changed results. Whole-gut and colonic times clustered at values 24 hours apart | Capsule, not food; one author from the capsule maker; normal values aren't in the abstract |
| Nandhra et al., *Neurogastroenterol Motil* 2020 [4] | Normative study, 3D-Transit electromagnetic capsule | 111 healthy volunteers, UK and Denmark, ages 21–88 | Older age was linked with longer colonic and whole-gut transit; higher BMI with shorter whole-gut transit. Times clustered about 24 hours apart | One author from the device maker; normal values aren't in the abstract |
| Degen & Phillips, *Gut* 1996 [5] | Repeat-measure physiology study | 32 healthy volunteers, standardized diet and exercise, tested twice | Colonic transit was faster in men. Group averages matched across tests, but single tests in the same person sometimes varied considerably | Small; one center |
| Asnicar et al., *Gut* 2021 [6] | Single-arm study within ZOE's PREDICT 1, blue dye marker | 863 healthy adults | Blue-dye transit time was linked to gut microbiome makeup (AUC 0.82 for telling transit classes apart), more strongly than stool form or frequency | Sponsored by ZOE (Zoe Global Ltd), which helped design the study; several authors are ZOE employees or consultants. Shows microbiome links, not health outcomes |
| Lewis & Heaton, *Scand J Gastroenterol* 1997 [7] | Volunteer study with radiopaque markers | 66 volunteers | Stool form correlated with whole-gut transit time (r = −0.54), better than frequency (r = 0.35) | Volunteers; moderate correlation |

## What this means

- **"Normal" is wide.** Healthy people spanned 12 to 120 hours in one normal-range study [1], so day-to-day differences are expected.
- **The colon is a big part of it.** Colonic transit alone averaged about 35 hours in healthy adults [2].
- **Sex, age and body size shift it.** Women tended to be slower [2][3][5]. Older age went with longer transit and higher BMI with shorter [4].
- **One test is a rough snapshot.** Results cluster in 24-hour steps [3][4], and the same person can vary between tests [5].

## What's still uncertain

- What transit time is best for health isn't established. The blue-dye study linked transit to microbiome makeup, not to health outcomes [6].
- Numbers depend on the method (markers, capsules, dye) and the test protocol [3], so values from different studies aren't directly comparable.
- The blue-dye study was run with ZOE, a commercial company. That doesn't make it wrong, but it isn't independent [6].
- Most normal-range studies are small and in healthy volunteers [1][2][5].

## Track it

You can't see transit time directly. Stool form is the everyday stand-in with the best evidence [7]: harder types suggest slower transit, looser types faster. Log your Bristol type for 2 weeks and watch the trend. If you try a colored-food test at home, treat it as one rough reading, since single measurements vary [5]. # What the research says about fiber and constipation

> **Short answer:** Fiber helps some people with constipation, but not everyone. The 2023 AGA–ACG guideline gives fiber a *conditional* recommendation for chronic constipation in adults, a weaker endorsement than for osmotic laxatives such as polyethylene glycol [1]. Soluble fiber such as psyllium has the best evidence [2]. In one clinic study, most people whose constipation came from slow transit or a defecation disorder did not improve on fiber, while most people without those findings did [3].

**Last reviewed:** [SET AT PUBLISH] by [CONFIRM: clinician, with consent] · **Evidence checked:** 20 Oct 2026

## The evidence at a glance

| Study | Type | Who | What it found | Limits |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Chang et al., AGA–ACG guideline, *Am J Gastroenterol* 2023 [1] | Clinical practice guideline (systematic reviews, GRADE) | Adults with chronic idiopathic constipation | **Conditional** recommendation for fiber; **strong** recommendations for polyethylene glycol, sodium picosulfate, linaclotide, plecanatide and prucalopride | Covers supplements and drugs; diet advice beyond fiber supplements isn't its focus |
| Eswaran, Muir & Chey, *Am J Gastroenterol* 2013 [2] | Review | Functional bowel disorders | Evidence for fiber is "limited"; a soluble supplement such as ispaghula/psyllium is best supported; fiber can worsen distension, gas, constipation or diarrhea | Narrative review, not a meta-analysis |
| Voderholzer et al., *Am J Gastroenterol* 1997 [3] | Clinical cohort | 149 patients with chronic constipation, psyllium (Plantago ovata) 15–30 g/day for ≥6 weeks | 80% with slow transit and 63% with a defecation disorder did not respond; 85% without a pathological finding improved or became symptom-free | Two Munich GI departments, 1997, no placebo arm |
| Gunn et al., *Am J Clin Nutr* 2020 [4] | Randomized crossover trial with MRI | 14 healthy volunteers; psyllium, wheat bran, nopal | All three raised colonic water over 24 h. | Barberio et al., *Lancet Gastroenterol Hepatol* 2021 [5] | Systematic review and meta-analysis | 45 studies, 275,260 adults | Functional constipation affects about 10% of adults by Rome IV criteria (10.1%), more often women | Large heterogeneity between studies |

## What this means

- **Fiber is a reasonable first step, not a cure-all.** The guideline backs it conditionally [1], and the clinic study suggests trying it before tests [3].
- **Type matters.** Psyllium is the best-supported supplement [2]. It ferments more slowly and produces less gas in the lab than wheat bran [4], which may matter if you bloat easily.
- **Go slowly.** Fiber can cause gas, distension and even worse constipation in some people [2]. Increase over 2–3 weeks with enough fluid. [CONFIRM reviewer: fluid wording]
- **If fiber doesn't work after a fair trial, that's information.** Non-response is common with slow transit or pelvic-floor (defecation) problems [3]. A clinician can test for these. Other treatments have stronger evidence [1].

## What's still uncertain

- Most fiber trials are small and short, and use different fibers and doses [2].
- Food fiber vs supplements: the guideline graded supplements [1]; the evidence for whole-diet changes is thinner.
- How much fiber helps *you* depends on why you're constipated [3]. That's why a 2–3 week log of stool type, fiber and fluids is more useful than a generic target.

## Track it

Log your stool type (Bristol scale), fiber and water for 2–3 weeks while you increase fiber slowly. If your stool moves toward types 3–4 and straining eases, it's working. If not, take the log to your doctor. [Find your stool type](/tools/bristol-stool-chart) · [Printable bowel movement log](/resources/bowel-movement-log-pdf) · [Constipation hub](/learn/constipation)

# What the research says about food diaries vs memory

> **Short answer:** Memory is a weak guide to gut symptoms. In a Swedish study, what people recalled on a questionnaire agreed poorly with what they wrote in a 7- or 14-day diary, especially for complex questions such as whether pain eases after a bowel movement [1]. A diary is a measuring tool, not a treatment: it can surface food patterns, but a suspected trigger still needs testing, because blinded trials show beliefs about trigger foods don't always hold up [5].

**Last reviewed:** [SET AT PUBLISH] by [CONFIRM: clinician, with consent] · **Evidence checked:** 20 Oct 2026

## The evidence at a glance

| Study | Type | Who | What it found | Limits |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Jones et al., *Eur J Gastroenterol Hepatol* 2019 [1] | Comparative study | 238 adults from the general population and 503 primary-care patients (10 health centres) | Agreement between recall questionnaires and 7- or 14-day stool and symptom diaries was generally poor. It was best for a simple item (any abdominal pain) and worse for complex ones (pain relieved by defecation) | Neither method is a gold standard; Rome III questions |
| Walter et al., *Dig Dis Sci* 2011 [2] | Population study with colonoscopy and 1-week diary | 268 adults from the Swedish population register; 23 excluded for organic disease | 2,194 bowel movements and 370 pain episodes logged. In the 28 people (12%) who met Rome II IBS criteria, only 4% of pain episodes improved after a bowel movement, and 29% started or worsened after a meal | One week of diaries; older Rome II criteria |
| Böhn et al., *Am J Gastroenterol* 2013 [3] | Questionnaire study | 197 IBS patients | 84% linked symptoms to at least one food. Most often named: fried and fatty foods (52%), dairy (49%), beans and lentils (36%). More trigger foods went with more severe symptoms and lower quality of life | Self-report only; no food challenges, so the beliefs weren't tested |
| Zia et al., *Clin Transl Gastroenterol* 2016 [4] | Pilot feasibility study | 11 IBS patients logging meals and symptoms in an app for 2 weeks | 24-hour compliance was 90% for meals and 94% for symptoms. 73% showed at least one strong link between a nutrient and later symptoms, and the patterns differed from person to person. Most did not have a clinically significant drop in symptom scores | Very small; no control group; links were statistical, not confirmed by challenge |
| Seiler et al., *Lancet Gastroenterol Hepatol* 2025 [5] | Randomized, double-blind, sham-controlled crossover trial | 28 adults with IBS who believed a gluten-free diet helped them | Symptoms worsened after wheat in 39%, gluten in 36% and a gluten-free sham in 29%, with no significant difference. The authors concluded expectations played a major role | Small, single centre, 7-day challenges |

## What this means

- **Write it down when it happens.** Recall and diaries disagree, and the gap grows as questions get more complex [1].
- **Memory can overcall links.** Recall-based criteria may overstate the link between pain and bowel movements compared with a diary [2].
- **Triggers seem personal.** In the app pilot, food–symptom patterns differed between people [4], so a generic "avoid" list may not fit you.
- **Treat a pattern as a lead, not a verdict.** Most people with IBS name trigger foods [3], but in a blinded test, people who believed gluten affected them reacted about as often to a sham [5]. Test a suspected trigger with a clinician or dietitian before cutting foods for good.

## What's still uncertain

- No large trial shows that keeping a diary, by itself, improves IBS symptoms. In the small pilot, most participants did not improve [4].
- Diaries are self-report too. The comparison study measured agreement, not which method is right [1].
- The best logging period isn't settled. Studies used 1 week [2] to 2 weeks [1][4].
- Expectation can shape symptoms [5], and a diary can't separate that from a true food effect. Blinded re-testing can.

## Track it

Log meals, symptoms and stool type as they happen for about 2 weeks. Note portion size and timing. Then look for repeats, not one-offs, and bring the log to your appointment. # What the research says about lactose intolerance

> **Short answer:** Not fully digesting lactose (malabsorption) is not the same as getting symptoms from it (intolerance). An NIH consensus panel concluded that lactose intolerance is real but its true prevalence is unknown, and that most people with malabsorption don't have clinical intolerance [1]. A systematic review found that most adults with presumed intolerance tolerate 12–15 g of lactose, about 1 cup of milk [2].

**Last reviewed:** [SET AT PUBLISH] by [CONFIRM: clinician, with consent] · **Evidence checked:** 20 Oct 2026

## The evidence at a glance

| Study | Type | Who | What it found | Limits |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Suchy et al., NIH Consensus Statement 2010 [1] | Consensus statement (14-member panel plus an AHRQ systematic review) | General population | Intolerance is real, but its true prevalence is unknown. Most people with malabsorption don't have clinical intolerance, and many who think they're intolerant aren't malabsorbers. Most don't need to cut dairy completely; avoiding it can mean too little calcium and vitamin D | 2010; an independent panel report, not NIH policy |
| Shaukat et al., *Ann Intern Med* 2010 [2] | Systematic review of 36 randomized trials | Adults with lactose intolerance or malabsorption | Moderate-quality evidence that 12–15 g of lactose (about 1 cup of milk) is well tolerated by most. Evidence was insufficient for lactose-reduced milk, probiotics and colonic adaptation | Most trials enrolled people with malabsorption, not intolerance; results couldn't be pooled |
| Varjú et al., *Neurogastroenterol Motil* 2019 [3] | Meta-analysis of 14 articles | Adults with IBS vs healthy controls | People with IBS reported lactose intolerance more often (odds ratio about 3.5) and had objective intolerance more often (about 2.5). Malabsorption itself was not more common | Few studies; varied lactose doses and tests |
| Misselwitz et al., *Gut* 2019 [4] | Review | Lactose malabsorption and intolerance | Whether lactose causes symptoms depends on dose, lactase levels and gut microbes. People with IBS or anxiety-linked gut sensitivity are at higher risk, regardless of digestion. Treatment results are modest | Narrative review; one author reports research funding from Nestlé for lactose studies |

## What this means

- **A positive test isn't the whole story.** Many people who malabsorb lactose have no symptoms, and many who blame lactose don't malabsorb it [1][4].
- **Cutting all dairy usually isn't needed.** Most people with presumed intolerance handle about 1 cup of milk [2], and strict avoidance can leave calcium and vitamin D short [1].
- **With IBS, dairy symptoms may not be about lactose digestion.** People with IBS don't malabsorb lactose more often than others, but they do get symptoms more often [3]. Gut sensitivity linked to IBS raises the risk of intolerance, regardless of how well lactose is digested [4].
- **Lactose-free swaps have thin trial support.** Evidence that lactose-reduced milk beats regular milk for symptoms was insufficient [2]. Lactose is one of several poorly absorbed carbs that can cause similar symptoms [4].

## What's still uncertain

- **How many people are truly intolerant.** The NIH panel said the true prevalence isn't known [1]. A widely quoted estimate, that about two-thirds of adults worldwide malabsorb lactose, came from a 2017 meta-analysis [5] that the journal retracted in January 2025 [6]. We don't use its numbers.
- Tests (genetic, breath and others) measure malabsorption. How well they predict symptoms and diet results varies [4].
- Most treatment trials studied malabsorption rather than intolerance, and they were too different to combine [2].

# What the research says about gluten without celiac disease

> **Short answer:** Many people with IBS feel better off gluten, but blinded trials find that gluten itself triggers symptoms in only a minority of them. In a 2025 trial of people with IBS who believed a gluten-free diet helped, symptoms worsened about as often after a gluten-free sham (29%) as after gluten (36%) or wheat (39%) [1]. Celiac disease needs to be checked before cutting gluten: the ACG *suggests* celiac blood testing for people with IBS and diarrhea [5].

**Last reviewed:** [SET AT PUBLISH] by [CONFIRM: clinician, with consent] · **Evidence checked:** 20 Oct 2026

## The evidence at a glance

| Study | Type | Who | What it found | Limits |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Seiler et al., *Lancet Gastroenterol Hepatol* 2025 [1] | Randomized, double-blind, sham-controlled crossover trial | 28 adults with Rome IV IBS who had improved on a gluten-free diet | Worsening of at least 50 points on the IBS symptom score: wheat 39%, gluten 36%, sham 29%. No significant difference. Authors: expectations played a major role, and only some patients may benefit from restriction | Small, single centre, 7-day challenges |
| Biesiekierski et al., *Gastroenterology* 2013 [2] | Double-blind crossover trial | 37 adults with self-reported gluten sensitivity and IBS, celiac disease excluded | Symptoms improved on a reduced-FODMAP diet, then worsened to a similar degree with gluten or whey protein. Gluten-specific effects in only 8%. No diet-specific changes in any biomarker | Small; an order effect was seen |
| Skodje et al., *Gastroenterology* 2018 [3] | Double-blind, placebo-controlled crossover trial | 59 adults on a self-started gluten-free diet, celiac disease excluded | Fructans (2.1 g) raised symptom scores more than gluten (5.7 g). Gluten did not differ from placebo. Highest scores: 24 people on fructan, 22 on placebo, 13 on gluten | One centre; 7-day challenges; one type of food bar |
| Molina-Infante & Carroccio, *Clin Gastroenterol Hepatol* 2017 [4] | Analysis of 10 double-blind, placebo-controlled gluten-challenge trials | 1,312 adults | Only 38 of 231 people with suspected gluten sensitivity (16%) had gluten-specific symptoms, and 40% of those also reacted to placebo | Trials varied in dose (2–52 g/day), length (1 day to 6 weeks) and placebo |
| Lacy et al., ACG IBS guideline, *Am J Gastroenterol* 2021 [5] | Clinical practice guideline (GRADE) | Adults with IBS | *Suggests* celiac blood testing in IBS with diarrhea. *Recommends* a limited trial of a low-FODMAP diet | Covers IBS testing and treatment broadly, not gluten sensitivity itself |
| Rubio-Tapia et al., ACG celiac guideline, *Am J Gastroenterol* 2023 [6] | Clinical practice guideline | People with suspected celiac disease | Celiac disease is a permanent immune response to gluten. Blood antibody tests help screen; a biopsy is needed to confirm in most patients | Focused on celiac disease, not gluten sensitivity |

## What this means

- **Feeling better off gluten doesn't prove gluten was the cause.** In blinded tests, people reacted to a sham almost as often as to gluten [1], and many gluten "reactors" also reacted to placebo [4].
- **Fructans may matter more than gluten for some people.** Wheat foods that contain gluten often contain fructans, a type of FODMAP. In one trial, fructans, not gluten, raised symptoms [3]. The ACG recommends a limited low-FODMAP trial for IBS [5].
- **A true gluten-specific reaction looks uncommon, not impossible.** Rates ranged from 8% [2] to 16% [4] of people who believed they were sensitive.
- **Test for celiac disease first.** Celiac tests look for the immune response to gluten [6], and the ACG suggests testing people with IBS and diarrhea [5]. Ask about testing before you cut gluten. [CONFIRM reviewer: add guideline wording that testing should happen while still eating gluten; not stated in the abstract]

## What's still uncertain

- There is no biomarker for non-celiac gluten sensitivity [3]. Trials found no diet-specific biomarker changes [2].
- Trials are small and short, and they differ in gluten dose, length and placebo, including placebos that themselves contained fermentable carbs [4].
- Who the minority with a real gluten or wheat effect are, and how to identify them, is still open [1].

# What the research says about low FODMAP and IBS diet guidelines

> **Short answer:** A low-FODMAP diet has more research behind it than any other IBS diet, but it doesn't help everyone and the evidence quality is mostly low. The 2021 American College of Gastroenterology (ACG) guideline says "we recommend a limited trial" of a low-FODMAP diet "in patients with IBS to improve global symptoms" [1]. A 2025 analysis of 28 trials concluded that "the most evidence exists for a low FODMAP diet," while other diets are still being studied [4].

**Last reviewed:** [SET AT PUBLISH] by [CONFIRM: clinician, with consent] · **Evidence checked:** 20 Oct 2026

## The evidence at a glance

| Study | Type | Who | What it found | Limits |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Lacy et al., ACG guideline, *Am J Gastroenterol* 2021 [1] | Clinical practice guideline (GRADE, modified Delphi) | Adults with IBS | "We **recommend** a limited trial" of a low-FODMAP diet to improve global IBS symptoms | Published before Rome V. The abstract doesn't state the evidence grade for this statement [CONFIRM reviewer: strength and quality grade from full guideline text] |
| Corsetti et al., Rome V Bowel Disorders, *Gastroenterology* 2026 [2] | Expert committee report (Rome V) | People with bowel disorders | Updates the Rome V classification into 6 categories, including IBS, with sections on diagnosis and treatment | Consensus document; the abstract doesn't summarize diet advice |
| Goyal et al., *Indian J Gastroenterol* 2026 [3] | Narrative review | Rome V changes | Rome V brings back "abdominal discomfort," requires symptoms to come and go in IBS, and adds "Rome Clinical Criteria" for people below full diagnostic thresholds | Narrative review; authors note no validated biomarkers and new criteria still need validation |
| Cuffe et al., *Lancet Gastroenterol Hepatol* 2025 [4] | Systematic review and network meta-analysis | 28 randomized trials, 2,338 patients, 11 diets | Vs a usual diet, low FODMAP cut the risk of global symptoms not improving (RR 0.51, 24 trials). It was the only diet tested in more than one trial to beat a usual diet for bloating. No diet beat controls for bowel habit | Most comparisons rated low or very low confidence; low FODMAP vs usual diet rated moderate |
| Dionne et al., *Am J Gastroenterol* 2018 [5] | Systematic review and meta-analysis (GRADE) | 7 trials, 397 people (low FODMAP); 2 trials, 111 people (gluten-free) | Low FODMAP reduced global symptoms vs control interventions (RR 0.69). Trials with the strictest control diets showed the smallest effect. "Insufficient evidence" to recommend gluten-free | Overall evidence quality "very low" by GRADE |
| Almario et al., *Gastroenterology* 2023 [6] | Nationwide online survey | 88,607 US adults | 6.1% met Rome IV IBS criteria | Run in May–June 2020, during COVID-19; uses Rome IV, not Rome V |

## What this means

- **IBS is common, and diet response varies.** About 6.1% of US adults met IBS criteria in one large survey [6]. In the 2025 analysis, people on low FODMAP were about half as likely as people on their usual diet to report no improvement [4].
- **It's a trial, not a forever diet.** The ACG wording is "a limited trial" [1]. A 2023 AGA expert review on bloating advises that when diet changes such as low FODMAP are needed, "a gastroenterology dietitian should preferably monitor treatment" [7].
- **Bloating is where it stands out most.** Low FODMAP was the only diet tested in more than one trial to beat a usual diet for bloating, but no diet clearly changed bowel habit [4].
- **Gluten-free is a different question.** One meta-analysis found too little evidence to recommend it for IBS symptoms [5]. See [gluten without celiac](/science/evidence/gluten-without-celiac).

## What's still uncertain

- **Evidence quality.** A 2018 review rated it "very low" [5]. By 2025, the direct comparison with a usual diet was rated moderate, but most other comparisons stayed low or very low [4].
- **Blinding is hard.** People usually know what diet they're on. Trials with the strictest control diets showed the smallest benefit [5].
- **Other diets.** A starch- and sucrose-reduced diet ranked first for global symptoms, but in only two trials [4].
- **Rome V.** The diet trials enrolled people diagnosed under older criteria. Rome V changes the IBS definition [2][3], and how well past results carry over isn't known yet.

## Track it

If you try a low-FODMAP diet, log meals, symptoms and stool type (Bristol scale) through each phase, especially reintroduction. That record shows which foods matter for you and is useful to bring to a dietitian or doctor. **See a doctor promptly** if gut symptoms come with blood in the stool, unexplained weight loss, a new change after age 50, or symptoms that wake you at night [CONFIRM reviewer: alarm list per site standard]. Don't start a restrictive diet in place of getting these checked.

# What the research says about stress and IBS

> **Short answer:** Stress and IBS symptoms are closely linked, often within the same day. In a 2026 study of 357 adults with IBS who rated stress and symptoms three times a day for a week, times of higher-than-usual stress came with worse symptoms, and afternoon stress was tied to worse evening symptoms [1]. The link runs both ways and is an association, not proof that stress causes flares; the ACG guideline states "we suggest" gut-directed psychotherapy for global IBS symptoms [5].

**Last reviewed:** [SET AT PUBLISH] by [CONFIRM: clinician, with consent] · **Evidence checked:** 20 Oct 2026

## The evidence at a glance

| Study | Type | Who | What it found | Limits |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Yunusova et al., *Clin Gastroenterol Hepatol* 2026 [1] | Prospective observational study (real-time surveys, 3 a day for 7 days) | 357 adults meeting Rome IV IBS criteria | Higher-than-usual stress came with worse symptoms, independent of chronic stress (P < .001). Afternoon stress was linked to worse evening symptoms (P = .002), and afternoon symptoms to more evening stress (P = .04). Evening-to-next-morning links were "disrupted or inverse" | One week; self-report; observational. One author is Chief of Science for Equa Health (disclosed) |
| Levy et al., *J Behav Med* 1997 [2] | Daily diary study over two menstrual cycles | Women aged 20–45: 26 IBS patients, 23 with IBS-type symptoms who weren't IBS patients, 26 controls | Within each woman, days with more stress had more GI symptoms in both IBS groups. Adjusting for menstrual cycle didn't change this. No group difference in recalled life events | Small, women only, older study |
| Jarrett et al., *Nurs Res* 1998 [3] | Interviews, questionnaires and 2-month daily diaries | 97 women (IBS, IBS symptoms without diagnosis, controls) | At least 40% of women in the IBS groups showed a positive link between daily distress and daily GI symptoms | Same research group as [2]; women only; the link wasn't shown for everyone |
| Qin et al., *World J Gastroenterol* 2014 [4] | Narrative review | Clinical and experimental (including animal) studies | Stress affects gut sensitivity, motility, secretion and permeability through the gut-brain axis; calls IBS "a stress-sensitive disorder" | Not a systematic review; mechanisms partly from animal work |
| Lacy et al., ACG guideline, *Am J Gastroenterol* 2021 [5] | Clinical practice guideline (GRADE) | Adults with IBS | "We **suggest** that gut-directed psychotherapy be used to treat global IBS symptoms" | "Suggest" is weaker wording than "recommend"; abstract doesn't give the evidence grade [CONFIRM reviewer] |

## What this means

- **The link shows up day to day, not just over years.** Moments of higher stress came with worse symptoms, even after accounting for chronic stress [1]. Older diary studies found the same pattern within individual women [2].
- **It runs both ways.** Afternoon stress was linked to worse evening symptoms, and afternoon symptoms to more evening stress [1]. Feeling unwell is itself stressful.
- **Not everyone's gut tracks their stress.** In one diary study, at least 40% of women in the IBS groups showed the link [3]. Your own pattern may differ, which is why personal tracking helps.
- **Treating the gut-brain connection is a guideline option.** The ACG suggests gut-directed psychotherapy for global IBS symptoms [5], consistent with the gut-brain mechanisms described in research [4].

## What's still uncertain

- **Cause vs. association.** These are observational studies [1][2][3]. They show stress and symptoms move together, not that stress starts IBS or triggers a given flare.
- **Who the findings apply to.** The two diary studies included only women [2][3]. The 2026 study ran for one week [1].
- **Whether within-day stress management works.** The 2026 authors say their findings point to its "potential value," but that wasn't tested in this study [1].
- **Mechanisms.** Much of the biology comes from experimental and animal studies [4].

## Track it

Rate your stress alongside symptoms and stool type (Bristol scale) at the same times each day for 2–3 weeks. Then look for patterns: do bad afternoons come before bad evenings? Bring the log to your doctor. # What the research says about food poisoning and IBS

> **Short answer:** Yes, a gut infection such as food poisoning can be followed by IBS, though most people in the studies did not develop it. A 2017 meta-analysis of 45 studies found that 10.1% of people developed IBS within 12 months of an infectious enteritis, and their risk was 4.2-fold higher than in people without such an infection [1]. Risk was higher in women, after more severe illness, with antibiotic use during the infection, and with anxiety or depression [1].

**Last reviewed:** [SET AT PUBLISH] by [CONFIRM: clinician, with consent] · **Evidence checked:** 20 Oct 2026

## The evidence at a glance

| Study | Type | Who | What it found | Limits |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Klem et al., *Gastroenterology* 2017 [1] | Systematic review and meta-analysis | 45 cohort studies, 21,421 people with infectious enteritis, followed 3 months to 10 years | IBS in 10.1% at 12 months and 14.5% after more than 12 months. Risk 4.2-fold higher in the first 12 months, 2.3-fold higher after that. 41.9% after protozoa or parasites vs 13.8% after bacterial infection | "Considerable" heterogeneity between studies; observational data |
| Barbara et al., Rome Foundation working team, *Gastroenterology* 2019 [2] | Systematic review with Delphi consensus | People with post-infection IBS (PI-IBS) | PI-IBS develops in about 10% after infectious enteritis. Risk factors: female sex, younger age, psychological distress before or during the illness, and severity. "No evidence-based, effective pharmacologic strategies" for treatment | Treatment algorithm is consensus-based; mechanisms "incompletely understood"; several authors report industry ties |

## What this means

- **It's a real, recognized pattern.** A meta-analysis and a Rome Foundation report (with an overlapping author) both put the rate at about 1 in 10 after infectious enteritis [1][2]. Foodborne illness affects about 15% of the US population each year [1].
- **The type of infection matters.** Rates were much higher after protozoal or parasitic infections (41.9%) than after bacterial ones (13.8%) [1].
- **Some factors raise the odds.** Women, people with severe illness, antibiotic users during the infection, and people with anxiety, depression or somatization had higher risk [1]. Younger age was also a risk factor [2]. These raise odds; they don't decide who gets IBS.
- **Timing helps your doctor.** Symptoms that started after a gut infection are a useful clue. The Rome Foundation report offers a diagnosis and treatment algorithm for clinicians [2]. See [doctor visits](/learn/doctor-visits).

## What's still uncertain

- **Why it happens.** Changes in the gut microbiome and in epithelial, serotonin and immune factors appear to be involved, but these are "incompletely understood" [2].
- **Best treatment.** There are no evidence-based, effective drug treatments specific to PI-IBS; current advice is consensus-based [2].
- **How long it lasts.** Studies followed people for 3 months to 10 years. IBS was still found in 14.5% after more than 12 months, but the abstract doesn't report how many people recover over time [1].
- **How exact the numbers are.** Studies differed a lot in design and setting, so the pooled rates are estimates [1].

## Track it

If gut symptoms started or changed after a stomach bug or food poisoning, note the date of the illness, any antibiotics, and how your stool type (Bristol scale) and symptoms have changed since. A clear timeline is useful to bring to a doctor. # What the research says about bloating

> **Short answer:** Bloating is a feeling of trapped gas, pressure or fullness; distension is a measurable increase in belly size, and the two often but not always go together [3][5]. Bloating is common (13.9% of 88,795 surveyed Americans had it in the past week) and has several possible causes, including food intolerances, constipation or slow transit, a more sensitive gut and an unusual reflex of the belly and diaphragm muscles [2][3][6]. The 2023 AGA clinical practice update offers best-practice advice, but it is expert advice without formal evidence grades [1].

**Last reviewed:** [SET AT PUBLISH] by [CONFIRM: clinician, with consent] · **Evidence checked:** 20 Oct 2026

## The evidence at a glance

| Study | Type | Who | What it found | Limits |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Moshiree, Drossman & Shaukat, AGA Clinical Practice Update, *Gastroenterology* 2023 [1] | Expert review with 15 best-practice advice statements | Adults with belching, bloating or distension | Use Rome criteria to diagnose primary bloating. ## What this means

- **Feeling bloated and looking bloated are different things.** About half of people with IBS who feel bloated also measurably swell, and the link is strongest with constipation [5]. That's why it helps to note both [3].
- **There isn't one cause.** Food intolerances, constipation, a sensitive gut and muscle reflexes can all play a part [3][6]. Your diet and bowel pattern give clues [4].
- **Don't start with probiotics.** The AGA update advises against them for bloating. It favors a dietitian-guided diet, treating constipation if present, and brain-gut therapies such as CBT or hypnotherapy [1]. Diaphragmatic breathing is used when the diaphragm reflex is involved [1].
- **You're not alone, and it's worth raising.** Most Americans with recent bloating never sought care for it [2]. A doctor can rule out causes such as celiac disease with a blood test [1].

## What's still uncertain

- Bloating treatments have limited evidence. The AGA update says so directly, and its advice isn't formally graded [1].
- No treatment works for everyone [3]. Finding the mechanism in a given person "is not always feasible" [4].
- The girth and diaphragm studies are small [5][6].
- How Rome V's bloating criteria differ from Rome IV needs the full text [7].

## Track it

For 2–3 weeks, log meals, stool type (Bristol scale) and when bloating starts. Note whether your belly looks swollen or just feels full. A log gives your doctor more to work with than memory. # What the research says about gut microbiome tests

> **Short answer:** Not yet as a guide to diagnosis or treatment. A 2025 international expert consensus found that evidence for the clinical usefulness of microbiome testing is scarce, said there is "insufficient evidence to widely recommend the routine use" of these tests in clinical practice, and discouraged people from ordering them without a clinician's recommendation [1]. When a US government lab sent the same standardized stool material to seven direct-to-consumer services, results differed between companies about as much as stool from different people differs [2].

## The evidence at a glance

| Study | Type | Who | What it found | Limits |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Porcari et al., *Lancet Gastroenterol Hepatol* 2025 [1] | International consensus statement (expert panel, Delphi voting) | Microbiome testing in clinical practice | Evidence for clinical use is "scarce." Consumer tests are sold "without any consensus on their regulation or any proven value in clinical practice." Variability between providers was on the same scale as variability between different donors. Authors blame differing methods and weak quality control | Tests one reference material, not people's health outcomes; company names not given in the abstract |
| Rodriguez et al., *Microbiome* 2024 [3] | Expert workshop review of real kits | 1 stool sample sent to 6 companies (5 in Europe, 1 in the US); 21 experts from 8 countries reviewed the reports | Experts flagged "over-promising messages," unclear methods and unreliable results, and judged report advice "premature" and of "limited clinical utility" | European focus; small number of kits |

## What the consensus actually says

The consensus doesn't call microbiome science useless. It sets minimum standards for when testing is done, and it is clear that routine clinical use isn't supported yet [1]. From the full text, the panel agreed that:

- Test providers should make customers "clearly aware of the currently limited evidence" for clinical use.
- Patients shouldn't order tests directly "without a clinical recommendation."
- There is "not enough information to report strict healthy reference ranges" for species, and "insufficient evidence" to include any dysbiosis index. Reporting the Firmicutes-to-Bacteroidetes ratio is discouraged.
- Multiplex PCR and bacterial culture "cannot be considered microbiome testing."
- Testing companies shouldn't give treatment advice based on the results.

## What this means

- **A consumer test isn't a reliable guide to what's wrong or what to eat.** There are no agreed healthy ranges, and the panel discouraged treatment advice from test providers [1].
- **The same stool can get different answers.** In the NIST study, which company ran the test mattered about as much as whose stool it was [2].
- **"Personalized" advice in reports is ahead of the science.** Experts reviewing real reports called it premature [3].
- **If your doctor suggests one, ask what question it answers.** The consensus frames testing as part of clinical care, with your clinician deciding what results mean, while stressing that the evidence is still limited [1].

## What's still uncertain

- Which conditions, if any, will benefit from microbiome testing. The panel called for dedicated studies before routine use [1].
- Whether standards will make results comparable. NIST's authors say standards are needed first [2].
- US regulation: these tests "straddle the line" between medical devices and wellness products [2].

## Track it

Things you can see change, like stool type, how often you go, what you ate and how you felt, are cheaper to track and easier to act on. A 2–3 week log gives your doctor real patterns to look at. | Chotwanvirat et al., *J Med Internet Res* 2024 [8] | Scoping review | 84 articles, 2008–2021 | Most systems were validated for energy and macronutrients, few for micronutrients | Predates current chatbots |
| Boushey et al., *Proc Nutr Soc* 2017 [9] | Review | Image-based diet methods, 2013–2016 | In every study reviewed, images reduced underreporting compared with traditional methods | Larger studies needed |

## What this means

- **Stool-photo AI is promising but not proven at scale.** Each study had 6 to 46 people, and all three had company-affiliated authors [1][2][3]. That doesn't make them wrong, but they aren't independent.
- **People aren't perfect at this either.** Volunteers misjudged types 2, 3, 5 and 6 most often [4]. That's the baseline any AI should be compared with.
- **Meal AI is better at "what" than "how much."** Portion and nutrient estimates often run low, especially for big meals [5][6][7].
- **Photos help with completeness.** Adding images reduced underreporting in the studies reviewed [9].

## What this means for Get Regular

Get Regular uses OpenAI's API to suggest a Bristol type from a stool photo and to estimate meal ingredients and nutrients [CONFIRM: model in use]. The studies above tested **other systems**, and their results don't transfer to our app. **Get Regular has not published an accuracy study of its own**, so we make no accuracy claims. Any future claim will come with its published method [CONFIRM: product/legal]. See [How our AI works](/science/how-our-ai-works) for inputs, limits and data flow.

## What's still uncertain

- How stool-photo AI performs in large, independent studies across different lighting and toilets [1][3].
- Whether general-purpose models will close the portion-size gap [6][7].

## Track it

Treat AI estimates as a starting point. Check a suggested stool type against the [Bristol stool chart](/tools/bristol-stool-chart), edit wrong meal ingredients, or log stool type manually with no photo.
