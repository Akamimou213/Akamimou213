# Get Regular — Science Fact Bank ("understand your gut" series)

Compiled: 2026-10-21. Scope: short educational 9:16 videos (45–55 s) for a US gut-health tracking app.

## How to read this file

- **[VERIFIED]**: I opened the original source (abstract, guideline text, or official page) and the number or wording matches it.
- **[VERIFIED-SECONDARY]**: the number comes from an official or peer-reviewed secondary source I opened, which cites the primary. The primary was not opened, or was paywalled.
- **[INFERRED]**: my synthesis or interpretation of verified sources, or general physiology not tied to one opened source. Do not put these on screen as a stat without a check.
- **Confidence** (hook list only): strong (guideline, large or representative dataset, or meta-analysis), moderate (single RCT, small or old study, or expert review), weak (observational, cross-sectional, or industry-only).
- PubMed and PMC pages blocked automated access. Abstracts were read through Europe PMC (same records). Each PMID is given so it can be checked on PubMed.

### Important 2026 context

- **Rome V was released in 2026.** The Rome Foundation FAQ gives a ship date of June 1, 2026. Validation papers say the criteria were "proposed in May 2026".
  - Most prevalence data below (Sperber 2021, Palsson 2020) use Rome IV or Rome III.
  - Population prevalence under Rome V had not been published in anything I found.
  - Rome V widens the IBS definition, so expect future estimates to sit between Rome IV and Rome III levels. This is [INFERRED], not yet a published population figure.
- **2025–2030 Dietary Guidelines for Americans** were released Jan 7, 2026. I could not confirm a restated gram-per-day fiber target in the new consumer document; secondary summaries say the specific gram numbers come from the 2020–2025 edition. Fiber figures below therefore cite the 2020–2025 DGA and NHANES-based papers.

## 1. Stool as a signal

### 1a. Bristol Stool Form Scale (BSFS): origin and meaning

- The BSFS was validated by Lewis & Heaton (1997). In 66 volunteers they measured whole-gut transit time with radiopaque markers, then changed it with senna and loperamide. **[VERIFIED]**
  - Source: Lewis SJ, Heaton KW. "Stool form scale as a useful guide to intestinal transit time." *Scand J Gastroenterol* 1997;32(9):920–924. PMID 9299672. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9299672/
- Changes in transit time correlated best with changes in stool form (r = −0.65, P < 0.001), better than with stool frequency. Form is a better transit proxy than "how often". **[VERIFIED]** (same source)
- Rome Foundation card wording for the 7 types: **[VERIFIED]**
  - 1 "Separate hard lumps, like nuts"
  - 2 "Sausage-shaped but lumpy"
  - 3 "Like a sausage but with cracks on its surface"
  - 4 "Like a sausage or snake, smooth and soft"
  - 5 "Soft blobs with clear-cut edges"
  - 6 "Fluffy pieces with ragged edges, a mushy stool"
  - 7 "Watery, no solid pieces"
  - The card also states "Constipated stool – Types 1 or 2; Diarrheal stool – Types 6 or 7".
  - Source: Rome Foundation, Bristol Stool Form Scale card (2021 PDF), "Copyright 2000 © Rome Foundation." https://theromefoundation.org/wp-content/uploads/Bristol-Stool-Form-Scale-Card-2021.pdf
- In 863 healthy adults (blue-dye method), median transit was **>5 days for Type 1** and about **1 day for Type 6**. Lower BSFS types mean slower transit. **[VERIFIED]**
  - Source: Asnicar F, et al. "Blue poo: impact of gut transit time on the gut microbiome using a novel marker." *Gut* 2021;70:1665–1674. PMID 33722860. https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8349893/
- Plain-language reading: types 1–2 suggest slow transit or constipation, 3–5 are the usual range, and 6–7 suggest fast transit or diarrhea. This is a simplification, not a diagnosis. **[INFERRED]** (from Lewis & Heaton, the Rome card, and Mitsuhashi below)

### 1c. How often is "normal"?

- In NHANES 2009–2010, 4,775 US adults reported normal bowel habits. **95.9%** of them reported **3 to 21 bowel movements per week**. **[VERIFIED]**
  - Source: Mitsuhashi S, Ballou S, Jiang ZG, et al. "Characterizing Normal Bowel Frequency and Consistency in a Representative Sample of Adults in the United States (NHANES)." *Am J Gastroenterol* 2018;113(1):115–123 (online 2017). PMID 28762379. DOI 10.1038/ajg.2017.213
- In the same sample, 90% of men reported a usual BSFS type of 3–5, while 90% of women reported 2–6. The authors suggest normal consistency may differ by sex. **[VERIFIED]** (same source)
- The authors say their data "bolster the common '3 and 3' metric" (3 per day to 3 per week). **[VERIFIED]** (same source)
- Caveat: "usual" stool type was self-reported. A later technician-scored study found far more hard stools (31.2% vs 7.70%), so self-report may understate variation. **[VERIFIED-SECONDARY]**
  - Source: *J Nutr* 2022, "Technician-Scored Stool Consistency Spans the Full Range of the Bristol Scale…", https://jn.nutrition.org/article/S0022-3166(22)00192-4/fulltext
- NIDDK lists "fewer than three bowel movements a week" as a sign of constipation and says "only you know what's normal for you". **[VERIFIED]**
  - About 16 in 100 US adults have constipation symptoms, rising to 33 in 100 at age 60+.
  - Source: NIDDK, "Definition & Facts for Constipation" (last reviewed May 2018). https://www.niddk.nih.gov/health-information/digestive-diseases/constipation/definition-facts
- Rome IV functional constipation includes straining, lumpy/hard stools (BSFS 1–2), incomplete evacuation, blockage sensation or manual maneuvers in **>25% of defecations**, or **<3 spontaneous BMs/week**. Two or more are needed, over the last 3 months, with onset at least 6 months earlier. **[VERIFIED]**
  - Source: Rome Foundation, Rome IV criteria page. https://theromefoundation.org/rome-iv/rome-iv-criteria/

### 1d. Stool colour: when to see a doctor

- **Black or dark red poo:** NHS says ask for an urgent GP appointment or contact NHS 111 if poo is black or dark red, or for bloody diarrhoea. **[VERIFIED-SECONDARY]** (NHS page summarized by search; direct fetch 404'd on an old URL)
  - Source: NHS, "Bleeding from the bottom (rectal bleeding)." https://www.nhs.uk/symptoms/bleeding-from-the-bottom-rectal-bleeding/
- **Heavy bleeding:** NHS says go to A&E or call 999 for non-stop bleeding, or a lot of blood (toilet water turns red, large clots). **[VERIFIED-SECONDARY]** (same)
- **Harmless dark stool:** NHS lists iron tablets and dark foods (liquorice, blueberries) as harmless causes. Bismuth is another known cause; this is [INFERRED] general knowledge, not in the NHS text I saw. **[VERIFIED-SECONDARY]**
- **GI bleeding:** NIDDK lists "black or tarry stool" and "dark or bright red blood mixed with stool" as symptoms of acute GI bleeding. **[VERIFIED]**
  - Source: NIDDK, "Symptoms & Causes of GI Bleeding" (last reviewed July 2024). https://www.niddk.nih.gov/health-information/digestive-diseases/gastrointestinal-bleeding/symptoms-causes
- **Pale or clay-colored stool:** this "may be due to problems in the biliary system" (liver, gallbladder, pancreas drainage). It often comes with jaundice or dark urine. MedlinePlus says to contact a provider "if your stools are not the normal brown color for several days". **[VERIFIED-SECONDARY]** (MedlinePlus page content via search result)
  - Source: MedlinePlus, "Stools – pale or clay-colored." https://medlineplus.gov/ency/article/003129.htm

### 1e. Floating stool, mucus

- In 33 healthy subjects (9 with floating stools) and 6 with steatorrhea, all floating stools sank when their gas was compressed. Floating depends on **gas, not fat**. **[VERIFIED-SECONDARY]**
  - Even fatty (steatorrheic) stools had near-normal gas content. The authors conclude a floating stool "should not be considered a sign of steatorrhea".
  - Source: Levitt MD, Duane WC. "Floating stools — flatus versus fat." *N Engl J Med* 1972;286:973–975. PMID 5015442. (Abstract via U. Minnesota record, https://experts.umn.edu/en/publications/floating-stools-flatus-versus-fat/)
- Caveat: tiny, 1972 study. Greasy, foul, hard-to-flush stools with weight loss are a different picture (malabsorption) and worth a doctor visit. **[INFERRED]**
- NHS lists "pooing mucus" among IBS symptoms. **[VERIFIED]**
  - Source: NHS, "Symptoms of IBS" (last reviewed 17 Mar 2025). https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/irritable-bowel-syndrome-ibs/symptoms/
- Mucus with blood, or with fever and urgent diarrhoea, warrants medical review (inflammation or infection). **[INFERRED]** (consistent with NHS bleeding guidance; no single opened source states this)

### 1f. Transit time and self-testing

- The "blue muffin" test: two blue-dyed muffins after fasting, then time to first blue stool. It cost about $1 per person and was well tolerated. **[VERIFIED]** (Asnicar 2021, above)
  - Median gut transit was **28.7 hours** (n=863 healthy UK adults).
  - Groups: fast <14 h (n=79), normal 14–38 h (n=424), normal 38–58 h (n=186), slow >58 h (n=174).
- Blue-dye transit predicted microbiome composition better than stool frequency or consistency (AUC 0.82). **[VERIFIED]** (same)
- Evidence quality: the blue-dye test is validated for **population research**, not as a clinical diagnostic. Clinical gold standards are radiopaque markers, scintigraphy and wireless motility capsules. **[VERIFIED]** (Asnicar background) + **[INFERRED]** for the "not a diagnostic" framing.
- The **sweetcorn test** (time to undigested kernels) is folk practice. I found no validation study. Kernels are hard to spot and can appear in several stools. Treat it as weak or anecdotal. **[INFERRED]** (absence of evidence in Europe PMC title search)
- Monash cites transit studies placing whole-gut transit at "somewhere between 12 and 48 hours" in healthy people. Another Monash blog cites 10–73 hours. **[VERIFIED]** (12–48 h) / **[VERIFIED-SECONDARY]** (10–73 h)
  - Source: Barrett J, "Timing of symptoms and FODMAPs," Monash FODMAP blog, 20 Jun 2016. https://www.monashfodmap.com/blog/timing-of-symptoms/ ; Collins L, "Eating and IBS symptoms," Monash blog, Sept 2018. https://www.monashfodmap.com/blog/eating-and-ibs-symptoms/

### 1g. Toilet time and straining

- In 125 adults having screening colonoscopy (Boston), 66% used smartphones on the toilet. **[VERIFIED]**
  - 37.3% of phone users sat >5 minutes per visit, vs 7.1% of non-users.
  - Phone use was associated with **46% higher odds of hemorrhoids** after adjusting for age, sex, BMI, exercise, straining and fiber.
  - Source: Ramprasad C, et al. "Smartphone use on the toilet and the risk of hemorrhoids." *PLoS One* 2025;20:e0329983. PMID 40901789.
  - Cross-sectional, single centre, small sample: association, not causation (weak–moderate).
- Straining in >25% of bowel movements is one Rome IV criterion for functional constipation. **[VERIFIED]** (Rome IV page)

## 2. IBS

### 2a. Definitions

- **Rome IV IBS:** recurrent abdominal pain on average at least 1 day/week in the last 3 months, linked to 2+ of: related to defecation; change in stool frequency; change in stool form. Onset must be at least 6 months before diagnosis. **[VERIFIED]** (Rome IV criteria page and Rome BSFS card)
- **Rome IV subtypes** are based on days with abnormal bowel movements: **[VERIFIED]** (Rome card)
  - IBS-C: >25% types 1–2 and <25% types 6–7
  - IBS-D: >25% types 6–7 and <25% types 1–2
  - IBS-M: >25% of both
  - IBS-U: neither
- **Rome V IBS (2026):** **[VERIFIED-SECONDARY]**
  - Re-includes "discomfort" alongside pain.
  - Lowers frequency to **at least 3 days/month** (from 1 day/week).
  - Specifies pain or discomfort must not be continuous.
  - Formally separates clinical and research criteria.
  - Medscape reports a possible shorter minimum duration (8 weeks); I did not see this in the Rome text. Treat it as unconfirmed.
  - Sources: Rome Foundation, "Rome V: A Global Framework for DGBI." https://theromefoundation.org/rome-v-a-global-framework-for-disorders-of-gut-brain-interaction/ ; AGA GI & Hepatology News, 20 Aug 2026. https://news.gastro.org/issues/2026/august-2026/rome-v-criteria-capture-more-people-with-ibs-but-a-milder-group/
- Early Rome V validation (Leeds secondary care, n=726): **[VERIFIED]**
  - Sensitivity: Rome V 66.1%, Rome IV 78.9%, Rome III 87.5%.
  - Specificity: Rome V 80.1%, Rome IV 81.0%, Rome III 75.0%.
  - Source: Staller K, et al. *Lancet Gastroenterol Hepatol* 2026;11:812–820. PMID 42392123.
- **"Disorder of gut–brain interaction" (DGBI):** Rome IV recasts these disorders this way. They involve motility disturbance, visceral hypersensitivity, altered mucosal/immune function, altered microbiota and altered CNS processing. **[VERIFIED]**
  - Source: Drossman DA. "Functional GI Disorders: History, Pathophysiology, Clinical Features and Rome IV." *Gastroenterology* 2016. PMID 27144617.

### 2b. Prevalence: why numbers range from ~1.5% to ~12%

- Rome Foundation Global Study (73,076 adults, 33 countries): **[VERIFIED]**
  - IBS prevalence with Rome IV vs Rome III: **4.1% vs 10.1%** (internet survey) and **1.5% vs 3.5%** (household interviews).
  - At least one DGBI: **40.3%** (internet) and **20.7%** (household).
  - Women had higher odds (OR 1.7 internet, 1.3 household).
  - Source: Sperber AD, et al. "Worldwide Prevalence and Burden of Functional Gastrointestinal Disorders, Results of Rome Foundation Global Study." *Gastroenterology* 2021;160:99–114.e3. PMID 32294476. DOI 10.1053/j.gastro.2020.04.014
- US/Canada/UK population survey (n=5,931): **[VERIFIED]**
  - Rome IV IBS 4.4–4.8%. Rome IV vs Rome III overall: **4.6% vs 9.0%**, "primarily due to higher Rome IV minimum pain frequency".
  - Any Rome IV functional bowel disorder: 28.6–31.7%.
  - Functional bloating/distension: 2.0–3.9%.
  - Source: Palsson OS, et al. *Gastroenterology* 2020;158:1262–1273. PMID 31917991.
- NIDDK still states "about 12 percent of people in the United States have IBS". That figure predates Rome IV; the page was last reviewed November 2017. **[VERIFIED]**
  - Source: NIDDK, "Definition & Facts for IBS." https://www.niddk.nih.gov/health-information/digestive-diseases/irritable-bowel-syndrome/definition-facts
- **Why the range:** **[INFERRED]** (from the above)
  - Rome IV requires weekly pain, which roughly halves prevalence vs Rome III.
  - Internet panels report about 2× higher than face-to-face household interviews.
  - Rome V lowers the bar again (3 days/month, adds "discomfort").
  - Safe on-screen phrasing: "about 1 in 20 to 1 in 10 adults, depending on how it's defined".

### 2c. Women vs men

- Meta-analysis of 56 studies (188,229 people): OR for IBS in women vs men **1.67** (95% CI 1.53–1.82). Women were more likely to have IBS-C. The difference was not significant in South Asian, South American or African studies. **[VERIFIED]**
  - Source: Lovell RM, Ford AC. *Am J Gastroenterol* 2012;107:991–1000. PMID 22613905.
- NIDDK: "Women are up to two times more likely than men to develop IBS." **[VERIFIED]** (NIDDK IBS page)

### 2d. Time to diagnosis

- A 2016 review states IBS patients "wait an average of 4 years before a diagnosis of IBS is established". It cites the AGA "IBS in America" 2015 survey (3,254 sufferers; GfK; industry-funded) and a prescribing study. **[VERIFIED-SECONDARY]**
  - Source: Schoenfeld PS. "Advances in IBS 2016." *Gastroenterol Hepatol (N Y)* 2016;12(8 Suppl 3):1–11. PMID 28070176. https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5210026/
- A widely repeated "6.6 years" figure could **not** be verified in any source I opened. Do not use it.
- AGA "IBS in America" 2024 survey (2,013 IBS-C/IBS-D patients; Harris Poll; Salix-supported): **[VERIFIED]**
  - Symptoms disrupt productivity about 11 days/month and personal activities 8 days/month (~19 days total).
  - Patients miss 3.6 days/month of work or school (2.1 in 2015).
  - 76% find symptoms somewhat or very difficult to manage.
  - Source: AGA press release, 7 Aug 2025. https://gastro.org/press-releases/ibs-in-america-despite-advances-ibs-remains-a-burden-for-many-millions/
- Celiac disease misdiagnosed as IBS (UK GP database): **16%** of celiac patients had a prior IBS diagnosis vs 4.9% of controls (OR 3.8). There was a clear excess of IBS diagnoses even 10 years before the celiac diagnosis. **[VERIFIED]**
  - Source: Card TR, et al. *Scand J Gastroenterol* 2013;48:801–807. PMID 23697749.

### 2e. Positive diagnosis vs diagnosis of exclusion (ACG 2021)

- ACG: "We suggest a positive diagnostic strategy as compared to a diagnostic strategy of exclusion… to improve time to initiate appropriate therapy." **[VERIFIED]**
  - Source: Lacy BE, Pimentel M, Brenner DM, et al. ACG Clinical Guideline: Management of IBS. *Am J Gastroenterol* 2021;116:17–44. PMID 33315591.
- ACG: in a US study of IBS care costs, **nearly 80%** of costs in the first 2 years went to an exclusion approach (testing, imaging, hospitalizations, ED visits). Only about 20% went to treatment. **[VERIFIED]** (same)
- ACG: celiac serology in IBS with diarrhea; fecal calprotectin (or lactoferrin) plus CRP in suspected IBS-D without alarm features. Against routine colonoscopy under age 45 without warning signs. **[VERIFIED]** (same)
- ACG: "We do not recommend testing for food allergies and food sensitivities in all patients with IBS unless there are reproducible symptoms concerning for a food allergy." **[VERIFIED]** (same)

### 2f. Red flags / alarm features

- **ACG 2021:** "hematochezia, melena, unintentional weight loss, older age of onset of symptoms, family history of IBD, colon cancer, or other significant GI disease". ACG notes these have low predictive value in IBS but still guide testing. **[VERIFIED]** (ACG PDF)
- **NIDDK:** anemia, rectal bleeding, bloody or black tarry stool, weight loss "may suggest that you have another health problem instead of IBS". **[VERIFIED]**
  - Source: NIDDK, "Diagnosis of IBS." https://www.niddk.nih.gov/health-information/digestive-diseases/irritable-bowel-syndrome/diagnosis
- **NHS (urgent GP / 111):** unexplained weight loss, bleeding from the bottom or bloody diarrhoea, a hard lump or swelling in the tummy, or breathlessness with palpitations and pale skin (possible anaemia). See a GP if IBS-type symptoms last over 4 weeks. **[VERIFIED]** (NHS IBS symptoms page)
- **NICE CG61 (2008 list):** unintentional and unexplained weight loss; rectal bleeding; family history of bowel or ovarian cancer; change in bowel habit to looser or more frequent stools for >6 weeks in someone over 60. Also anaemia, abdominal or rectal masses, and raised inflammatory markers. The current NICE text defers to NG12 (suspected cancer). **[VERIFIED-SECONDARY]**
  - Source: https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/cg61/chapter/Recommendations
- **Young adults:** colorectal cancer incidence is rising about **3% per year in ages 20–49**, and death rates in under-50s are up about 1%/yr since 2004. About **3 in 4** under-50 cases are diagnosed at regional or distant stage. ACS calls CRC the most common cancer-related death in people under 50. **[VERIFIED]**
  - Source: American Cancer Society, "Colorectal Cancer Statistics, 2026," *CA Cancer J Clin* (news release 2 Mar 2026). https://www.cancer.org/research/acs-research-news/colorectal-cancer-drops-in-older-adults-and-rises-in-young-ones.html
- USPSTF (May 2021) recommends average-risk screening from **age 45**. **[VERIFIED-SECONDARY]**
  - Source: https://www.uspreventiveservicestaskforce.org/uspstf/announcements/final-recommendation-statement-screening-colorectal-cancer-0

### 2g. Overlaps: SIBO, celiac, IBD

- **SIBO:** meta-analysis of 25 case-control studies (3,192 IBS, 3,320 controls). **[VERIFIED]**
  - SIBO OR in IBS 3.7 overall, 4.9 vs healthy controls only.
  - By breath test: 35.5% of IBS vs 29.7% of controls positive.
  - Lactulose breath tests gave 3.6× (IBS) and 7.6× (controls) higher positivity than glucose tests, so test choice drives the numbers.
  - Source: Shah A, et al. *Am J Gastroenterol* 2020;115:190–201. PMID 31913194.
- SIBO is real but over-diagnosed by some breath tests. "SIBO" claims from consumer breath kits are weak evidence. **[INFERRED]**
  - ACG has a dedicated SIBO guideline: Pimentel M, et al. *Am J Gastroenterol* 2020;115:165–178. PMID 32023228.
- **Celiac:** pooled global seroprevalence **1.4%**, biopsy-confirmed **0.7%** (North America 0.5%). **[VERIFIED]**
  - Source: Singh P, et al. *Clin Gastroenterol Hepatol* 2018;16:823–836. PMID 29551598.
- ACG 2023 celiac guideline recommends **case-finding** (testing people with symptoms or risk) rather than mass community screening. **[VERIFIED-SECONDARY]**
  - Source: Rubio-Tapia A, et al. *Am J Gastroenterol* 2023;118:59–76.
- Test for celiac **before** going gluten-free. Serology needs gluten in the diet. **[INFERRED]** (standard practice; in ACG guidance but not quoted here)
- **IBD:** ACG recommends fecal calprotectin to rule out IBD in suspected IBS-D. **[VERIFIED]** (ACG 2021)

## 3. Bloating

- Rome Foundation Global Epidemiology Study (51,425 people, 26 countries; organic disease excluded): **[VERIFIED]**
  - Nearly 18% report bloating at least weekly, from 11% in East Asia to 20% in Latin America.
  - Women are about twice as likely as men to report it. Prevalence falls with age.
  - Bloating was strongly associated with abdominal pain (OR 2.90).
  - Source: Ballou S, et al. "Prevalence and Associated Factors of Bloating: Results From the Rome Foundation Global Epidemiology Study." *Gastroenterology* 2023;165:647–655. PMID 37315866.
- Rome IV functional bloating/distension requires bloating or distension at least 1 day/week, predominating over other symptoms, without meeting criteria for IBS or other disorders. US/CA/UK prevalence: 2.0–3.9%. **[VERIFIED]** (Rome IV page; Palsson 2020)
- AGA 2023 best-practice advice for bloating and distension: **[VERIFIED]**
  - Use Rome IV criteria.
  - Rule out carbohydrate enzyme deficiencies by dietary restriction and/or breath testing.
  - Use SIBO testing only in a subset of at-risk patients.
  - Consider gut-directed behavioral therapies and diaphragmatic breathing.
  - Source: Moshiree B, Drossman D, Shaukat A. "AGA Clinical Practice Update on Evaluation and Management of Belching, Abdominal Bloating, and Distention." *Gastroenterology* 2023;165:791–800. PMID 37452811.
- **Distension mechanism (abdomino-phrenic dyssynergia):** **[VERIFIED]**
  - In IBS and functional dyspepsia patients with visible distension, imaging showed **no increase in gas volume**. Instead the diaphragm descended and the abdominal wall relaxed, pushing contents forward.
  - Healthy people do the opposite: the diaphragm relaxes and the front wall contracts.
  - EMG biofeedback reduced girth and distension in trials. Diaphragmatic breathing (about 30 min after meals) is suggested, though protocols are not standardized.
  - Source: Damianos JA, Tomar SK, Azpiroz F, Barba E. "Abdominophrenic Dyssynergia: A Narrative Review." *Am J Gastroenterol* 2023;118:41–45. PMID 36191283. https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9810002/
- **Normal gas:** 10 healthy volunteers (normal diet + 200 g baked beans) passed **476–1,491 mL of flatus per day** (median 705 mL), more after meals. **[VERIFIED]**
  - A fibre-free diet cut this to a median of 214 mL; fermentation gases are the largest share.
  - Source: Tomlin J, Lowis C, Read NW. *Gut* 1991;32:665–669. PMID 1648028.
  - I did not verify the popular "10–20 times a day" count claim.
- **NHS bloating red flags:** **[VERIFIED]**
  - See a GP for regular bloating, bloating that persists after diet change, or bloating with weight loss or blood in poo.
  - Get an urgent appointment for bloating with vomiting, diarrhoea or constipation, a lump, or inability to pass stool or wind.
  - Call 999 for a swollen stomach with sudden severe pain, coffee-ground vomit, or breathing difficulty.
  - NHS notes persistent bloating can occasionally be a sign of ovarian cancer.
  - Source: NHS, "Bloating" (last reviewed 21 Jan 2026). https://www.nhs.uk/symptoms/bloating/

## 4. Food and patterns

### 4a. Low-FODMAP diet (Monash)

- Monash: "IBS symptoms improve in 3 out of 4 people who follow a low FODMAP diet". The diet "does not cure IBS". **[VERIFIED]**
  - Source: Monash FODMAP, "About FODMAPs and IBS." https://www.monashfodmap.com/about-fodmap-and-ibs/
- Three steps: **[VERIFIED]**
  - (1) swap high- for low-FODMAP foods for **2–6 weeks**
  - (2) reintroduce FODMAP groups in 3-day challenges
  - (3) "relax dietary restrictions as much as possible" toward a "less restrictive, more nutritionally balanced" long-term diet
  - Monash says it should be done "under the guidance of a dietitian".
  - Source: Monash, "Starting the low FODMAP diet." https://www.monashfodmap.com/ibs-central/i-have-ibs/starting-the-low-fodmap-diet/
- Network meta-analysis (13 RCTs, 944 patients): low-FODMAP ranked first vs habitual diet for global symptoms. **[VERIFIED]**
  - RR of symptoms not improving 0.67 (95% CI 0.48–0.91).
  - Most trials were in secondary or tertiary care and did not test reintroduction.
  - Source: Black CJ, Staudacher HM, Ford AC. *Gut* 2022;71:1117–1126. PMID 34376515.
- RCT responder rates: 68% on low-FODMAP vs 23% on habitual diet. **[VERIFIED]**
  - Source: Staudacher HM, et al. *J Nutr* 2012;142:1510–1518. PMID 22739368.
- In Peters 2016, about 71% improved on diet (≥20 mm VAS). **[VERIFIED]** (see 5b)
- **Range explanation:** Monash's "3 in 4" comes from its own research. RCT responder rates run roughly 50–75% depending on endpoint and comparator. Against a placebo-like "sham" diet the margin is smaller. Use "about half to three-quarters". **[INFERRED]**
- ACG 2021: "We recommend a limited trial of a low FODMAP diet." The recommendation table grades this conditional, very low quality of evidence. **[VERIFIED]** (ACG PDF; abstract wording says "recommend")
- Caveat (microbiome): low-FODMAP restriction **lowered luminal bifidobacteria** after 4 weeks. This is one reason not to stay in phase 1 long-term. **[VERIFIED]** (Staudacher 2012)

### 4b. Fiber

- DGA 2020–2025 sets about **14 g per 1,000 kcal**. **[VERIFIED-SECONDARY]**
  - Adult targets by age: women 19–30: 28 g, 31–50: 25 g, 51+: 22 g; men 34, 31, 28 g.
  - "More than 90% of women and 97% of men do not meet recommended intakes for dietary fiber."
  - Source: Thompson HJ. *Nutrients* 2021;13:4034 (citing DGA 2020–2025). PMID 34836289 ; DGA site https://www.dietaryguidelines.gov/
- US mean fiber intake was **11–19 g/day** (ages 2+, NHANES 2009–2010). The highest group, men 31–50, averaged about 20 g vs 38 g recommended. Fewer than 5% of most groups meet the IOM Adequate Intake of 25–38 g/day. **[VERIFIED]**
  - Source: Mobley AR, et al. "Identifying practical solutions to meet America's fiber needs." *Nutrients* 2014;6:2540–2551. PMID 25006857.
- On-screen-safe: "Most Americans eat about half the fiber they need (~15 g vs 25–34 g)." **[INFERRED]** (consistent with both sources)
- **Soluble vs insoluble:** ACG 2021 says "We suggest that soluble, but not insoluble, fiber be used to treat global IBS symptoms" (strong recommendation; moderate quality). **[VERIFIED]** (ACG PDF)
  - Soluble examples: psyllium, oat bran, barley, beans. Insoluble examples: wheat bran, whole grains, some vegetables. **[VERIFIED-SECONDARY]**
- AGA-ACG 2023 chronic idiopathic constipation guideline gives a **conditional** recommendation for fiber. It gives strong recommendations for PEG, sodium picosulfate, linaclotide, plecanatide and prucalopride. **[VERIFIED]**
  - Source: Chang L, Chey WD, et al. *Gastroenterology* 2023;164:1086–1106. PMID 37211380.
- **Water:** 117 adults with chronic constipation all ate about 25 g fiber/day for 2 months. **[VERIFIED]**
  - Increasing fluid to 1.5–2.0 L/day significantly enhanced stool frequency vs ad-lib fluid (~1.1 L).
  - Source: Anti M, et al. *Hepatogastroenterology* 1998;45:727–732. PMID 9684123.
  - Single older trial (moderate). Water alone, without fiber, has little evidence as a constipation fix. **[INFERRED]**

### 4c. Kiwifruit, prunes, psyllium

- Gearry 2023 (international RCT, crossover): 2 green kiwifruit/day vs psyllium 7.5 g/day for 4 weeks. **[VERIFIED]**
  - Kiwifruit increased complete spontaneous bowel movements (CSBM) by **1.53/week** in functional constipation and **1.73/week** in IBS-C.
  - It improved GI comfort, with no significant adverse events.
  - **Funded and design-approved by Zespri International** (kiwifruit marketer).
  - Source: Gearry R, et al. *Am J Gastroenterol* 2023;118:1058–1068. PMID 36537785.
- Chey 2021 (US, 79 chronic constipation patients, partially randomized): kiwifruit (2/day), prunes (100 g/day) and psyllium (12 g/day) all increased weekly CSBMs. **[VERIFIED]**
  - Kiwifruit had the fewest adverse events and the least dissatisfaction.
  - Source: Chey SW, Chey WD, Jackson K, Eswaran S. *Am J Gastroenterol* 2021;116:1304–1312. PMID 34074830.
- Prunes RCT (120 healthy adults, low fiber): 80 g/d and 120 g/d increased stool weight and frequency vs water control (80 g: 6.8 vs 5.4 BMs/week). **[VERIFIED]**
  - Transit time was unchanged and flatulence increased.
  - Source: Lever E, et al. *Clin Nutr* 2019;38:165–173. PMID 29398337.
- Note: prunes and kiwi contain FODMAPs or sorbitol. They can worsen bloating in some IBS patients. **[INFERRED]**

### 4d. Coffee and the gastrocolic response

- 99 young volunteers: **29%** said coffee induced a desire to defecate. **[VERIFIED]**
  - In responders, both regular and **decaf** coffee raised rectosigmoid motility within **4 minutes**, lasting ≥30 min. Hot water did not.
  - Source: Brown SR, Cann PA, Read NW. "Effect of coffee on distal colon function." *Gut* 1990;31:450–453. PMID 2338272.
- Caffeinated coffee stimulated colonic motor activity "similar to a meal, 60% stronger than water and 23% stronger than decaffeinated coffee". **[VERIFIED]**
  - Source: Rao SS, et al. "Is coffee a colonic stimulant?" *Eur J Gastroenterol Hepatol* 1998;10:113–118. PMID 9581985. (n=12)
- Because decaf works too, caffeine is not the only trigger. The effect is too fast to be digestion and is likely a reflex. **[INFERRED]** from Brown 1990.

### 4e. Why it's hard to connect food to symptoms (delayed reactions)

- Monash: FODMAP symptoms usually take "at least 4 hours after eating a high FODMAP meal". FODMAPs act in the small and large intestine, where fermentation gas stretches sensitive bowel. **[VERIFIED-SECONDARY]**
  - Source: Monash blog "Eating and IBS symptoms" (Sept 2018), via search summary.
- Monash: symptoms right after swallowing "cannot be attributed to the fermentation of FODMAPs". Eating moves along contents from **earlier** meals, so you can blame the wrong food. **[VERIFIED]**
  - Source: Monash blog "Timing of symptoms" (2016).
- Whole-gut transit has a median of about 29 h, and normal ranges run up to ~58 h (Asnicar). Effects of a food can surface a day or more later. **[INFERRED]** from verified transit data.
- ACG 2021: "Up to 20% of the population report adverse reactions to food", but on rechallenge "only 2%–3% develop recurrent symptoms". IBS patients report food reactions at rates "as high as 50%". **[VERIFIED]** (ACG PDF)

### 4f. Food and symptom diaries

- A contemporaneous Food and Symptom Times (FAST) diary was validated in 51 IBS patients. Its symptom scores correlated moderately with PROMIS GI scales (r 0.33–0.48). **[VERIFIED]**
  - Source: Wright-McNaughton M, et al. *Clin Transl Gastroenterol* 2019;10:e00103. PMID 31800544.
- Real-time logging beats recall, and diaries are used in research and in dietitian-led FODMAP reintroduction. There is little trial evidence that diaries *by themselves* improve outcomes. **[INFERRED]** (no RCT of diary-alone found)
- Example of a counter-intuitive diary finding: in 147 IBS patients' 4-day diaries, those eating the **least** gluten had **worse** symptoms (lower intake tracked lower calories). **[VERIFIED]**
  - Source: Algera JP, et al. *Clin Nutr* 2021;40:5365–5372. PMID 34560607.

### 4g. Elimination diets: risks

- In a French tertiary IBS clinic (n=326, Rome IV), **47.5%** had ARFID-like symptoms on screening and **33.4%** met DSM-5-compatible ARFID criteria. **[VERIFIED]**
  - Source: Mikhael-Moussa H, et al. *Neurogastroenterol Motil* 2026;38:e70456. PMID 42823900. (Tertiary-care sample; general-population rates will be lower.)
- In the Italian Rome Foundation data (n=1,930), people with DGBI and high distress showed "broader, nonselective restriction" of fruits and vegetables regardless of FODMAP content. **[VERIFIED]**
  - Source: Ribichini E, et al. 2026, Italian arm of the Rome Foundation Global Epidemiology Study. PMID 42458685.
- Low-FODMAP lowers bifidobacteria (Staudacher 2012). Monash stresses reintroduction and dietitian supervision. **[VERIFIED]**
- Message for the app: tracking should aim to **narrow down** triggers and **re-expand** the diet, not cut food groups indefinitely. **[INFERRED]**

## 5. Gut–brain axis

- 12-year prospective population study (Australia, n=1,002 at follow-up): **[VERIFIED]**
  - Higher baseline **anxiety** predicted new functional GI disorders (OR 1.11 per unit).
  - People with a GI disorder but no anxiety or depression at baseline developed **more anxiety and depression** later.
  - Conclusion: "The central nervous system and gut interact bidirectionally."
  - Source: Koloski NA, et al. *Gut* 2012;61:1284–1290. PMID 22234979.
- 1-year follow-up (n=1,900): "in one-third of individuals a mood disorder precedes FGID but in two-thirds an FGID precedes the mood disorder". **[VERIFIED-SECONDARY]** (quote via Wiley page summary; abstract conclusion verified)
  - The abstract conclusion says "a major subset begin with gut symptoms first and only then psychological distress develops".
  - Source: Koloski NA, Jones M, Talley NJ. *Aliment Pharmacol Ther* 2016;44:592–600. PMID 27444264.
- Meta-analysis (73 studies): anxiety symptoms in **39.1%** and depressive symptoms in **28.8%** of IBS patients, about 3× the odds of healthy people. **[VERIFIED]**
  - Source: Zamani M, et al. *Aliment Pharmacol Ther* 2019;50:132–143. PMID 31157418.

### 5b. Brain–gut therapies

- ACG 2021: "We suggest that gut-directed psychotherapy be used to treat global IBS symptoms." **[VERIFIED]**
- Network meta-analysis (41 RCTs, 4,072 people): self-guided/minimal-contact CBT (RR 0.61), face-to-face CBT (RR 0.62) and **gut-directed hypnotherapy (RR 0.67)** were effective. **[VERIFIED]**
  - High risk of bias means efficacy is likely overestimated.
  - Source: Black CJ, et al. *Gut* 2020;69:1441–1451. PMID 32276950.
- RCT (n=74): gut-directed hypnotherapy, low-FODMAP diet and their combination improved symptoms equally (72%, 71%, 72% responders). Hypnotherapy improved anxiety more. **[VERIFIED]**
  - Source: Peters SL, et al. *Aliment Pharmacol Ther* 2016;44:447–459. PMID 27397586.
- Rome V formally integrates low-FODMAP, biofeedback, gut-directed CBT and hypnotherapy into care. **[VERIFIED]** (Rome Foundation Rome V page)

### 5c. Serotonin "90–95% made in the gut": accurate with nuance

- "About 95% of 5-HT is estimated to be found in gut mainly within the enterochromaffin cells whereas about 5% is found in the brain." **[VERIFIED]**
  - Source: Banskota S, Ghia JE, Khan WI. "Serotonin in the gut: Blessing or a curse." *Biochimie* 2019;161:56–64. PMID 29909048.
- Other sources say "~90%" or "most". Gut 5-HT is made by TPH1 in enterochromaffin cells; brain 5-HT by TPH2. **[VERIFIED]**
  - Sources: Gershon MD, Tack J. *Gastroenterology* 2007;132:397–414. PMID 17241888 ; Berger M, Gray JA, Roth BL. *Annu Rev Med* 2009;60:355–366 ("most serotonin is found outside the central nervous system"). PMID 19630576.
- **Nuance:** serotonin does **not** cross the blood–brain barrier. Gut serotonin does not become brain serotonin or set your mood directly. **[VERIFIED-SECONDARY]**
  - It acts locally on motility, secretion and sensation, signals via vagal and spinal nerves, and much of it is stored in platelets.
  - Source: ScienceDirect review text via search, "The roles of peripheral serotonin in metabolic homeostasis" (FEBS Lett 2015) ; Banskota 2019.
- Gut microbes (spore-forming bacteria) promote 5-HT synthesis by colonic enterochromaffin cells (mouse and human microbiota). **[VERIFIED]**
  - Source: Yano JM, et al. *Cell* 2015;161:264–276. PMID 25860609.
- Safe on-screen phrasing: "Most of your body's serotonin lives in your gut, where it helps run digestion." Avoid "your gut makes your happiness". **[INFERRED]**

### 5d. Vagus nerve

- About **80–90%** of vagal fibers are afferent (gut/organs → brain) and 10–20% efferent. **[VERIFIED]**
  - Source: Breit S, et al. "Vagus Nerve as Modulator of the Brain-Gut Axis…" *Front Psychiatry* 2018;9:44. PMID 29593576.
- Popular "vagus nerve hacks" (cold plunges, humming) for gut symptoms lack RCT evidence. Diaphragmatic breathing has some evidence for belching, rumination and distension (AGA 2023; Damianos 2023). **[INFERRED]** (with the cited support)

## 6. Gut and skin, sleep, energy, mood (be skeptical)

- **Sleep:** meta-analysis (36 studies, 63,620 people): sleep disorder in **37.6%** of IBS patients; pooled OR 2.6 vs controls. Observational, so direction is unclear. **[VERIFIED]** (moderate)
  - Source: Wang B, Duan R, Duan L. *Saudi J Gastroenterol* 2018;24:141–150. PMID 29652034.
- **Fatigue:** NHS lists tiredness among IBS symptoms. **[VERIFIED]** (NHS IBS page)
  - Unexplained tiredness with bowel change is also an NHS bowel-cancer prompt.
- **Mood:** anxiety and depression are about 3× more common in IBS (Zamani 2019), and the link runs both ways (Koloski). **[VERIFIED]** (moderate–strong for association; causation unproven)
- **Skin (rosacea):** Danish national cohort (49,475 rosacea patients vs 4.3 million controls). **[VERIFIED]**
  - Rosacea was associated with later celiac disease (HR 1.46), Crohn's (1.45), UC (1.19) and IBS (1.34), but not SIBO or H. pylori.
  - Source: Egeberg A, et al. *Br J Dermatol* 2017;176:100–106. PMID 27501017.
  - Association only (moderate for association, weak for mechanism).
- **Skin (acne) and probiotics:** reviews call the gut–skin axis plausible, but "clinical trials… are scarce". **[VERIFIED]** (weak)
  - Source: Sánchez-Pellicer P, et al. *Microorganisms* 2022;10:1303. PMID 35889022.
- Strong exceptions: dermatitis herpetiformis is a skin form of celiac disease, and IBD has recognized skin manifestations. **[INFERRED]** (standard medicine; not opened here)
- **Microbiome testing:** an international consensus (Lancet Gastroenterol Hepatol 2025) says evidence for clinical usefulness "is scarce". **[VERIFIED]**
  - Direct-to-consumer microbiome tests are offered "without any consensus on their regulation or any proven value in clinical practice".
  - Source: Porcari S, et al. *Lancet Gastroenterol Hepatol* 2025;10:154–167. PMID 39647502.
- **Overhyped claims to avoid:** **[INFERRED]** (backed by Porcari, AGA 2020, Banskota)
  - "Your microbiome causes your anxiety or depression or weight."
  - "Leaky gut causes skin or fatigue."
  - "A healthy microbiome" as a measurable target (no consensus definition).
  - "Probiotic X fixes bloating or mood."
  - Human evidence is mostly associational or from small trials.

## 7. Myths worth correcting

1. **"You must poop every day."**
   - Correction: normal ranges from 3×/day to 3×/week; 95.9% of US adults with normal habits fall in 3–21/week. Consistency and change from *your* baseline matter more.
   - Source: Mitsuhashi 2018 (AJG); NIDDK constipation page. **[VERIFIED]**
2. **"Floating poop means you're not absorbing fat."**
   - Correction: floating is mainly due to gas. In the 1972 study even fatty stools floated because of water, not fat.
   - Source: Levitt & Duane 1972 (NEJM). **[VERIFIED-SECONDARY]**
3. **"Gluten is bad for everyone."**
   - Correction: celiac disease affects ~0.7% (biopsy) to 1.4% (serology) globally.
   - In blinded challenges of self-identified gluten-sensitive people, only **16%** (38/231) had gluten-specific symptoms, and 40% of those reacted similarly to placebo (nocebo).
   - Fructans in wheat may be a culprit for some.
   - Sources: Singh 2018 (CGH) **[VERIFIED]**; Molina-Infante J, Carroccio A. *Clin Gastroenterol Hepatol* 2017;15:339–348, PMID 27523634 **[VERIFIED]**; ACG 2021 on fructans **[VERIFIED]**.
4. **"Detoxes and cleanses flush toxins."**
   - Correction: NCCIH finds only a small number of low-quality human studies, and "no compelling research" for detox diets.
   - Colon cleansing "may have side effects, some of which can be serious". FDA and FTC have acted against detox sellers.
   - Source: NCCIH, "'Detoxes' and 'Cleanses': What You Need To Know" (updated March 2025). https://www.nccih.nih.gov/health/detoxes-and-cleanses-what-you-need-to-know **[VERIFIED]**
5. **"More fiber always helps bloating."**
   - Correction: ACG recommends **soluble**, not insoluble, fiber for IBS. Fermentable fibers and FODMAPs can increase gas and bloating; prunes increased flatulence in an RCT.
   - Increase gradually, with fluids.
   - Sources: ACG 2021 **[VERIFIED]**; Lever 2019 **[VERIFIED]**; Tomlin 1991 (fermentation drives gas volume) **[VERIFIED]**.
6. **"Probiotics fix everything."**
   - Correction: AGA 2020 says for IBS: "we recommend the use of probiotics only in the context of a clinical trial" (knowledge gap). ACG 2021: "We suggest against probiotics for the treatment of global IBS symptoms."
   - Effects are strain-specific; the BSG 2021 guideline is more permissive.
   - Sources: Su GL, et al. *Gastroenterology* 2020;159:697–705, PMID 32531291 **[VERIFIED-SECONDARY for exact wording]**; ACG 2021 **[VERIFIED]**; Vasant DH, et al. BSG IBS guideline, *Gut* 2021;70:1214–1240, PMID 33903147.
7. **"IBS is all in your head."**
   - Correction: IBS is a disorder of gut–brain **interaction** with measurable features (motility, visceral hypersensitivity, immune and microbiota changes).
   - In two-thirds of cases gut symptoms came *before* mood symptoms. The best psychological therapies work by changing gut–brain signalling, which doesn't make the condition imaginary.
   - Sources: Drossman 2016; Koloski 2016; Black 2020. **[VERIFIED]**
8. **"IgG food intolerance tests tell you what to cut."**
   - Correction: food-specific IgG4 "indicates that the organism has been repeatedly exposed to food components". It is a marker of exposure or tolerance, and testing "should not be performed in case of food-related complaints".
   - ACG: "Serum IgG panels have not been validated and cannot be recommended."
   - Sources: Stapel SO, et al. EAACI Task Force. *Allergy* 2008;63:793–796, PMID 18489614 **[VERIFIED]**; Carr S, et al. CSACI position statement. *Allergy Asthma Clin Immunol* 2012;8:12, PMID 22835332 (title verified); ACG 2021 **[VERIFIED]**.
9. **"If tests are normal, nothing is wrong."**
   - Correction: ACG recommends a *positive* symptom-based diagnosis of IBS rather than endless exclusion. The exclusion approach drove ~80% of early IBS costs.
   - Source: ACG 2021. **[VERIFIED]**
10. **"Bloating means too much gas."**
    - Correction: in many patients with visible distension, gas volume is normal. A reflex pattern (diaphragm pushes down, belly wall relaxes) creates the protrusion.
    - Source: Damianos 2023 (AJG). **[VERIFIED]**
11. **"Most of your serotonin is in your gut, so your gut controls your happiness."**
    - Correction: ~90–95% of serotonin is in the gut, but it doesn't cross into the brain.
    - Source: Banskota 2019; Berger 2009. **[VERIFIED]**
12. **"Symptoms right after eating mean that food is the trigger."**
    - Correction: FODMAP effects take hours. Eating can push along earlier meals.
    - Source: Monash 2016. **[VERIFIED]**

## 8. When to see a doctor: official lists (combined)

- **Seek emergency care (999/911, A&E/ER):** **[VERIFIED]** (NHS bloating; NHS rectal bleeding [VERIFIED-SECONDARY])
  - heavy or non-stop rectal bleeding
  - a swollen stomach with sudden severe pain
  - vomiting blood or "coffee grounds"
  - severe difficulty breathing
- **Urgent/same-day:** **[VERIFIED]** (NHS IBS, bloating, rectal bleeding pages; NIDDK GI bleeding)
  - black/tarry or dark red stool
  - bloody diarrhoea
  - unexplained weight loss
  - a lump in the abdomen
  - inability to pass stool or wind
  - breathlessness, palpitations or pallor (possible anaemia)
- **Book a doctor visit:** **[VERIFIED]** (NHS; MedlinePlus; ACG 2021; NICE CG61 [VERIFIED-SECONDARY])
  - blood in poo for 3 weeks
  - stools softer, thinner or more frequent for 3+ weeks (NHS bowel-cancer page: change for 3 weeks)
  - IBS-type symptoms for over 4 weeks
  - pale or clay stool for several days
  - regular bloating
  - new bowel change at older age (NICE: >6 weeks if over 60)
  - family history of bowel or ovarian cancer, or of IBD or celiac disease
  - anaemia
  - persistent bloating (NHS: occasionally ovarian cancer)
- **Under 50 is not "too young":** CRC incidence is rising ~3%/yr in ages 20–49, and ~3 in 4 cases are found at an advanced stage. US screening starts at 45. **[VERIFIED]** (ACS 2026; USPSTF 2021 [VERIFIED-SECONDARY])

## CLAIMS TO AVOID IN ADS (wellness app)

Primary reference: FTC, *Health Products Compliance Guidance* (Dec 20, 2022; replaces 1998 dietary-supplement guide; covers "health-related apps"). https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/health-products-compliance-guidance **[VERIFIED]**

Key FTC principles (section numbers from the guidance) **[VERIFIED]**:

- Implied claims count. The FTC judges ads by their "net impression", including images, product name and context (III.A.1).
- Health benefit claims generally need "randomized, controlled human clinical testing" to meet the "competent and reliable scientific evidence" standard (III.B).
- Anecdotes and testimonials are "never sufficient" to substantiate health effects. An app's user testimonials about symptom relief don't substantiate the claim (III.B.2; Examples 21, 39).
- Disclaimers can't contradict the claim. An app claiming acne improvement was not saved by "for entertainment purposes only" (III.A.3, Example 14).
- Vague qualifiers ("may", "helps") don't fix an unsupported claim (III.A.3).
- Atypical results need a clear disclosure of typical results; "results not typical" isn't enough (III.C.1). Paid endorsements and influencers need clear material-connection disclosure (Example 40).
- Medical imagery or "clinically proven" language implies scientific proof that must exist (Examples 15, 18).

**Do NOT say or imply:**

1. **Diagnosis:** "Find out if you have IBS / SIBO / celiac / food intolerance." "Get your diagnosis." The app tracks and summarizes; clinicians diagnose (ACG positive diagnosis is a clinical process). Saying an app diagnoses may also raise FDA device questions. **[INFERRED]**
2. **Cure or treat:** "Fix your gut", "cure bloating", "heal IBS", "end constipation", "treats", "relieves symptoms" (without RCT evidence for *the app itself*). Disease claims need disease-level substantiation (FTC III.A.1).
3. **Outcome numbers borrowed from other interventions:** "3 in 4 people feel better" (that's Monash low-FODMAP, not the app). FTC III.B.5: results from a different product, population or dose don't transfer.
4. **Microbiome claims:** "rebalance / reset / optimize your microbiome", "heal leaky gut", "boost good bacteria", "personalized microbiome score". No validated consumer target exists (Porcari 2025 consensus).
5. **Serotonin and mood:** "Your gut makes your happiness", "fix your gut, fix your anxiety / depression / skin / sleep / energy". Evidence is associational (Section 6).
6. **Pattern-finding as proof:** "We'll find your trigger foods" or "know exactly what's causing your symptoms". Say "spot possible patterns to discuss with your doctor or dietitian". Correlation in a diary is not causation, and timing is confounded (Section 4e).
7. **Implied safety or replacement of care:** "No need to see a doctor", "skip the tests", "natural alternative to medication". FTC requires disclosure of significant risks; delayed care is the key risk for red-flag symptoms.
8. **Food-intolerance testing language:** never pair the app with IgG-test-style claims ("discover your intolerances").
9. **Detox or cleanse language:** "flush", "detox", "reset your colon" (NCCIH; FDA/FTC enforcement history).
